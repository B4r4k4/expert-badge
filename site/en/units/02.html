Unit Introduction

This domain carries about 26 of the 150 questions, and its scenarios all share the same shape: “The model scored 98% in evaluation, then failed in production — why?”, “Which metric should the team report?”, “The data is imbalanced, leaks, or drifts — what is the first step?”. The rule the exam rewards never changes: diagnose the data problem before you touch the model. This unit builds the foundation the rest of the domain stands on: how to split data without leaking the future into training, how to read overfitting and underfitting, how to choose the metric by the cost of error rather than by habit, how to deal with imbalance, missing values and label noise, and how to tell real drift from a fault in the data pipeline. The next unit builds on this with the algorithm map, statistics, and metrics worked through with numbers.

Before You Start

This unit is about data: how to split it, how to know the model has really learned and has not cheated, how to choose the right metric, and how to keep data quality up after launch. It is the foundation of Domain 1, the second-heaviest domain of the exam (about 26 questions out of 150).

One idea recurs throughout the unit: most model problems are caused by the data, not the algorithm. That is why the correct answer in the exam is often “inspect the data or fix the split”, not “use a bigger model”.

You need two things from Unit 00 first: Chapter 2 (data, splitting and overfitting) and Chapter 3 (the confusion matrix, precision, recall and the threshold). If you are not comfortable with them, go back to them first.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

  • Choose the correct splitting method (stratified random, temporal, grouped, cross-validation) according to the nature of the data, and justify the choice.
  • Detect data leakage from its signals in the scenario and identify the correct first remedy.
  • Diagnose overfitting, underfitting and drift from the patterns in training, validation and production numbers, and propose the right remedy for each case.
  • Match the metric to the cost of error: recall, precision, F1, PR-AUC, calibration, RMSE, MAE and Macro-F1.
  • Apply imbalanced-data techniques in the correct order, moving the threshold before considering retraining.
  • Assess data quality across its eight dimensions, and design data contracts and validation at the ingestion, training and serving points.
  • Design labels and features: measure inter-annotator agreement, use active learning, apply standard transformations and know their traps, and respect the serving constraint.

Chapter 1

Splitting, Leakage and Generalisation

1.1 The Correct Split for Each Situation

In Plain Terms

You learned in Unit 00 that we split data into training, validation and test sets. This section answers: how do we split? Randomly? Not always. The way to split depends on the shape of the data.

The general rule: the test must resemble the reality in which the model will operate. If the model will predict the future, we must test it on a “future” it has not seen, meaning the most recent period. If it will serve new customers, we must test it on customers it has not seen.

Real-World Example

You have a hospital visits log: the patient Ahmed visited 10 times. If we split randomly, 7 of Ahmed's visits will fall in training and 3 in the test. The model will recognise Ahmed and predict his condition well, not because it understood the disease but because it “knows Ahmed”. In reality it will meet patients it has never seen. The fix: a group split, meaning all of Ahmed's visits go on one side.

Key Terms
i.i.d. — independent and identically distributedمستقلة ومتماثلة التوزيع
The assumption that each row is unrelated to the others, and that all rows come from the same reality. A random split is valid for it. It is not valid with temporal data or with multiple rows for the same person.
Stratified splitالتقسيم الطبقي
A split that preserves the proportion of each class in every part. If fraud is 1% of the data, it will be 1% of the training set and 1% of the test set.
Time-based splitالتقسيم الزمني
Training on the older period and testing on the more recent one, so that the model is tested on a future it has not seen.
Group splitالتقسيم بالمجموعة
Placing all the rows of a single entity (customer, patient, device) in just one part of the split.
Churnتسرّب العملاء
A customer leaving the service, such as cancelling a subscription. Predicting it is a common task in telecoms and banking.
k-fold cross-validationالتحقق المتقاطع
Splitting the data into k parts and training the model k times, each time testing on a different part, then taking the average. Useful when the data is small.
Hyper-parametersالمعاملات الفائقة
Settings we choose ourselves before training, such as the depth of a tree or the learning rate. They differ from the parameters that the model learns by itself.

What the Exam Tests

Before any metric, the split itself must be honest: the test set represents what the model will see in production, and carries no information that will be unavailable at prediction time. The splitting method is not a personal preference; it is derived from the structure of the data.

SituationCorrect splitReason
Independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) tabular dataRandom train/validation/test split (for example 70/15/15), stratified if the classes are imbalancedPreserves the class proportions in every part
Time series, fraud, demand, customer churnTemporal split: train on the past, validate on a later period, and test on the most recentA random split leaks the future, so scores are inflated
Multiple rows per customer/patient/deviceGroup split: all rows of an entity fall in one partOtherwise the model memorises the entity instead of learning the pattern
Small datasetk-fold cross-validation (stratified / grouped / for time series)A stable estimate that uses all the data
Hyper-parameter tuningTune on the validation set, and touch the test set once at the endReusing the test set repeatedly = overfitting to the test set itself
The test set is touched once. Every tuning decision made while looking at it has made it part of the training.

1.2 Data Leakage: Definition and Warning Signs

In Plain Terms

Leakage is when the model sees, during training, a “hint” about the answer that will not exist in reality. The result: excellent scores in the test, then failure in production.

Leakage has two routes. The first is a feature that carries the answer: a column that is known only after what we want to predict has happened. The second is a wrong order of steps: processing all the data (computing the mean, for example) before the split, so that information enters training from the test data without our noticing.

Real-World Example

You want to predict how many days a patient will stay in hospital, on the day of admission. You put “discharge date” among the features. The model will compute the difference and give a perfect answer! But on the day of admission we do not know the discharge date at all, so the model is useless in reality. An example of the wrong order: computing the mean age from all the data to fill in missing ages, then splitting. The mean here has “seen” the test data.

Key Terms
Data leakageتسريب البيانات
The presence in training of information that will not be available at real prediction time, so the model looks far better than it is.
Chargebackالاعتراض على العملية
A customer disputing a card transaction and getting the amount refunded. It happens days or weeks after the transaction, so it counts as leakage if used as a feature to predict fraud.
Normalisationالتطبيع / التوحيد
Converting numbers to a common scale, such as subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation. It needs the mean to be computed, which is why it must be computed from the training data only.
Imputationتعويض القيم المفقودة
Filling empty cells with an estimated value, such as the median. The value must be computed from the training data only.
Foldالطيّة
A part of the data in cross-validation. The “training fold” is the part used for learning in that round.
Target encodingترميز الهدف
Converting a text category (such as a city name) into a number that is the target mean for that category, such as the fraud rate in each city. If it is computed with the row itself participating, it leaks the answer, which is why it is computed “out-of-fold”.

What the Exam Tests

Data leakage is information that is available at training time but will not be available at prediction time. A leaking model does not learn the problem; it learns the “answer” hidden in a feature or in the way the data was processed, so it looks excellent in evaluation and collapses in production.

Warning signs in the scenario text

  • “Suspiciously high accuracy” or “excellent in evaluation, poor in production”.
  • A feature computed after the outcome occurs: for example chargeback_flag for predicting fraud, or discharge_date for predicting length of hospital stay.
  • Normalisation, imputation or feature selection carried out on the whole dataset before the split.
  • Duplicate records across training and test.
  • Target encoding without out-of-fold computation.

Note that half of these causes have nothing to do with the features themselves, but with the order of the steps: any processing that “learns” something from the data (a mean, a standard deviation, a list of selected features, the target mean per category) must learn it from the training fold alone.

1.3 The First Remedy for Leakage

In Plain Terms

If you discover leakage, the remedy is three steps in order: remove the leaking feature, make all processing learn from the training data alone, then re-run the test with a correct split.

The trap in the exam: options such as “collect more data” or “use a stronger model” seem logical because they address “poor performance”. But they do not touch the cause. The new data will carry the same leaking column, and the stronger model will exploit it more.

Real-World Example

A student scored 100% because the answers were written on the back of the question sheet. Is the fix to give him more questions? No, because the answers will be on the back of those too. The fix is to remove the answers from the back of the sheet, then test him again.

Key Terms
Pipelineخط المعالجة
A chain of processing steps linked together (imputation, then normalisation, then model) that is always applied in the same order. It guarantees that each step learns from the training data only, and prevents leakage automatically.
Regularisationالتنظيم
Constraints added during training that stop the model from becoming too complex and memorising details. A remedy for overfitting, but it does not treat leakage.

What the Exam Tests

  1. Remove the leaking feature (any feature that cannot be computed at prediction time).
  2. Refit the preprocessing inside the training fold, preferably using a pipeline that guarantees this automatically.
  3. Re-run the evaluation on a correctly split test set (temporally or by group where needed).
The remedy for leakage is not “collect more data” and not “use a bigger model”. The extra data carries the same leakage, and the bigger model exploits it more.

In the exam, the tempting wrong options here are those that treat the symptom (poor performance in production) with a generic tool: more data, a different algorithm, or more regularisation. The correct answer always starts by fixing the split or the feature.

1.4 Bias and Variance: Overfitting and Underfitting

In Plain Terms

To find out what is wrong with a model, compare three numbers: its performance in training, in validation, and in production. Each pattern has a different diagnosis, just as a doctor diagnoses from a set of symptoms.

The most important pattern in the exam: training and validation are both excellent, but production is poor. Many people think it is overfitting, and it is not. Overfitting shows up as a gap between training and validation. Here there is no gap between them, and the problem is that all the evaluation data “knows something” that reality does not: leakage, or a difference between past data and present data.

Real-World Example

Three students. The first scores 98 on the question bank and 70 on the mock exam: he memorised and did not understand, and this is overfitting. The second scores 55 on both: he did not study enough, and this is underfitting. The third scores 95 on both, then 60 on the final exam: the mock exam's questions were leaked to him, or the final came from a different syllabus. This is leakage or shift.

Key Terms
Biasالانحياز (في النماذج)
The error resulting from simplifying the model more than necessary. High bias means underfitting. It differs from “bias against a group” in fairness.
Varianceالتباين
The model's sensitivity to the specific details of the training data. High variance means it has memorised the noise, that is, overfitting.
Data augmentationزيادة البيانات
Creating additional training examples by simple modifications to the existing ones, such as rotating an image or changing its lighting, so that the model learns a more general pattern.
Regularisation methodsL1 / L2 / Dropout
Types of regularisation. L1 and L2 penalise large weights. Dropout switches off random neurons during training so that the network does not depend on a single neuron.
Early stoppingالإيقاف المبكر
Stopping training when validation performance stops improving and begins to decline, before the model starts to memorise the training set.
Distribution shiftالانزياح في التوزيع
Real-world data differing from the training data, such as new customers or new behaviour. It makes the model weaker in production than it was in the test.
Lossالخسارة
A number that measures how much error the model makes during training. The lower it is, the better.

What the Exam Tests

You read the state of the model by comparing three numbers: training performance, validation performance and production performance. Each pattern has a different diagnosis and remedy.

SignalDiagnosisRemedy
High training, much lower validationOverfitting — high varianceMore data, augmentation, regularisation (L1/L2, dropout), a simpler model, early stopping
Both lowUnderfitting — high biasRicher features, a bigger model, longer training, less regularisation
Both high in evaluation, poor in productionLeakage or distribution shiftFix the split/features; compare training distributions with live data
Validation loss rises while training loss fallsOnset of overfittingEarly stopping at the lowest validation loss

The third pattern is the most frequent in the exam, because it looks like overfitting but is not: overfitting shows up as a gap between training and validation, whereas here the validation itself is high, which means the validation set “knows” something production does not.

A gap between training and validation = overfitting. No gap but a collapse in production = leakage or shift.
Chapter 2

Metrics, Threshold and Imbalance

2.1 Choose the Metric by the Cost of Error

In Plain Terms

Each problem has a metric that suits it, and the question that decides it is one: which error is more expensive? If missing the case is catastrophic (disease, fraud), we measure recall. If the false alarm is annoying or costly (blocking an innocent account), we measure precision.

The long table in this section links each “cue” in the question text to its metric. Do not memorise all the names at once. Understand the idea: classification has metrics, continuous numbers (regression) have metrics, and search and ranking have metrics.

Real-World Example

A spam filter: if it wrongly hid an important message from your manager (a false positive), that is a big problem. If an advertising message got through (a false negative), it is not a great matter. So we care about precision. Cancer screening is exactly the opposite: missing a single patient is a catastrophe, so we care about recall.

Key Terms
F-beta scoreFβ وF2
A version of F1 that gives more weight to one of the two metrics. F2 gives recall more weight than precision, and is used when the miss is more expensive.
Area Under the ROC CurveROC-AUC
A number between 0.5 and 1 that measures the model's ability to rank positive cases above negative ones, regardless of the threshold. 0.5 means random guessing, and 1 means perfect ranking.
Area Under the Precision–Recall CurvePR-AUC
Like ROC-AUC but built on precision and recall. More reliable than it when the target case is very rare.
Calibrationالمعايرة
That the probability the model gives matches reality. If it says “70%” for a hundred cases, about 70 of them should really be positive. Important when we use the probability itself, as in loan pricing.
Brier scoreدرجة Brier
A measure of the quality of probabilities: the mean of the squared difference between the predicted probability and the actual outcome (0 or 1). The lower it is, the better.
Regression errorsRMSE وMAE وMAPE
Metrics for the error in predicting numbers. MAE: the mean absolute difference (in the target's own units). RMSE: penalises large errors more. MAPE: the error as a percentage, and it fails if there are zeros in the true values.
Multi-class F1Macro-F1 وWeighted-F1
When there are several classes: Macro computes F1 for each class then takes the average with equal weights, so it exposes the small, weak class. Weighted weights by the size of each class, so the large classes dominate.
Ranking metricsMRR وnDCG
Metrics for the quality of ranking search results. MRR: how early the first correct result appears. nDCG: whether the most important results are at the top. You will see them worked through with numbers in Unit 03.

What the Exam Tests

There is no metric that is “best” in absolute terms. The right question is: which error is more expensive — the false alarm (false positive) or the miss (false negative)? Do we need a decision at a threshold, a ranking, or probabilities that are used in pricing?

Precision = TP / (TP + FP)

Recall = TP / (TP + FN)

F1 = 2PR / (P + R) Fβ : β > 1 favours recall (e.g., F2)

2.2 The Accuracy Paradox and the Two Meanings of “Precision”

In Plain Terms

You saw this in Unit 00: when the target case is rare, the model that “does nothing” gets a very high overall accuracy. So any option in the exam that says “the model is successful because its overall accuracy is 99%” on data with rare cases is a distractor.

Note a wording warning: the word “precision” has two meanings in the exam. The first is the classification metric TP/(TP+FP), and it is the more common. The second is the “numerical precision” inside the model (FP16 and INT8). The context determines which is meant: if the talk is about memory and quantisation, it is numerical precision.

Real-World Example

In 1,000 transactions containing 5 frauds (0.5%), a model that says “legitimate” for all of them is right 995 times: overall accuracy of 99.5%. Its recall is 0 of 5 = 0%. An impressive number for a model that does not catch a single fraudster.

Key Terms
Accuracy paradoxمفارقة الدقة الكلية
The phenomenon whereby a useless model achieves very high overall accuracy when the target class is rare.
Numerical precisionدقة الأرقام
The number of bits used to store each number in the model (FP32, FP16, INT8). A completely different meaning from the precision metric.

What the Exam Tests

The accuracy paradox: if the fraud rate is 0.5%, a model that always says “no fraud” achieves 99.5% accuracy and detects nothing. Therefore any option that cites accuracy on imbalanced data as evidence of success is a distractor.

Accuracy(always 'not fraud') = 99.5% , Recall = 0%

A note on wording: the word “precision” in the exam usually means the classification metric TP/(TP+FP), not numerical precision (FP16/INT8), unless the scenario is about quantisation or GPU memory. Read the context before answering.

2.3 Threshold versus Model

In Plain Terms

The model gives a score, and we choose the threshold above which we say “yes”. Moving the threshold is like moving a switch between two ends: the side of “catch everything even if I err a lot” and the side of “do not say yes unless you are sure”.

The lesson: when people complain about too many false alarms or too many misses, try moving the threshold first. It needs no retraining, and costs almost nothing. We retrain only if no threshold achieves what is required.

Real-World Example

A security guard at a gate. If he searches every visitor (a low threshold), no one will slip past him, but the queue will grow long and the visitors will get angry. If he searches only those who look very suspicious (a high threshold), everyone will pass quickly, but he may miss a dangerous person. We do not need a new guard. We need to tell him when to search.

Key Terms
Scoreالدرجة
The number a classifier outputs for each case, usually between 0 and 1. The higher it is, the more confident the model is that the case is positive.
Precision–Recall curveمنحنى الدقة–الاستدعاء
A plot showing precision and recall at every possible threshold. You choose from it the point that suits your requirements.

What the Exam Tests

A trained classifier outputs a score, and the threshold is what turns it into a decision. Many scenarios of the type “too many false alarms” or “we miss too many cases” are solved by moving the threshold along the precision–recall curve: this is the cheapest solution and needs no retraining.

  • Too many false alarms → raise the threshold (precision rises and recall falls).
  • Too many misses → lower the threshold (recall rises and precision falls).
  • Retrain only if no threshold meets both requirements together, meaning the PR curve itself is low.
Move the threshold first; retrain only when the curve itself cannot satisfy both requirements.

2.4 Imbalanced-Data Techniques

In Plain Terms

When the target case is rare (0.5% fraud) the model tends to ignore it, because ignoring it makes it “right” most of the time. There are several ways to treat that, and the rule is: start with the cheapest.

The cheapest: we tell the model that its error on the rare case is more expensive (class weights), then we tune the threshold. There are methods that change the data itself: reducing the common cases, or creating extra copies of the rare cases. The big trap: creating the extra copies before the split, so that copies of test examples move into training, and this is leakage.

Real-World Example

A teacher in a class with 99 high-achieving students and one student who needs help. If he evaluates himself by “how many students passed”, he will look excellent even if he ignores the weak student. Class weights mean telling him: “This student's success counts for you as a hundred students”. So he starts to pay attention to him.

Key Terms
Class weightsأوزان الفئات
Making the model's error on the rare class count more heavily in the loss function, so that it cares about it more during training. The cheapest way to treat imbalance.
Undersamplingتقليص الأغلبية
Deleting some of the examples of the common class to balance it with the rare one. Fast, but it throws away information.
Oversampling / SMOTEزيادة الأقلية / SMOTE
Increasing the examples of the rare class by repeating them or by creating nearby synthetic examples (SMOTE). It must be applied to the training data only.
Anomaly detectionكشف الشذوذ
A model that learns only what “normal” looks like, then flags anything different from it. Useful when examples of the problem are very rare or non-existent.
Active learningالتعلم النشط
The model choosing the examples it is least sure about for humans to label, instead of random labelling. It saves a great deal of labelling effort.

What the Exam Tests

TechniqueWhenCaution
Class weights / cost-sensitive lossFirst choice, cheapCalibrate the probabilities afterwards
Threshold tuningAlways, after trainingTune it on validation, not on test
Undersampling the majorityHuge data and fast trainingLoses information
Oversampling the minority / SMOTESmall minority classOn the training fold only — never before the split (leakage)

Three traps recur: applying SMOTE before the split (so synthetic copies of test examples appear in training), forgetting that class weights distort the probabilities, which then need calibration and re-tuning of the threshold, and tuning the threshold on the test set.

2.5 Bank Fraud: The Full Exam Picture

In Plain Terms

Bank fraud is a scenario that recurred in the accounts of people who sat the exam, because it brings together many problems in a single story. This section lists six characteristics of it, and each characteristic leads you to a decision.

Memorise them as a checklist: rare (do not trust overall accuracy), temporal (split by time), its adversary adapts (fraudsters change their methods, so we need monitoring), fast (the decision comes before the payment is completed), labels are delayed (we learn the truth after weeks), and regulated (we must explain the reason for a decline).

Real-World Example

A customer pays by card in a shop. The bank has less than a second to decide “approve or decline”, so there is no room for a huge, slow model. The fraudsters the model caught last month will change their method this month. The customer whose transaction was declined has a right to know the reason: “an unusual amount from an unusual country”.

Key Terms
Adversarialعدائي
An environment in which a party deliberately tries to deceive the model and adapt to it, such as fraudsters or spammers.
Concept driftانجراف المفهوم
A change in the relationship itself between the features and the answer. For example, a new fraud method that makes the old fraud signs insufficient.
Gradient-boosted treesالأشجار المعززة
A model made up of hundreds of small decision trees, each tree correcting the errors of the ones before it. Fast and very accurate on tabular data. Among its best-known libraries are XGBoost and LightGBM.
Feature storeمخزن الميزات
A central system that computes features and stores them so they are used with the same definition in training and operation, such as “the number of the customer's transactions in the last 10 minutes”.
Proxy metricالمقياس البديل
A metric available immediately that we monitor instead of the real, delayed metric. For example the rate of declined transactions, until the chargeback results arrive after weeks.
Reason codesرموز الأسباب
Short, understandable reasons attached to each decision, such as “an amount five times higher than usual”. Regulators require them, and the customer has a right to know them.

What the Exam Tests

Bank fraud is the scenario that brings together the concepts of this domain in a single question. Expect questions that combine two or three of the following six characteristics:

  1. Highly imbalanced — metrics: recall, PR-AUC, expected cost; not accuracy.
  2. Time-dependent — use a temporal split.
  3. Adversarial — fraudsters adapt, so concept drift occurs; monitor and retrain periodically or when drift is detected.
  4. Latency-constrained — the score must arrive before the transaction is approved (tens of milliseconds), so a lightweight model such as gradient-boosted trees is served in real time, with live aggregates such as “number of transactions in the last 10 minutes” drawn from the feature store.
  5. Delayed labels — chargebacks arrive weeks later; monitor proxy metrics in the meantime.
  6. Regulated and explainable — reason codes for declined transactions, and fairness across customer segments.

2.6 Evaluation Beyond a Single Number

In Plain Terms

A single number on a single test set is not enough for a launch decision. This section lists practices that complete the picture.

The most important is evaluation by slice: a model may be excellent on average and very poor for a particular group, such as users of a particular dialect or region, and the average hides that. The second is confidence intervals: a small improvement on a small sample may be chance.

Real-World Example

A restaurant with an average rating of 4.5 out of 5. But in detail: lunch customers give it 4.9, and dinner customers 3.1, because the evening chef is weak. The average alone will not tell you this. Similarly, a model with 92% accuracy may have only 70% accuracy on the southern dialect.

Key Terms
Slice / segmentالشريحة
A part of the data that shares a trait, such as a region, an age group, a channel or a dialect. We measure performance for each slice separately.
Confidence intervalفترة الثقة
A range in which we expect the true value to lie. “Accuracy 90% ± 2%” means it is probably between 88% and 92%. If the intervals of two models overlap, one of them may not really be better.
Golden setالمجموعة الذهبية
A carefully chosen set of examples with trusted correct answers, on which every new release is tested.
Regression testاختبار التراجع
A test that makes sure the new release has not broken what worked in the previous release. The word “regression” here means going backwards, not statistical regression.
Text overlap metricsBLEU / ROUGE
Automatic metrics that compare generated text with a reference text by the number of shared words. Cheap, but they do not measure correctness or usefulness well.
Error analysisتحليل الأخطاء
Reading dozens of the model's actual errors by hand to understand their types before deciding what to change.
Offline / online evaluationالتقييم غير المتصل والحي

In Plain Terms

Real data is messy: empty cells, strange numbers, duplicate records, and multiple sources that do not agree. This table gives each problem its preferred remedy.

Real-World Example

In a survey, high earners leave the income field empty more than others. If we filled the gap with the mean we would hide an important piece of information: “whoever left the field empty probably has a high income”. The fix is to add a new column called “was it empty?” with a value of yes or no.

3.2 The Feature Store and Training–Serving Skew

In Plain Terms

Imagine that “customer age” is computed in training precisely from the date of birth, whereas in the real-time system it is computed from the year of birth only. The name is the same and the number is slightly different. The model receives in reality numbers it did not learn on, so its performance gets worse.

This is called training–serving skew. The problem here is not that the world changed, nor that the model is bad, but that we have two ways of computing the same thing. The fix: one place that defines the feature once and serves training and operation together, and that is the feature store.

Real-World Example

A restaurant with two branches cooking “the same recipe”, but each branch measures the salt its own way, so the taste differs. The fix is not to retrain the cooks, but one recipe book with specified quantities that both branches use.

Key Terms
Training–serving skewانحراف التدريب عن الخدمة
A difference in how features are computed between the training environment and the live operating environment, so that the model sees in reality data different from what it learned.
Batchالدفعات
Processing a large amount of data at once at a set time, such as every night, instead of processing each request as soon as it arrives.
Offline / online storeالمخزن غير المتصل والمتصل
The two parts of the feature store. Offline: keeps a long history for training. Online: gives the latest value of the feature within fractions of a second for real-time operation.
Point-in-time correct joinالربط الصحيح زمنيًا
When building training data, we take the value of each feature as it was at the moment of the event, not its value today, so that the future does not leak into the past.

What the Exam Tests

The feature store appears in training–serving skew scenarios: “The same feature is computed one way in the batch pipeline and another way in the real-time interface”. The problem here lies neither in the world nor in the model, but in the existence of two definitions of the feature.

  • One definition of the feature, used by training and serving alike.
  • An offline store for training, with point-in-time correct joins that prevent leakage.
  • An online store for low-latency serving.
Training–serving skew is fixed by fixing the pipeline (the feature store); retraining does not help.

3.3 The Data Lifecycle

In Plain Terms

Data passes through stages like any product: it is collected, then enters the systems, then is checked, then stored, then prepared, then labelled, then versioned, then used, then monitored, then either retained or destroyed.

The idea for the exam: the problem appears at one stage and its cause lies at an earlier stage. The model errs today (the usage stage), but the cause is that the data source changed the unit a week ago (the ingestion stage).

Real-World Example

A meal in a restaurant tastes spoiled. The problem appeared at the table, but the cause may be a supplier who sent old vegetables two days ago, and no one checked them on receipt. Fixing the way the food is served will not solve the problem.

Key Terms
Ingestionالاستيعاب
The stage of bringing data from its sources into our systems. The best place to check it before it spreads.
Versioningإدارة الإصدارات
Keeping numbered copies of the data and the models, so that we know exactly which data each model was trained on, and can go back to any version.
Destruction / deletionالإتلاف
Deleting data permanently when the need for it ends or the statutory retention period ends.

What the Exam Tests

Most machine-learning failures in production are data failures. A model is a compressed copy of the data it was trained on: if that data is late, incomplete, mislabelled or unrepresentative, no architecture will fix it. The expert engineer therefore treats data as a product with an owner, contracts, quality metrics and a lifecycle:

collect → ingest → validate → store → prepare → label → version → train/serve → monitor → retain or destroy

As a sequence: collection → ingestion → validation → storage → preparation → labelling → versioning → use in training and serving → monitoring → retention or destruction. Each stage has its own failure modes and controls, and the exam often describes a symptom at one stage whose cause lies at an earlier stage (for example: performance drops at serving because a unit changed at the source during ingestion).

Two quick cues from the exam: if the question says “the model is weak for a particular group”, think representativeness. If it says “audit” or “a request to delete a person's data”, think lineage.

Real-World Example

A voice-recognition model trained only on men's voices from Riyadh. The data is “accurate”, “complete” and “recent”, but it is not representative. The model will be weak with women's voices and with the dialects of other regions.

3.5 Data Contracts and Validation at Three Points

In Plain Terms

One team sends the data, and another team builds a model on it. One day the first team changes something small, such as converting the amount from riyals to halalas, without telling anyone. The model does not break with an error message, but simply starts giving wrong results silently.

A data contract prevents this: a written agreement on the shape of the data, its meaning and its units. An automated check on every arrival of data that stops any batch that violates the contract before it reaches the model. It is checked at three points: on receipt, before training, and during operation.

Real-World Example

A transaction amount of 150 riyals started arriving as 15,000 because it became in halalas. The model thinks all the transactions are huge and suspicious, so it fires thousands of alerts in a single day. Had the contract said “unit: riyal, and the amount between 1 and 500,000”, the automated check would have stopped the batch immediately and sent an alert.

Key Terms
Data contractعقد البيانات
An explicit, versioned agreement between the producer of the data and its consumer: the shape, the meaning, the units, the ranges, the arrival time, the owner, and the way of making changes.
Producer / consumerالمنتِج والمستهلِك
The producer: the team or system that creates the data and sends it. The consumer: the one who uses it, such as the model team.
Quarantineالعزل
Setting violating data aside instead of deleting it or using it, until someone reviews it.
Referential integrityالسلامة المرجعية
Every reference pointing to something that really exists. For example: every transaction is linked to a customer number that exists in the customers table.

What the Exam Tests

When a source team changes a column's meaning, unit or type, every downstream model silently breaks. A data contract is an explicit, versioned agreement between producer and consumer that specifies: the schema, the semantics, the units, the allowed ranges, the freshness agreement, the owner, and the change process. The contract is enforced by automated validation at ingestion; violations stop the pipeline or divert the data to quarantine, instead of letting corrupted data reach training or serving.

“The model degraded overnight after another team's release” → the permanent fix is a data contract plus validation, not retraining.

3.6 Drift: What Changed?

In Plain Terms

Drift is when reality changes away from the data the model learned from. But “what exactly changed?” determines the remedy. This table distinguishes five types of change.

The most important distinction for the exam: a sudden change on a specific day usually means a fault in the data, such as a changed unit or a column that became empty, and the fix is to repair the data, not to retrain. A gradual deterioration over weeks means real drift that may need retraining.

Real-World Example

The types of change, with a clothes-shop example. (1) Younger customers started coming: the inputs changed, and this is data drift. (2) The same customers started preferring different colours: the relationship changed, and this is concept drift. (3) At Eid the number of buyers doubles: the base rate changed, and this is label drift. (4) The cashier started recording sizes in a different system from the warehouse: a data fault.

Key Terms
Data / covariate driftانجراف البيانات / المتغيرات
A change in the distribution of the inputs (features) while the relationship between them and the answer stays as it is. Written P(X).
Concept driftانجراف المفهوم
A change in the relationship itself between the inputs and the answer: the same features came to mean something different. Written P(Y|X).
Label / prior driftانجراف التسميات / المعدل الأساسي
A change in the proportion of positive cases, such as fraud doubling in the busy seasons. Written P(Y).
Population Stability Index — PSIمؤشر استقرار المجتمع
A number that measures how much the distribution of a feature differs between two periods. The common rule: below 0.1 is stable, 0.1 to 0.25 is a moderate change, and above 0.25 is a large change. You will see it in detail in Unit 07.
Kolmogorov–Smirnov testاختبار KS
A statistical test that compares two distributions and says whether they differ. With very large data it detects trivial differences, so it is not sufficient on its own.
Upstream breakعطل في المصدر
An unintended change in data coming from another system: a new unit, an empty column, or a different format.

4.1 The Four Label Sources

In Plain Terms

Supervised learning needs “correct answers” to learn from, that is, labels. Where do we get them from? Four sources, each with a price and a flaw.

Humans are more accurate but expensive and slow. Behaviour (clicks, purchases) is cheap and plentiful but biased. Rough rules are fast but error-prone. Models suggest and humans confirm, and this is fast, but humans tend to accept the suggestion without thinking.

Real-World Example

You want to know which products customers like. The behavioural label: “what the customer clicked”. But the customer clicks only on what we showed him in the first place. A wonderful product we never showed will not get a click, and the model will think no one wants it. This is bias from the existing system.

Automation biasانحياز الأتمتة
The human tendency to accept what an automated system suggests without sufficient scrutiny, even when it is wrong.

4.2 Measuring Label Quality

In Plain Terms

How do we know the labels are good? We give the same sample to two or more annotators and see how far they agree. If the experts themselves disagree a lot, the problem is in the task definition: the guidelines are vague, or the case itself is ambiguous. The model will not learn what humans have not agreed on.

But agreement can happen by chance. If 90% of messages are legitimate, two annotators who always say “legitimate” will agree 90% of the time without any understanding. That is why we use kappa: a measure that subtracts the agreement that happens by chance and keeps only the real agreement.

Real-World Example

Two referees judge 100 incidents: is it a foul or not? They agreed on 80 incidents. But if 75 incidents were very clear and no one disputes them, the “real” agreement on the difficult incidents is small. Kappa reveals this. A kappa below 0.4 means: sit down first and agree on what counts as a foul.

Key Terms
Inter-annotator agreementالاتفاق بين المُوسِّمين
A measure of how far several people agree on labelling the same examples. An indicator of how clear the task is and of the quality of the labels.
Cohen's Kappaكابا كوهين
A measure of agreement between two annotators that subtracts the agreement expected by chance. 1 is perfect agreement, and 0 is agreement at chance level only.
Fleiss' Kappa / Krippendorff's Alphaكابا فلايس وألفا كريبندورف
Agreement measures like Cohen's kappa but suitable for more than two annotators.

What the Exam Tests

Have several annotators label the same sample, and compute inter-annotator agreement: Cohen's kappa for two annotators, and Fleiss' kappa or Krippendorff's alpha for more than two. Kappa corrects for the agreement that occurs by chance.

4.3 Active Learning, Synthetic Data and Arabic Labelling Issues

In Plain Terms

Labelling is expensive, so how do we save? Active learning: instead of labelling a thousand random examples, we label the hundred the model is most unsure about. The easy examples teach it nothing new. synthetic data: we generate examples by computer for the rare cases. Useful for training, but we never test on it, because it carries the biases of whoever made it and gives optimistic results.

In Arabic there are labelling challenges of its own: multiple dialects, mixing with English in the same sentence, writing Arabic in Latin letters (“3arabizi”), and diacritics. The labelling guidelines must say explicitly how each is to be handled.

Real-World Example

A student studying for the exam. The clever student does not re-solve the problems he has mastered, but focuses on those he gets wrong. That is active learning. An Arabic example: ابغى اكنسل الاشتراك (“I want to cancel the subscription”) is a colloquial sentence with an Arabised English word. Does the annotator label it a “cancellation request”? The guidelines must answer that in advance.

Key Terms
Uncertainty samplingأخذ العينات بعدم اليقين
Choosing the examples on which the model is least confident (its probability close to 50%) to send for human labelling.
Synthetic dataالبيانات الاصطناعية
Data generated by computer, by simulation or by a generative model, instead of being collected from reality. Useful for rare cases and for protecting privacy.
Code-switchingالتبديل اللغوي
Switching between two languages within the same sentence, such as mixing Arabic and English in a conversation.
Arabiziالعربيزي
Writing Arabic in Latin letters and numbers, such as “7abibi” and “3ala”. Common in messages and social media.

What the Exam Tests

Active learning: instead of labelling random samples, label the examples the current model is least confident about (uncertainty sampling), or the most diverse (coverage), or those on which the members of a committee of models disagree. This often reaches the same accuracy with a fraction of the labels — and it is the correct answer when “the labelling budget is the bottleneck”.

Synthetic data: generated records (simulation, generative models, text written by large language models) that help with rare classes, privacy-preserving sharing and testing. Its risks: it reproduces the biases of the generator, it may leak the generator's training data, and it makes evaluation optimistic if it enters the test set.

Synthetic data may be added to training; evaluation, however, is done on held-out real data.

Labelling issues specific to Arabic: dialect variation (Gulf, Levantine, Egyptian, Maghrebi), code-switching with English, Arabizi (Arabic written in Latin letters), and diacritisation — all of them change what the annotator sees. The guidelines must state how to handle each, and the annotator pool must cover the dialects of the target users.

4.5 Missing Values, Feature Selection and the Serving Constraint

In Plain Terms

Three ideas in this section. The first: why is the data missing? There are three reasons, and each reason has a remedy: missing by pure chance, or missing because of something else we know, or missing because of its own value.

The second: feature selection. Many unhelpful features slow the model down and confuse it, so we remove them. The third, and the most important for the exam: the serving constraint. A feature is valid only if it can be computed at the moment of prediction, at the required speed, and from data we are permitted to use.

Real-World Example

The three types with a survey example. (1) MCAR: the server crashed and random answers were lost. The missingness has no relation to anything. (2) MAR: young people skip the income question more, and age is recorded with us. The missingness is related to age, which we know, so we can impute with the help of age. (3) MNAR: very high earners hide their income. The missingness is related to the missing value itself, so the gap is information in itself.

Key Terms
Missingness typesMCAR / MAR / MNAR
Types of missingness: missing completely at random, missing at random because of other known variables, and missing for a reason related to the value itself.
Feature selectionاختيار الميزات
Removing unhelpful or duplicated features to reduce overfitting, slowness and maintenance burden.
Mutual informationالمعلومات المتبادلة
A measure of how much a feature tells us about the answer. If it is zero, the feature is of no use in prediction.
Permutation importanceأهمية التبديل
We shuffle the values of one feature randomly and see how much the model's performance gets worse. The more it worsens, the more important the feature.
Serving constraintقيد الخدمة
The condition that the feature can be computed at prediction time, within the permitted time, and from data that is legally permitted for use.

Simple imputers (median, most frequent) must be fitted inside the training fold. Model-based imputation (kNN, iterative) is more accurate but heavier. Boosted trees handle missing values natively.

Feature selection

Removes noisy or redundant inputs to reduce overfitting, latency and maintenance burden:

  • Filter methods: correlation, mutual information, the chi-squared test.
  • Wrapper methods: recursive feature elimination (RFE).
  • Embedded methods: L1 regularisation, tree importance.
  • Permutation importance on the validation set is the most reliable general-purpose measure.
  • Highly correlated features share importance among themselves — removing one of them may change little.

The serving constraint

A feature is valid only if it can be computed at prediction time, within the latency budget, and from data the system is permitted to use. A feature that needs a nightly batch job cannot drive a real-time decision within 50 milliseconds unless it is precomputed and stored in an online feature store. Features derived from personal data inherit the obligations of the Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL).

4.6 Choosing the Model Family, Baseline First

In Plain Terms

Which model do we choose? This table gives the “strong default choice” for each type of data. For tables: boosted trees. For images: pretrained image networks. For text with few examples: a fine-tuned small language model, or a large model with examples.

The governing principle: build a simple baseline first. A simple model that you measure well tells you how much complexity is worth. If the huge deep model beats it by a trivial margin, it is not worth its cost in speed, operation and interpretation.

Real-World Example

You want to predict tomorrow's sales. The baseline: “tomorrow's sales = sales on the same day of last week”. This needs no AI at all, and its error may be 12%. After it you build a complex model, and if its error is 11.5%, is it worth it? Without the baseline you would not know at all.

Key Terms
Baselineخط الأساس
The simplest reasonable solution, built first so that more complex solutions can be measured against it. If the complex model does not clearly beat it, there is no need for it.
Logistic regressionالانحدار اللوجستي
A simple classification model that gives a probability and is easy to interpret, because each feature has a clear weight. Preferred when regulators require interpretation.
SHapley Additive exPlanationsSHAP
A method that explains each prediction by how much each feature contributed to it. For example: “Loan declined: income −0.3, existing debts −0.2”.
Transfer learningالتعلم بالنقل
Starting from a model pretrained on huge data, then adapting it to your task with little data, instead of starting from scratch.
BERT encoder familyBERT
A family of language models that understand text, but do not generate it. Excellent for classification and information extraction, and they have Arabic versions.
Few-shotالتعلم بأمثلة قليلة
Giving a large language model a few solved examples inside the prompt so that it understands the task, without any training.
Lag featuresميزات التأخير
Past values as features to predict the future, such as “yesterday's sales” and “last week's sales”.
Distilled modelالنموذج المقطَّر
A small model trained to imitate a large model. Faster and cheaper with a small loss in quality.

In a scenario where the team jumps straight to a huge deep model, the best answer is often: “Build a simple baseline and sound evaluation first.” The complex model is acceptable only when it beats the baseline by enough to be worth its cost in latency, operations and interpretability.

Applied Scenario

Apply the “diagnose the data before the model” approach to a bank fraud model that achieved excellent results in evaluation and then disappointed in production.

The Scenario

A team at a Saudi bank built a card-fraud detection model on 12 months of data in which the fraud rate is 0.5%. The team split the data randomly 80/20, applied SMOTE and then normalisation to the whole dataset before the split, and used among the features the field “transaction was disputed”, which comes from the disputes system. The team reported overall accuracy of 99.6% and an AUC of 0.99.

After launch, the analytics team complained of many false alarms and, at the same time, large fraud cases were missed. Three weeks later, the model's scores suddenly dropped in a single day after the payments team changed the representation of the amount from riyals to halalas. Management asks for an ordered remediation plan with a reason for each step.

Solution Steps

  1. Reject overall accuracy as evidence: with 0.5% fraud, a “never fraud” model achieves 99.5%. The right metrics: recall, PR-AUC and expected cost.
  2. Detect the leakage: the dispute field becomes known weeks after the transaction, so it is unavailable at prediction time — remove it. SMOTE and normalisation before the split are additional leakage — move them inside a pipeline that is fitted on the training fold only.
  3. Redo the split temporally: train on the older months, validate on the following ones, and test on the most recent; because fraud is time-dependent and adversarial.
  4. Handle the imbalance in the cheap order: class weights first, then calibrate the probabilities, then tune the threshold on the validation set according to analyst capacity and the cost of a miss.
  5. Handle the complaint about false alarms and misses by moving the threshold first; do not retrain unless no threshold satisfies both requirements together.
  6. Diagnose the sudden drop: a step-shaped change on a specific date after another team's release = a source fault, not drift. Fix the source, quarantine the corrupted data, and add a data contract (unit: riyal) with automated validation at ingestion.
  7. Add post-deployment monitoring: because labels are delayed, monitor input drift and the prediction distribution as an early warning, evaluate by slice, and keep reason codes for declined transactions.

Exam Traps

  • “Collect more data” or “use a deeper model” as a remedy for leakage — new data carries the same leakage.
  • Citing overall accuracy or ROC-AUC on highly imbalanced data instead of recall and PR-AUC.
  • Applying SMOTE, normalisation or feature selection before the split.
  • Retraining immediately on a sudden drop caused by a source change or by training–serving skew.
  • Tuning the threshold on the test set, or using the test set repeatedly during tuning.
  • A random split of temporal data, or of data with multiple rows per customer.
What You Must Master

An ordered seven-step remediation plan that starts with the split and leakage, chooses the metric and threshold according to cost and capacity, and distinguishes a source fault from real drift.

Exercises & Unit Review

Try to answer on your own first, then open the question to see the worked answer.

  1. 1A model achieved 97% on validation and 95% on test, then 70% in production. Is this overfitting?
    Answer

    No. Overfitting shows up as a gap between training and validation. High evaluation scores across the board with a production collapse point to leakage or distribution shift; the first step is to inspect the split and the features and to compare training distributions with live data.

  2. 2Why is normalising the whole dataset before the split considered leakage?
    Answer

    Because the mean and standard deviation are computed with the test data's participation, so information about it leaks into training. The fix is to fit the preprocessing inside the training fold through a pipeline.

  3. 3Hospital data with several visits per patient. Which split?
    Answer

    A group split by patient, so that all of a patient's visits fall in one part; otherwise the model memorises the patient instead of learning the pattern, and the scores are inflated.

  4. 4The team complains of many false alarms. What is the cheapest fix?
    Answer

    Raise the threshold on the precision–recall curve without retraining. Retraining is needed only if no threshold meets the precision and recall requirements together.

  5. 5When is PR-AUC better than ROC-AUC?
    Answer

    Under heavy imbalance; because ROC-AUC is affected by the large number of true negatives and so looks high. Neither says anything about the threshold chosen for operation.

  6. 6A team used class weights, and the probabilities are used to price loans. What is the missing step?
    Answer

    Calibration: class weights inflate the probabilities, so they must be calibrated (Platt or isotonic), the Brier score and reliability curve monitored, and then the threshold re-tuned.

  7. 7The model's performance suddenly dropped in a single day after the payments team's release. What is the diagnosis and the permanent fix?
    Answer

    A sudden step-shaped change on a specific date indicates a source fault, not drift. The fix: repair the source and quarantine the corrupted data; the permanent fix is a data contract with automated validation at ingestion, not retraining.

  8. 8The same feature is computed one way in the batch pipeline and another way in the real-time interface. What is the fix?
    Answer

    This is training–serving skew; it is fixed with a single definition of the feature through a feature store (an offline store for training with point-in-time correct joins, and an online store for serving). Retraining does not help.

  9. 9Cohen's kappa between two annotators is 0.35. What do you do before training?
    Answer

    The value is below 0.4, which indicates that the guidelines or the task definition need work. Fix the specification and relabel the ambiguous slice; the model will not learn a consistent boundary if the experts disagree.

  10. 10Can synthetic data be used in the test set?
    Answer

    No. Synthetic data may be added to training, but it reproduces the generator's biases and makes evaluation optimistic; evaluation is done on held-out real data.

Exam-Style Questions

Choose an answer to see the correct one and its explanation. These 10 questions are drawn from the 86 questions assigned to this unit in the exam simulator.

  1. 1Leaking post-outcome featuresHard

    A hospital group builds a model to predict, at admission, whether a patient will stay longer than seven days, so that bed managers can plan. The gradient-boosted model reaches an AUC of 0.97 on a random hold-out, but after go-live it performs barely better than the old rule of thumb. Feature importance shows that the top features are 'number of discharge-medication orders' and 'discharge summary word count', both extracted from the patient's final record. The data science lead has two weeks before a steering-committee review. What should the team do first?

    Explanation

    Suspiciously high offline AUC plus weak live performance, with top features that exist only at discharge, is the classic signature of leakage: the model learned from information unavailable at admission. The first fix is to remove the leaking features, refit preprocessing inside the training fold (a pipeline) and re-evaluate on a clean hold-out; only then is the real performance known. More data, more regularisation or recalibration all keep the leaking features and therefore keep the false offline picture. Exam lesson: diagnose the data problem before touching the model.

  2. 2Early stopping at the validation minimumHard

    A media company fine-tunes an image classifier to tag news photos into 30 categories. Training runs for a fixed 60 epochs and the last checkpoint is deployed. The curves show training loss falling steadily throughout, while validation loss reaches its lowest point at epoch 14 and rises after that; validation macro-F1 also peaks around epoch 14. GPU time is billed per hour and the team retrains every week. Which change gives the best model at the lowest cost?

    Explanation

    Validation loss rising while training loss keeps falling means overfitting has started; the best-generalising model is the one at the validation minimum. Early stopping saves that checkpoint and halts soon after, which also cuts the weekly GPU bill by roughly two-thirds (about 20 epochs instead of 60). Training longer or using a larger backbone increases overfitting and cost; averaging the last ten checkpoints still averages models from the overfitted region.

  3. 3Screening where misses are unacceptableHard

    A health cluster's diabetic-eye screening programme uses a model to flag retinal photographs for ophthalmologist review. Missing sight-threatening retinopathy can lead to blindness, while a false flag costs one extra specialist read of about four minutes. Prevalence is 3%, and the specialists can read up to 25% of all photographs. Two operating points are available on the validation set: T1 gives recall 0.81 and precision 0.40; T2 gives recall 0.97 and precision 0.16. Which choice is best?

    Explanation

    When missing a case is the serious error, recall governs and more false alarms are accepted — provided the workload fits. At T2 the flagged share is 3% × 0.97 ÷ 0.16 ≈ 18.2% of photographs, under the 25% capacity. T1 flags 3% × 0.81 ÷ 0.40 ≈ 6.1% but misses 19% of sight-threatening cases versus 3%. F1 (≈0.54 for T1 vs ≈0.27 for T2) weighs both errors equally, which contradicts the stated costs; an F2 view would favour recall. Retraining is unnecessary when an available threshold already meets the constraints.

  4. 4SMOTE applied before the splitHard

    A Hajj and Umrah services company predicts which visa applications contain document errors (2% of applications). The engineer applied SMOTE to the whole labelled dataset to reach a 50/50 class balance, then made a stratified 80/20 split. Validation recall is 0.95 and precision 0.93, but on the first two weeks of live applications recall is 0.61 and precision 0.22. Input distributions in production match the training period. What is the most likely cause, and the right fix?

    Explanation

    SMOTE creates synthetic minority points by interpolating between real neighbours. Done before the split, near-copies of the same applications land in both training and validation, and the validation set also has an artificial 50% prevalence — so recall and precision are both inflated. The memo rule: oversample only the training fold, never before the split, and evaluate on data with natural prevalence. With input distributions unchanged, drift is unlikely; more synthetic data or a threshold move does not repair a contaminated evaluation.

  5. 5Outliers that are the signalHard

    A payment processor builds a merchant-fraud model. During data cleaning, an engineer removes the top 1% of transactions by amount and any merchant whose daily volume jumped more than tenfold, calling them 'outliers that distort training'. Validation AUC rises from 0.86 to 0.89, but in a back-test on last quarter the model misses most of the large confirmed fraud cases. What is the most appropriate correction?

    Explanation

    The memo's rule for outliers is to investigate first: is it a data error or a real rare event? In fraud, unusual amounts and sudden volume spikes are frequently the very behaviour to detect. Deleting them makes validation look better because the hard, important cases vanish from it, while the model goes blind to large fraud. Restore the records, separate genuine errors (fix or drop) from real events (keep), and use robust models or transforms only where needed. A blanket block rule, capping without investigation, or a longer window repeat the mistake.

  6. 6Covariate drift without performance lossHard

    A telecom operator's network-fault prediction model monitors PSI on its input features. After a new 5G router model was rolled out to 30% of sites, PSI on 'signal-to-noise ratio' rose to 0.31 and on 'device type' to 0.45, triggering an automatic retraining ticket. Labels arrive within 48 hours, and precision and recall on the last three weeks of labelled faults are within one point of baseline, including on the new-router sites. Retraining and revalidation take about two weeks of an engineer's time. What is the most appropriate action?

    Explanation

    Data (covariate) drift — a change in P(X) — is a reason to investigate, not an automatic reason to retrain. The memo's response is 'investigate; retrain if performance drops'. Here labels arrive quickly and sliced precision and recall, including on the new-router sites, are stable, so the model generalises to the new inputs. Keep watching that slice and retrain when performance degrades. Retraining on PSI alone spends two engineer-weeks for no gain; rolling back a business change or dropping informative features is disproportionate.

  7. 7Baselines firstHard

    A government housing programme wants to predict which beneficiaries will fall behind on subsidised mortgage payments. A newly hired team proposes a six-month project to build a transformer over each beneficiary's payment sequence and requests a GPU cluster. No model exists yet; current triage is a manual rule ('two missed payments'). Data covers about 300,000 beneficiaries with 30 tabular attributes and monthly payment history. The programme director wants results that can be explained to auditors within three months. What should the technical lead recommend first?

    Explanation

    Baselines first: when a team jumps to a large deep model with no model in place, the better answer is a simple baseline and a sound evaluation. Lag features from monthly payments plus logistic regression or gradient-boosted trees are quick, explainable to auditors and fit the three-month window; a time-based split mirrors use. The baseline also quantifies what any complex model must beat. Complexity must earn its cost — the transformer may come later if the baseline leaves measurable value on the table.

  8. 8Is the improvement real?Hard

    A bank's document-classification team compares a new model with the production model on the same 1,000 labelled test documents: 91.4% versus 90.9% accuracy (the classes are roughly balanced). The new model costs 40% more to serve. The product owner wants to switch immediately because 'it is better'. Using the normal approximation, the standard error of a 91% accuracy measured on 1,000 items is about 0.9 percentage points. What is the most appropriate recommendation?

    Explanation

    The memo warns that 0.5% better on 1,000 samples may be noise. A single accuracy estimate here has a standard error of about √(0.91 × 0.09 / 1,000) ≈ 0.9 points, so a 95% interval is roughly ±1.8 points — far wider than the 0.5-point gap. Because both models were scored on the same documents, a paired test such as McNemar's on the items where they disagree, or a larger evaluation set, is the right check — especially before accepting a 40% cost increase. A small gap is not yet evidence of improvement, nor proof of equivalence.

  9. 9Data contractsHard

    A mobile operator's credit-scoring model for device instalment plans degraded overnight. The cause: in a routine release, the billing team changed the field 'avg_topup' from a 90-day to a 30-day average without renaming it, so its values shifted. This is the third silent upstream change this year, each costing about a week of investigation and retraining. The billing team says it cannot know which downstream models use its fields. What is the most durable fix?

    Explanation

    When upstream producers change a column's meaning, unit or type, downstream models break silently. The durable fix is a data contract: an explicit, versioned agreement between producer and consumer on schema, semantics, units, allowed ranges, freshness, ownership and change process, enforced by automated validation that blocks or quarantines violating data. It also makes consumers visible to the producer. Retraining adapts to one break and normalises the next; emails depend on memory; drift alerts detect the damage only after it reaches the model.

  10. 10Active learning when labels are the bottleneckHard

    A hospital wants a model that flags radiology reports needing urgent follow-up. It has 400,000 unlabelled reports, but only two consultant radiologists can label, at about 150 reports a day between them, for the next two months. A first model trained on 3,000 randomly chosen labelled reports is confident on routine reports and unsure on a small fraction. Which labelling strategy gets the most model improvement from the radiologists' limited time?

    Explanation

    When the labelling budget is the bottleneck, the memo's answer is active learning: label the examples the current model is least certain about (uncertainty sampling), the most diverse ones, or those where a committee of models disagrees, then retrain and repeat. It often reaches the same accuracy with a fraction of the labels. Random labelling spends most of the scarce time on routine reports the model already handles; labelling confident cases teaches it nothing new; keyword rules are weak supervision — fast but noisy, and risky for a clinical urgency decision. Keep a random labelled test set aside so that evaluation is not biased by the selection.

Unit Summary

You have mastered this unit when, facing any data scenario, you can name the correct first step and its reason: a split that matches the structure of the data (temporal, grouped, stratified), leakage removed inside a pipeline, overfitting diagnosed against leakage from the pattern of the numbers, a metric that reflects which error is more expensive, a threshold that is moved before any retraining, source faults and serving skew told apart from real drift, and then data managed as a product with contracts, validation, labels of measured quality, and features that can be computed at prediction time.
