Unit Introduction

This unit is the engine that the rest of the units run on. It does not teach one particular technical topic; it gives you five small, reusable tools: a requirements extractor, a spec-matching grid, latency arithmetic, capacity and replica arithmetic, and expected-cost arithmetic. None of them needs more than a phone calculator — according to test-takers' reports, the exam contains no complex calculations — but each one turns the question from “which option looks right?” into “which option survives elimination?”. Because the exam tests the trade-off between two plausible solutions across cost, latency, accuracy and compliance together, mastering these tools raises your score in all seven domains.

Before You Start

The questions in this exam are not memorisation questions. Almost every question is a story from a workplace: a company or a government entity with a problem, followed by four proposed solutions. At least two of them look reasonable. Your task is to choose the solution that is the most suitable for this particular story.

This unit teaches you a fixed way of answering that you apply to every question, plus three kinds of simple calculation that recur in the exam: calculating response time, calculating the number of machines required, and calculating cost. All of them are only multiplication, division and addition.

If you have not read the preparatory Unit 00, read at least its Chapter 3 (Metrics) and Sections 5.2 and 5.5 (speed and cost), because this unit builds directly on them.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

  • Apply the six-step answering procedure to any scenario question, starting by reading the last line.
  • Extract a scenario's requirements into an eight-field card and classify each requirement as hard or soft.
  • Eliminate options with a ✓/✗ grid in spec-matching questions, then choose the cheapest or most suitable of the survivors.
  • Calculate a latency budget along the request path, and distinguish percentiles (p95/p99) from the mean, and time to first token from total time.
  • Calculate per-replica throughput and the number of replicas using Little's Law, with a utilisation target and N+1 redundancy.
  • Compare models by the expected cost of errors, calculate the break-even threshold, and respect capacity constraints before optimising.
  • Calculate the cost of instances and LLM APIs, and use prompt length as a cost lever.

How to Study This Unit

Study this unit with pencil and paper, not by reading alone. Redo every worked calculation yourself before looking at the result, and draw the ✓/✗ grid for each matching example before reading the verdict. Memorise the six-step procedure until you apply it automatically, then return to this unit whenever you study another one: every decision table on the platform is solved with the same tools. Start with it and finish with it; it is the first thing you read and the last thing you review before the exam.

Chapter 1

The Answering Procedure and Reading the Requirements

1.1 The Answering Procedure in Six Steps

In Plain Terms

Imagine you are buying a car and you have conditions: its price must not exceed 100K, it must have 7 seats, and it must be suitable for off-road driving. If you start by visiting showrooms before you write down your conditions, you will fall for a beautiful sports car and forget that you need 7 seats. The solution: write your conditions first, then eliminate every car that does not meet all of them, then choose the cheapest of those left.

This is exactly the way of answering in the exam. The wrong options are designed to be attractive: much cheaper, much faster, or the “most powerful”. If you read them before you understand what is being asked, you will be drawn to them.

Real-World Example

A question says: “A system for classifying customer complaints… what is the most suitable first step?”. The words “first step” mean that the answer is usually something like “measure”, “diagnose” or “collect the data”, not “buy a new model”. If you read the options first, you might have chosen the best final solution, which is not what is asked.

Key Terms
Scenarioالسيناريو
The story the question starts with: who the entity is, what the problem is, and what the conditions are. Almost all of this exam's questions are scenarios.
Distractorالمشتّت
A wrong option designed to look correct. It often meets most of the conditions and violates one important condition.
Over-engineeringالمبالغة الهندسية
A solution bigger, more complex and more expensive than the problem needs. The exam treats it as a mistake, because it rewards the sufficient solution.
Root causeالسبب الجذري
The origin of the problem, not its symptom. Like the cause of a high temperature, not the temperature itself. Treating the symptom without the cause makes the problem come back.
Regulatedمنظَّم
A sector or data subject to regulations and oversight, such as banks, health and government data. The presence of this word means that compliance with regulations is a non-negotiable condition.

What the Exam Tests

Most exam questions are long business scenarios ending in four options, at least two of which are plausible. The difference between guessing and deciding is a fixed procedure applied to every question in the same order:

  1. Read the last line first. “Most appropriate”, “most cost-effective”, “first step”, “which principle” and “root cause” are different questions, and each has a different answer for the same scenario.
  2. Extract the requirements before looking at the options (using the card in Section 1.2). If you read the options first, they bias your reading of the scenario.
  3. Classify every requirement as hard or soft. A hard requirement eliminates; a soft one ranks.
  4. Eliminate every option that breaks any hard requirement — including options that meet two out of three.
  5. From the survivors, choose the cheapest / simplest / lowest-risk. The exam rewards the sufficient solution, and over-engineering is a classic distractor.
  6. Check your choice against the priority words in the scenario: regulated, real-time, limited budget, small team, quickly, at scale.
Requirements before options, elimination before cost, sufficient before perfect.

1.2 The Requirements Extractor

In Plain Terms

This is a “card” of eight boxes that you fill in from the question text before you look at the options. Each box asks a question: how much speed is required? How many requests are there? What level of quality? Is the data personal? What is the budget? How often does the information change? Who is on the team? What exactly is asked?

Not every question fills all the boxes. An empty box is useful information: if the question mentions no budget, then cost is not the deciding factor here, so do not spend time on it.

Real-World Example

“A bank wants a system that responds to customers within one second, serves 500 customers at the same time, and the data must stay in the Kingdom.” The card: speed ≤ 1 second, load 500 concurrent, data personal, banking and inside the Kingdom. The budget was not mentioned, so the cost box is empty. Only now do you look at the options.

Key Terms
95th / 99th percentileالمئين p95 / p99
The time within which 95% (or 99%) of requests finish. “p95 ≤ 300 ms” means that 95 requests out of every 100 finish in 300 milliseconds or less. See Section 3.5 in Unit 00.
Time To First Token — TTFTزمن أول رمز
The time from sending the question until the first word of the language model's answer appears. It is what makes the user feel the system is fast or slow.
Queries Per Second — QPSالطلبات في الثانية
The number of requests that reach the system every second. A measure of the size of the load.
Faithfulnessالأمانة للمصدر
That the model's answer is based on the documents it was given, not on its own invention. An important measure in RAG systems.
Data residencyإقامة البيانات
The geographical place where data is stored and processed. “Inside the Kingdom” means servers physically located in Saudi Arabia.
Driftالانجراف
The data in the real world changes compared with what the model was trained on, so its performance declines over time without the model itself changing.

What the Exam Tests

1.3 Hard versus Soft

In Plain Terms

Not all conditions have the same weight. Some are hard: if not met, the whole solution fails, such as “the data must stay in the Kingdom”. Some are soft: we prefer them but they do not make the solution fail, such as “preferably easy to operate”.

The rule: a hard condition eliminates, and a soft condition only ranks. A soft condition cannot make up for a broken hard condition, however cheap the option is.

Real-World Example

You are looking for an apartment: “the rent must not exceed 4,000 SAR” is a hard condition, and “preferably close to work” is a soft condition. A wonderful apartment next to your work with rent of 5,000 SAR is eliminated. Between two apartments with rent of 3,500, you choose the one closer to work.

Key Terms
Hard requirementمتطلب صارم
A mandatory condition that eliminates any solution that does not meet it. It is signalled by words such as: must, required, no more than, the system requires.
Soft requirementمتطلب مرن
A preference used to choose between the surviving solutions only. It is signalled by words such as: preferably, we would like, if possible.
Service Level Agreement — SLAاتفاقية مستوى الخدمة
A written commitment, often in a contract, to a level of performance: an availability percentage or a response time. Whatever it states is a hard condition.

What the Exam Tests

The wording of a requirement tells you its weight. Learn to catch these words on the first read:

TypeSignal wordsRole in the solution
Hardmust, required, no more than, the system requires, service level agreement (SLA)Eliminates every option that breaks it
Softpreferably, ideally, we would like, if possibleRanks the surviving options only
A soft requirement never beats a hard one. A cheaper option that breaks a hard requirement is wrong, not a “trade-off”.

This is the most frequent trap: a much cheaper or much faster option that violates a single hard constraint (such as keeping data inside the Kingdom). Its appeal in the other numbers is exactly what makes it a successful distractor.

1.4 The Patterns Behind Correct Answers

In Plain Terms

Across hundreds of questions, certain “characters” of the correct answer recur. For example: the correct answer usually diagnoses before it treats, fixes the problem at its source rather than at its result, and chooses the smallest change that is enough.

In contrast, certain “characters” of the wrong answer recur: “retrain the model immediately”, “buy bigger machines”, and “let the AI act on its own without human approval”. This table collects them, so when you hesitate between two options ask: which of them resembles the correct pattern?

Real-World Example

Your car makes a strange noise. The first option: “buy a new car”. The second option: “have it checked at the mechanic to find the source of the noise”. The second is “measure before you change” and “the smallest sufficient change”. In the exam: for a model whose performance has dropped, the option “retrain it with a bigger model” is usually less correct than “check whether the incoming data has changed”.

Key Terms
Data contractعقد البيانات
A written agreement between the team that sends the data and the team that uses it on its shape: column names, types and the range of values. It prevents something from changing suddenly and breaking the model.
Access Control List — ACLقوائم التحكم في الوصول
A list that specifies who has the right to see each document or resource. In retrieval it means that the user is not shown a document that they have no right to see.
Cascade / Routingالتدرّج / التوجيه
Sending the easy cases to a cheap method, and only the hard cases to an expensive method. Like a receptionist who answers the simple questions and transfers the complicated ones to the specialist.
Queueالطابور
A waiting list in which tasks are placed to be processed later in order, instead of the user waiting until they finish. It suits long jobs.
Asynchronous processingالمعالجة غير المتزامنة
That the user submits their request and goes on their way, then is notified of the result when it is ready. Like leaving your clothes at the laundry and collecting them later.
Failure domainنطاق الفشل
A part of the architecture that, if it fails, takes down everything inside it, such as one data centre or one cloud region. Redundancy across two domains means that the failure of one of them does not stop the service.
Shadow / Canaryالتشغيل الظلّي والإطلاق الكناري
Two ways to try a new model safely. Shadow: the new one runs in the background without the user seeing its results. Canary: the new one serves a small percentage of users first. You will see them in detail in Unit 07.

What the Exam Tests

Across all domains, nine patterns recur in the correct answer, and each has a usual wrong alternative that appears among the options. When you hesitate between two options, ask: which one matches the pattern?

Chapter 2

The Spec-Matching Grid

2.1 The Grid and the Arabic Customer-Service Assistant Example

In Plain Terms

This is the hardest kind of question: the question gives you a table with four options and their numbers (speed, quality, location and price), and asks for the best of them. The tool is very simple: a grid of ✓ and ✗ marks.

You write the hard conditions as columns and the options as rows, and put ✓ if the option meets the condition and ✗ if it does not. Any row with a single ✗ is deleted. From the complete rows (✓✓✓) you choose the cheapest. Do not look at the price at all before the deletion.

Real-World Example

The condition: speed ≤ 1.5 seconds. Option B has a speed of 2.4 seconds, so we write ✗. It no longer matters that it is a huge model with 90% quality. A single ✗ is enough to delete it. This is what makes the method fast: you do not need to think about all the numbers, it is enough to find the first ✗.

Key Terms
Spec matchingمطابقة المواصفات
A pattern of questions that gives you requirements and a specification table for several options, and the required answer is the option that meets all the requirements at the lowest cost.
Parameters in billionsحجم النموذج 70B / 8B / 1B
The number of the model's parameters in billions. 70B means 70 billion parameters. The bigger is usually stronger but slower and more expensive and needs bigger machines.
Number precisionFP16 وINT8
The way each number is stored inside the model. FP16 uses 2 bytes per number, and INT8 uses 1 byte. The smaller makes the model lighter and faster, usually with a slight loss of quality. The conversion to the smaller is called quantisation.
Continuous batchingالتجميع المستمر
A way of serving several users together on one machine, by admitting new requests whenever a request finishes, instead of waiting for a whole group to finish. It increases the number of people the machine serves.
Fine-tuned for Arabicمُضبوط للعربية
A model that was retrained with additional training on Arabic texts, so its performance in Arabic improved.
Vendor lock-inالارتهان لمزوّد
That moving to another provider becomes difficult and costly after you have built your system on the services of a particular provider.

What the Exam Tests

The hardest pattern according to test-takers' reports: scenario → three core requirements → a specification table → options A/B/C/D. The winning option meets all three requirements, and is then the cheapest or most suitable among those that do. The tool: a ✓/✗ grid.

The scenario

A telecom company wants an Arabic chat assistant. Requirements: (1) p95 latency to first token ≤ 1.5 seconds; (2) answer quality ≥ 85% on the internal Arabic evaluation set; (3) customer data stays inside the Kingdom. Expected load is 40 requests per second at peak. Required: the most cost-effective option.

2.2 Example (A): Choosing an Embedding Model for Arabic Retrieval

In Plain Terms

Before a RAG system searches the documents, it converts the question and the documents into numbers (vector representations) using a model called an embedding model. This example is about choosing that model.

The most important lesson: the table has two quality columns, one on Arabic data and the other on an English benchmark. What is required is Arabic, so the English column does not matter to you, even if its numbers are higher. The exam puts in shiny numbers that have nothing to do with the question to test whether you know which ones matter.

Real-World Example

You are looking for a translator for an Arabic–English contract. A translator who has won a prize in French translation is no use to you. What matters to you is performance in Arabic and English. The same goes for model E3: weaker in English, but the best in Arabic, and it is the one required.

Key Terms
Embedding modelنموذج التضمين
A model that converts text into a list of numbers (a vector) so that texts close in meaning are close in numbers. The basis of semantic search.
Recall@5الاستدعاء عند k
Of the correct documents for a given question, how many appeared among the top 5 search results? 0.85 means that 85% of the correct documents appear in the top five results.
Benchmarkالمعيار المرجعي
A well-known general test set used to compare models. Useful as a start, but it may not represent your real data.
Dimensionsالأبعاد
The number of numbers in a single vector, such as 768 or 1,024. More dimensions mean more storage space and memory. For example 768 instead of 1,024 saves 25%.
Self-hostingالاستضافة الذاتية
Running the model on your own servers or servers you rent and control, instead of sending requests to an external provider.

2.3 Example (B): A Vector Store for a Growing Knowledge Base

In Plain Terms

Vectors need a place to be stored and searched quickly. This is the vector store. The options here: a small add-on to an existing database, or a managed service run by a provider, or a system you run yourself, or a library inside the application.

Note a special condition: deletion within 24 hours. The Personal Data Protection Law gives a person the right to request deletion of their data, so the system must be able to delete a document quickly. There is also a soft condition: the team is only three people. A system that needs daily maintenance will consume their time, and the team's time is a real cost.

Real-World Example

You are choosing between a cheap small refrigerator that will not be enough for your family after a year, a large refrigerator that needs weekly maintenance by yourself, and a large refrigerator with a maintenance contract. If your time is tight, the third is in fact cheaper, even though its price is higher. This is V2.

Key Terms
Vector store / databaseمخزن المتجهات
A specialised database that stores vectors and quickly finds the closest ones to a given question.
Metadataالبيانات الوصفية
Information about the document, not from its content: who owns it, what its classification is, when it was created, and who has the right to see it. It is used to filter search results.
Filtered searchالبحث المُصفّى
A search that returns only the documents that meet a condition, such as “the documents this user has the right to see”.
Managed serviceخدمة مُدارة
A service the provider runs on your behalf: updates, backup and repair are on them. You pay a little more and save your team's time.
Clusterالعنقود
A group of servers working together as one system. Each server in it is called a node.
Operational burdenالعبء التشغيلي
The continuous human effort to run the system: updates, monitoring, backups and fixing faults.

What the Exam Tests

Requirements: (1) support 20 million vectors within 18 months; (2) search p95 ≤ 50 ms with filtering on access-control (ACL) metadata; (3) delete a document within 24 hours (data-subject requests under the Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL)); with the lowest operational burden for a team of three people.

2.4 Example (C): A Deployment Option under a Service Level Agreement

In Plain Terms

Here there are three conditions: the service is available 99.9% of the time, fast, and its cost is predictable. A challenge is added: in Hajj and Ramadan the load quadruples.

The lesson: one machine, however strong, is a single point of failure. If it fails, the service stops. To achieve 99.9% you need replicas in more than one location. For peaks known in advance, such as Ramadan, capacity is prepared before them on a schedule; you do not wait until the system gets crowded.

Real-World Example

A restaurant knows that Ramadan nights are busy. The smart restaurant schedules extra staff from the start of the month, and does not wait until the queues get long and then call staff who arrive an hour later. If the restaurant has only one branch, a power cut there stops the whole service, whereas two branches in two different neighbourhoods ensure continuity.

Key Terms
Availabilityالتوفّر
The percentage of time that the service is working. 99.9% monthly means that the permitted downtime is only about 43 minutes a month.
Single point of failureنقطة الفشل الواحدة
A single component that, if it fails, stops the whole system, because there is no alternative.
Availability zone / regionمنطقة التوفر
A data centre or a group of centres separated in power and network. Spreading replicas across two regions means that the failure of one does not stop the service.
Autoscaling / scheduled scalingالتوسع التلقائي والمجدول
Automatic: the system adds replicas when the load actually rises. Scheduled: we add the replicas in advance at times we know will be busy.
Serverlessبلا خادم
A service in which you manage no server. It runs only when a request arrives and you pay per use. Its drawback with large models is the cold start.
Cold startالبدء البارد
The delay when starting a new replica from zero, because it needs to load the model into memory. It can reach tens of seconds or minutes with large models.

What the Exam Tests

Requirements: (1) 99.9% monthly availability for a citizen-facing service; (2) p95 ≤ 800 ms; (3) predictable cost with peaks of 4 times the average in the Hajj and Ramadan seasons.

D2 · Two replicas in one region, manual scaling~99.8% (a regional outage takes everything down)1,900 ms at peakFixed, and short of capacity at peaks
D3 · Replicas across two regions, autoscaling + pre-scheduled seasonal scaling, and a reserved minimum≥ 99.9%650 msReserved minimum + elastic peaks

Elimination: D1 and D2 fail on availability; D2 also fails on peak latency; and D4 fails on latency (cold starts) for a citizen-facing service. D3 is the only design that meets all three.

  • Availability targets mean redundancy across multiple zones.
  • Seasonal peaks mean scheduled scaling in addition to metric-based scaling.
  • Predictable cost comes from a reserved minimum, not from a fixed fleet sized for the peak.

2.5 Example (D): OCR and Extraction for Invoices

In Plain Terms

A company wants to extract data (name, amount and date) from 50,000 invoices every night. The conditions: high accuracy, finishing the work within 6 hours, and a low cost per invoice.

The winning solution does not use the strongest model on all the invoices. It uses a cheap method for the clear invoices (about 90%), and sends only the hard invoices to the strong, expensive model. This is the cascade, and one of the most frequently winning patterns in the exam.

Real-World Example

The calculation step by step: option O1 processes 4,000 invoices per hour. 50,000 ÷ 4,000 = 12.5 hours, and only 6 hours are available, so it is eliminated. Option O2 processes 12,000 per hour: 50,000 ÷ 12,000 ≈ 4.2 hours, and that is less than 6. Its cost is $0.006 per invoice, which is below the limit of $0.01. So O2.

Key Terms
Optical Character Recognition — OCRالتعرف الضوئي على الحروف
A technology that reads text from an image or a scanned document, converting the image of the words into text that can be searched and processed.

Latency and Capacity Arithmetic

3.1 Percentiles, Not Means

In Plain Terms

The “mean” deceives you in response times. If 99 requests are very fast and one request is very slow, the mean looks good, but the owner of the slow request is angry. Companies care about the angry user.

That is why speed is measured by percentiles: p50 is the typical user (half of the requests are faster than it), and p95 / p99 represent the slowest requests. Specialists call the slowest requests “the tail”.

Real-World Example

In a clinic, the mean wait is 15 minutes. But 5 patients out of every 100 wait two hours. The mean does not reveal this problem, and p95 reveals it immediately. If the question says “some users complain of intermittent slowness”, it is talking about the tail.

Key Terms
Median / p50الوسيط p50
The value that half of the requests are faster than and half slower than. It represents the typical experience.
Tail latencyكمون الذيل
The response time of the slowest requests (p95, p99). It is what the affected users feel, and agreements are written on it.

What the Exam Tests

p50 is the typical user; p95/p99 is the affected user, and service level agreements are written on them. A system whose mean latency is 120 ms may have a p99 of 2 seconds.

If the scenario says “users complain of intermittent slowness”, the metric required is tail latency (p95/p99), not the mean.

3.2 The Latency Budget along the Request Path

In Plain Terms

A user's request in a RAG system passes through several stations: it arrives over the network, then the question is converted into a vector, then the documents are searched, then the results are ranked, then the model starts writing, then completes the answer. Each station takes part of the time, and the total time is their sum.

When the system is slow, do not buy machines for all the stations. Find the station that takes the most time and treat it alone. Often the biggest station is the model's generation.

Real-World Example

Your trip from home to the airport is 90 minutes: 10 minutes to leave, 70 minutes on the road, and 10 minutes to park. Speeding up leaving home will not change much. What matters is changing the road. Likewise, the formula says: model time ≈ time to first token + (number of answer tokens × time per token). An answer of 250 tokens at 25 ms per token = 6.25 seconds, and that is much larger than any other station.

Key Terms
Latency budgetميزانية الكمون
Distributing the entire permitted time (such as 1.5 seconds) over the stages of the request, so that each team knows its stage's share.
Authenticationالمصادقة
Verifying the identity of the user or system sending the request, such as logging in.
Approximate Nearest Neighbor — ANNالفهرس التقريبي لأقرب جار
A search method that finds vectors very close to the question very quickly without comparing all the vectors, in return for a small chance of missing the exact closest. HNSW and IVF are names of methods of this kind.
Rerankingإعادة الترتيب
A second step after the search: a more accurate model reorders the best results (such as the top 20) to put the most suitable at the top. More accurate but slower, which is why it works on a small number.
Time Per Output Token — TPOTزمن الرمز الواحد
The time the model takes to write each token of the answer after the first token.
Speculative decodingفك الترميز التخميني
A trick to speed up generation: a small, fast model guesses several tokens, and the large model checks them in one go.
Prompt cachingالتخزين المؤقت للموجّه
Saving the processing of the fixed part of the prompt, such as long system instructions, to reuse in every request instead of computing it every time. It saves time and cost.

What the Exam Tests

Latency budgets accumulate along the request path. In a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) assistant the stages look like this, and each has a lever if it is slow:

Query embedding5–30 msA small embedding model, GPU or batching, caching
Vector search5–50 msAn approximate index (ANN: HNSW/IVF), filtering inside the index, more replicas

LLM latency ≈ TTFT + N_out × TPOT

where TPOT is the time for each output token (Time Per Output Token). When a question asks you to reduce latency, first identify which stage is consuming the budget, then pick its lever — do not scale up all the hardware.

3.3 Time to First Token, Total Time and Streaming

In Plain Terms

Streaming means that the answer appears to the user word by word as it is being written, instead of the user waiting until it is complete. The user sees something in less than half a second, so they feel the system is fast.

But note: streaming does not make the answer complete faster, and does not reduce the cost. It changes only the feeling. If the consumer is another programme waiting for the complete answer, streaming does not help it at all.

Real-World Example

You are in a restaurant and the waiter brings the salad first and then the main dish. You feel that the service is faster, although the whole meal took the same time. But if you were ordering takeaway food, there is no use in bringing the salad first: you will wait until everything is ready.

Key Terms
Streamingالبث التدريجي
Sending the answer to the user part by part while it is being generated. It reduces the time the user feels, and does not reduce the total time or the cost.
Perceived latencyالزمن المُدرَك
The time the user feels until they see a response, as opposed to the actual time until the work is complete.

What the Exam Tests

Worked example

TTFT 400 ms + 250 tokens × 25 ms = 0.4 + 6.25 ≈ 6.7 s

Streaming lets the user see text after 0.4 seconds, but it does not reduce the total time or the cost.

“Total completion time for an API consumer”No — reduce output tokens or use a faster model

3.4 Queues Blow Up the Tail

In Plain Terms

When the system is busy 60% of the time, requests pass smoothly. But as it approaches 100%, queues begin to form, and the waiting increases much faster than the increase in load. This is called “non-linear” behaviour: the last increase in load has the greatest effect.

That is why nobody designs a system to run at 100% of its capacity. Systems are set to add new replicas when utilisation reaches 60–75%.

Real-World Example

A motorway at half capacity: cars move fast. At 90% it is enough for one driver to press the brakes and a jam forms that stretches for kilometres. The extra cars are few, but their effect on the waiting is huge.

Key Terms
Utilisationنسبة الاستخدام
The percentage of time or capacity in which the machine is actually working. 70% means it is busy at 70% of its capacity.
Non-linearغير خطي
A relationship in which the result is not proportional to the cause at a fixed ratio. A small increase in load near the maximum causes a large increase in waiting.

What the Exam Tests

As utilisation approaches 100%, waiting time grows non-linearly. A service at 90% utilisation may have a p99 several times its value for the same service at 60%.

This is why autoscaling targets are usually set at 60–75% utilisation, not 100%.

3.5 Little's Law and the Number of Replicas

In Plain Terms

How many machines (replicas) do we need to serve the peak? The method is three steps: (1) calculate how many requests per second one replica serves, (2) do not calculate it at full capacity but at a safe percentage such as 70%, (3) divide the total load by this capacity, and add a spare replica.

The spare replica is called N+1: if you need N replicas, you run N+1, so that if one fails the capacity remains sufficient.

Real-World Example

A kitchen with 8 cooks, each of whom finishes a dish in 0.2 hours. So the kitchen produces 8 ÷ 0.2 = 40 dishes per hour. We do not want it pushed to 100%, so we calculate 70%: 40 × 0.7 = 28 dishes. The demand at peak is 600 dishes, so we need 600 ÷ 28 = 21.4, and we always round up: 22 kitchens. We add a spare kitchen: 23. The symbol ⌈ ⌉ means “round up”.

Key Terms
Little's Lawقانون ليتل
The number of requests in flight = the arrival rate of requests × the time a request stays in the system. For example: 10 requests/second × 2 seconds = 20 requests at any moment.
Replicaالنسخة
An identical copy of the service running on a separate machine. Increasing the replicas increases the number of requests the system can bear.
High Availability — HAالتوفر العالي
A design that ensures the service continues when part of it fails, usually through the presence of spare replicas.
N+1 redundancyN+1
Running one extra replica above what the load needs, so that the service can withstand the failure of a replica without being affected.
Ceiling ⌈x⌉التقريب للأعلى
Rounding the number up to the higher whole number. 21.4 becomes 22, because we cannot run 0.4 of a machine.

What the Exam Tests

Little's Law: the number of concurrent requests in flight = arrival rate × time in the system. From it we calculate per-replica throughput and the number of replicas:

L = λ × W

throughput per replica = concurrent slots per replica ÷ latency per request

replicas = ⌈ peak QPS ÷ (throughput per replica × target utilisation) ⌉ (+1 for HA)

Worked example (a high-load scenario)

  1. Peak is 600 requests/second. One replica handles 8 concurrent requests, each taking 200 ms.
  2. Replica throughput: 8 ÷ 0.2 = 40 requests/second.
  3. At 100% utilisation: 600 ÷ 40 = 15 replicas.
  4. At a 70% utilisation target: effective capacity 40 × 0.7 = 28, so you need ⌈600 ÷ 28⌉ = 22 replicas.
  5. Plus one replica for redundancy (N+1) = 23 replicas.
  6. Off-peak at 60 requests/second: ⌈60 ÷ 28⌉ + 1 = 4 replicas → autoscaling saves about 80% of replica-hours off-peak.

Expected-Cost and Running-Cost Arithmetic

4.1 The Expected Cost of Errors

In Plain Terms

Not all errors have the same price. In a bank, for the system to stop a legitimate transaction (a false alarm) costs little: an employee reviews it and a customer is annoyed. But to miss real fraud may cost thousands of riyals.

That is why we do not compare models by the number of their errors, but by the price of their errors. We multiply the number of each type of error by its price and add up. The best model is the one with the lowest total.

Real-World Example

Model A makes 100 errors, all false alarms at 10 SAR: 100 × 10 = 1,000 SAR. Model B makes only 5 errors, all missed fraud at 5,000 SAR: 5 × 5,000 = 25,000 SAR. Model B's errors are fewer in number, but it is much more expensive.

Key Terms
Expected costالتكلفة المتوقعة
The sum of (the number of each type of error × the price of a single error). The correct way to compare when the prices of errors differ.
False Positive (FP) / False Negative (FN)الإيجابي الكاذب والسلبي الكاذب
FP: the model said “yes” and reality is “no”, that is, a false alarm. FN: the model said “no” and reality is “yes”, that is, a missed case. See Section 3.1 in Unit 00.

What the Exam Tests

When the prices of errors differ, the best model is the one with the lowest expected cost — not the highest overall accuracy and not the highest F1.

Expected cost = FP × C_FP + FN × C_FN

where FP is the number of false positives and C_FP the cost of each one, and FN is the number of false negatives and C_FN the cost of each one.

4.2 Worked Example: Bank Fraud (an Exam Question)

In Plain Terms

This is a real question relayed by test-takers: three models for detecting fraud, each with different precision and recall. The required answer is the most suitable.

The trick is that the table does not give you the number of errors directly. You calculate it from the ratios, then multiply each error by its price. You will find that the model with the worst precision is the cheapest, because it catches most of the fraud, and missing fraud is the expensive error.

Real-World Example

The calculation for model Z step by step. The number of real fraud cases is 2,000. Recall is 0.97, so it caught 0.97 × 2,000 = 1,940, and missed 2,000 − 1,940 = 60. Its precision is 0.20, that is, only 20% of what it flagged is real fraud, so it flagged 1,940 ÷ 0.20 = 9,700 transactions, of which 9,700 − 1,940 = 7,760 were false alarms. The cost: 60 × 7,000 = 420,000, and 7,760 × 10 = 77,600, and the total ≈ 0.50 million dollars. That is less than Y (1.42 million) and X (4.20 million).

Fraud rate / prevalenceنسبة الاحتيال
The percentage of real positive cases in the data. 0.2% of a million = 2,000 cases.
Analystالمحلل
The employee who manually reviews suspicious transactions. Their time is the cost of a false alarm.

What the Exam Tests

The cost of a false positive (a legitimate transaction flagged as fraud → an analyst's review and customer annoyance) = $10. The cost of a false negative (missing real fraud) = $7,000. One million transactions a month, and a fraud rate of 0.2% (2,000 cases).

4.3 The Twist: Capacity Constraint First, Then Optimisation

In Plain Terms

The same question may come with a small addition that flips the answer: “the review team can handle only 3,000 alerts per month”. Now we have a new hard condition: every model that fires more than 3,000 alerts is eliminated, however low its expected cost.

The order is always: hard conditions first, then preference. Exactly as you learned in Section 1.3.

Real-World Example

Model Z fires 9,700 alerts per month. If the review team can handle only 3,000, then 6,700 cases will pile up unreviewed every month. That is, the “best” model on paper cannot be run in reality. The smarter solution in the question: take model Y and raise its threshold until its number of alerts drops to 3,000.

Key Terms

An upper limit on what a team or a system can handle, such as the number of alerts the employees review per month.

Every case the model flags as suspicious. Their number = true positives + false positives (TP + FP).

4.4 The Break-Even Ratio and Where to Put the Threshold

In Plain Terms

The model gives each transaction a probability of being fraud. When do we stop it? There is no reason for the threshold to be 50%. We stop it when the price of leaving it is greater than the price of reviewing it.

If missing fraud is 700 times more expensive than a review, a very small probability is enough for us to decide to review. That is why the optimal threshold here falls to about 0.14%.

Real-World Example

Everyday thinking: if forgetting to lock the house door costs 100,000 SAR if it is burgled, and checking the lock costs you two seconds, you will check it even if the probability of having forgotten it is only 1%. With the numbers here: the price of a review is 10, and the price of a miss is 7,000. We stop the transaction if the probability of fraud is greater than 10 ÷ (10 + 7,000) = 10 ÷ 7,010 ≈ 0.0014, that is, 0.14%.

Key Terms
Break-evenنقطة التعادل
The value at which the costs of two options are equal. Above it one becomes cheaper, and below it the other.
Optimal thresholdالعتبة المثلى
The probability value above which the model treats the case as positive, chosen to make the total cost as low as possible.

What the Exam Tests

If the review cost is paid only on wrongly flagged cases (as in the table), flag the transaction when:

P × C_FN > (1 − P) × C_FP ⇒ P > C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN) = 10 / 7,010 ≈ 0.14%

If the review cost is paid on every flag (fraud or not), the rule is:

P > C_FP / C_FN

When C_FN is much larger than C_FP, the two formulas become practically equal. In both cases the optimal threshold lies far below 0.5. (Charging $10 on the 1,940 true positives of model Z as well adds about $19,000 and changes no ranking.)

The default threshold of 0.5 is not sacred: when a miss is hundreds of times more expensive, the optimal threshold falls to a fraction of one percent.

4.5 Cost Arithmetic on One Page

In Plain Terms

Almost all cost questions depend on two formulas. The first is for machines: hourly price × number of hours × number of machines. The second is for language models via an API: number of requests × (question tokens × their price + answer tokens × their price).

Two important pieces of information: answer tokens are usually three to five times more expensive than question tokens. Arabic text often needs more tokens than English for the same meaning, so it costs more.

Real-World Example

A machine priced at 3 dollars per hour running 24 hours for 30 days: 3 × 24 × 30 = 2,160 dollars per month. If it were scheduled to run only 12 hours a day: 3 × 12 × 30 = 1,080 dollars. Half the cost just by changing the running schedule.

4.6 Worked Example: Prompt Length Is a Cost Lever

In Plain Terms

Every request sent to the model carries with it the full system prompt: the long text that explains to the model its role and rules. If it is 1,000 tokens, and a million requests come, you pay for a billion instruction tokens, most of it repeated text.

That is why shortening the instructions or caching them is one of the fastest ways to cut the bill, and it does not require changing the model.

Real-World Example

The calculation step by step. Question tokens: a million requests × 1,500 tokens = 1,500 million tokens. At a price of $0.50 per million tokens: 1,500 × 0.50 = $750. Answer tokens: a million × 300 = 300 million tokens × 1.50 = $450. The total is $1,200. Shortening the instructions by 500 tokens saves 500 million tokens × 0.50 = $250, that is, about 21% of the bill.

Key Terms
System promptتعليمات النظام
Fixed text that the developer writes and that is sent with every request, defining for the model its role, style and limits.
Input / output tokensرمز المدخل والمخرج
Input tokens: everything we send to the model (the instructions, the question and the documents). Output tokens: what the model writes. Each is charged at a different price.

What the Exam Tests

One million requests a month, each request 1,500 input tokens + 300 output tokens, and prices of $0.50 / $1.50 per million tokens:

input: 1M × 1,500 = 1,500M tokens × $0.50/M = $750

output: 1M × 300 = 300M tokens × $1.50/M = $450 → total $1,200 / month

Shortening the system prompt from 1,000 to 500 tokens saves 500 million input tokens = $250 per month (−21%) without touching the model.

Prompt length is a cost lever — and the system prompt is paid for with every request unless prompt caching is used.

Applied Scenario

Apply the whole procedure — extracting the requirements, elimination, then capacity and cost arithmetic — to a single scenario that brings together the unit's tools.

The Scenario

A government entity in Riyadh launches an Arabic assistant to answer citizens' enquiries about its services, built on retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) over procedural manuals that are updated weekly. The data includes ID numbers and personal enquiries, and “must” stay inside the Kingdom. The contract requires a p95 time to first token ≤ 1.5 seconds and quality ≥ 85% on an internal Arabic evaluation set, and the solution is “preferably” easy to operate for a team of four engineers.

The expected peak is 600 requests/second on results-announcement days, and 60 requests/second on ordinary days. One replica of the candidate option handles 8 concurrent requests at 200 ms per request. The average request is 1,500 input tokens, of which 1,000 are system-prompt tokens, and 300 output tokens. The manager asks you: which option is the most suitable, how many replicas do we need at peak and off-peak, and what is the fastest way to cut the token bill?

Solution Steps

  1. Read the last line: three things are asked — the most suitable option, the number of replicas, and the first cost lever.
  2. Fill in the requirements card: Speed p95 TTFT ≤ 1.5 s; Volume 600/60 requests per second; Quality ≥ 85%; Data personal and inside the Kingdom; Change weekly; People a team of four.
  3. Classify: latency, quality and data residency are hard (“requires”, “must”); ease of operation is soft (“preferably”) — it ranks and does not eliminate.
  4. Apply the ✓/✗ grid to the options: eliminate any external API however good its quality, any model that exceeds 1.5 s, and any model below 85%; then choose the cheapest of the survivors, and break ties with the soft constraint.
  5. Calculate capacity: replica throughput = 8 ÷ 0.2 = 40 requests/second; at a 70% target = 28; peak ⌈600 ÷ 28⌉ + 1 = 23 replicas; ordinary days ⌈60 ÷ 28⌉ + 1 = 4 replicas.
  6. Propose autoscaling with scheduled scaling ahead of the known results-announcement days, and replicas spread across two regions.
  7. The first cost lever: shorten the system prompt or enable prompt caching, because the 1,000 tokens are paid for with every request — the smallest sufficient change before changing the model.
  8. Check against the priority words: “regulated” and “personal data” confirm that compliance comes before price.

Exam Traps

  • Choosing the external API because it has the highest quality and is the cheapest — it breaks a hard requirement, so it is wrong, not a “trade-off”.
  • Sizing the fleet at 100% utilisation (15 replicas) — it blows up tail latency at peak.
  • Forgetting the N+1 replica or putting all replicas in one region.
  • Proposing streaming to cut total time or cost — streaming reduces only perceived time.
  • Judging latency by the mean instead of the p95 stipulated in the contract.
  • Jumping to a bigger model or more GPUs before measuring which stage is consuming the latency budget.
What You Must Master

A written decision of three lines: the surviving option with the reason each competitor was eliminated, the number of replicas at peak and off-peak with the calculation, and the first lever for cutting cost.

    0.4 + 6.25 ≈ 6.7 seconds. Streaming lets the user see text after 0.4 seconds, but it does not reduce the total time or the cost; it is the fix only if the requirement is the time until the first content appears.

  6. 6Why are autoscaling targets usually set at 60–75% rather than 100%?
    Answer

    Because queue waiting time grows non-linearly as utilisation approaches 100%, so p99 multiplies at 90% compared with 60%.

  7. 7A peak of 600 requests/second, and a replica handles 8 concurrent requests at 200 ms. How many replicas do you need at a 70% target with N+1?
    Answer

    Throughput is 8 ÷ 0.2 = 40 and effective throughput 40 × 0.7 = 28; ⌈600 ÷ 28⌉ = 22, plus one for redundancy = 23 replicas.

  8. 8In the bank-fraud example, why is Z chosen even though its precision is 0.20?
    Answer

    Because its total expected cost is about 0.50 million dollars versus 1.42 for Y and 4.20 for X; missing one fraud ($7,000) is equivalent to 700 false alarms ($10).

  9. 9A constraint is added: the review team can handle at most 3,000 alerts per month. What changes?
    Answer

    Z (9,700 alerts) and Y (3,600) become infeasible, and only X (1,556) fits the constraint; the better course is to re-tune Y's threshold to produce ≤ 3,000 alerts with the highest recall possible. Constraints first, then optimisation.

  10. 10One million requests a month at 1,500 input and 300 output tokens, priced at $0.50 / $1.50 per million tokens. What is the bill, and how much does shortening the system prompt from 1,000 to 500 tokens save?
    Answer

    750 + 450 = $1,200 per month; and the shortening saves 500 million input tokens = $250 (−21%) without changing the model.

  1. 1F1 versus expected costHard

    A factory inspects 10,000 parts a day; 2% (200) are defective. Shipping a defective part costs SAR 900 in warranty and recall; sending a good part to manual re-inspection costs SAR 30. Model A has precision 0.80 and recall 0.70 (F1 ≈ 0.75); Model B has precision 0.40 and recall 0.90 (F1 ≈ 0.55). The re-inspection station can handle 600 parts a day. Which model should the plant use?

    Explanation

    Decide by expected cost, not F1. A: TP = 0.70 × 200 = 140, FN = 60; flags = 140 ÷ 0.80 = 175, so FP = 35; cost = 60 × 900 + 35 × 30 = 54,000 + 1,050 = SAR 55,050 a day. B: TP = 180, FN = 20; flags = 180 ÷ 0.40 = 450, FP = 270; cost = 20 × 900 + 270 × 30 = 18,000 + 8,100 = SAR 26,100 a day. B halves the cost, and its 450 flags fit the 600-part capacity, so the constraint holds. F1 weights precision and recall equally, which is wrong when a miss costs 30 times a re-inspection.

  2. 2Percentile SLAs versus averagesHard

    An internal document-search API has a contractual SLA of p95 latency ≤ 800 ms per calendar month, with no maintenance exclusions. The operations dashboard shows a monthly mean of 210 ms, and the team reports the SLA as met. Users complain of occasional freezes. Request logs show that over the month 7% of requests took longer than 1.5 s, almost all during nightly bulk-import windows that share the same database. Which assessment is correct?

    Explanation

    The memo's first latency rule: percentiles, not averages — p95/p99 is what SLAs are written on, and a low mean can hide a slow tail. If 7% of the month's requests exceed 1.5 s, more than 5% of requests are slower than 1.5 s, so the 95th percentile is itself above 1.5 s — well over 800 ms. The SLA is therefore breached even though the mean is 210 ms. The right response is to report the contracted percentile and remove the cause, here resource contention with the bulk import (for example by isolating or rescheduling it). Changing the contract or excluding windows the contract does not exclude hides the breach.

  3. 3Replica count with headroom and N+1Medium

    A bank's real-time fraud-scoring service must handle 900 requests/s at peak. Load tests show one replica serves 12 concurrent requests with an average time in system of 300 ms. The SRE team targets 75% utilisation to protect tail latency and requires N+1 redundancy. How many replicas are needed at peak?

    Explanation

    By Little's Law, per-replica throughput = concurrency ÷ time in system = 12 ÷ 0.3 = 40 req/s. At a 75% target each replica should carry 40 × 0.75 = 30 req/s. Replicas = ⌈900 ÷ 30⌉ = 30, plus one for N+1 = 31. Sizing at 100% utilisation (⌈900/40⌉ = 23) would push the service into the queueing zone where p99 explodes.

  9. 9Mean vs tail latencyMedium
