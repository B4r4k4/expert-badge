Unit Introduction

This is the heaviest domain in the exam (about 27 questions out of 150), and those who have sat it say the operational topics are "harder than you expect". Its questions are mostly stories: a model launch failed — how should it have been rolled out? Monitoring missed a regression — what should have been monitored? Manual retraining is slow — what do we automate? A language feature degraded after a prompt change — which process was missing? This unit builds the answer to these questions layer by layer: the model lifecycle and its automation, then deployment, monitoring and drift, then reliability, incidents and resilience, and finally what differs in LLM operations (LLMOps) and how model, vendor and ethics risks are managed within day-to-day operations.

Before You Start

This unit is about life after the build: how to move a model from a successful experiment to a system that runs safely every day, how to launch it without harming users, how to monitor it and know when it degrades, what to do when things break, and how to govern and document it. It is the heaviest domain of the exam (Domain 4, about 27 questions out of 150).

The word MLOps means "machine learning operations": practices that make models get built, deployed and monitored regularly and reliably, as DevOps does for software. LLMOps is the version specific to language models.

You need these from earlier units first: from Unit 00, Section 5.4 (deployment, versioning and monitoring); from Unit 02, Section 3.6 (types of drift); and from Unit 01, Sections 2.4 and 3.5 (availability and replicas).

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

  • Diagnose a team's ML operations maturity level (0/1/2) and identify the correct next step.
  • Choose the right deployment strategy (shadow, canary, blue–green, A/B, multi-armed bandit, rolling update) from the constraints of the scenario.
  • Design four-layer monitoring, read drift indicators (PSI, KS, chi-square and others) and make the retraining decision with discipline.
  • Calculate the error budget and end-to-end availability, and choose a disaster recovery strategy according to RPO and RTO.
  • Order the incident-response steps and design burn-rate alerts and a fallback chain for graceful degradation.
  • Distinguish what differs in LLMOps: version pinning, the evaluation harness, the LLM judge, guardrails and cost levers.
  • Apply model risk management: inventory, tiering, independent validation, the documentation required for audit, and due diligence on vendors.

Chapter 1

Lifecycle, Automation and Model Governance

1.1 The ML Lifecycle and Maturity Levels

In Plain Terms

ML teams pass through three maturity levels. Level 0 (manual): the scientist trains the model on their own computer, hands a file to the engineers, and nobody monitors after launch. Level 1 (automated training pipeline): training runs automatically and repeats when new data arrives. Level 2 (full automation): even the training pipeline's own code is tested and deployed automatically.

The usual exam question is: "What is this team's next step?". The answer is always the very next level, not a jump to the tools of the higher level. A team at level 0 starts with version control and a repeatable training pipeline, not a full platform.

Real-World Example

A small restaurant prepares food with no written recipes (level 0). The correct next step: write the recipes and standardise the quantities so the dish is the same every day. Jumping straight to a "fully automated kitchen with robots" before even writing the recipes will fail, because the machines do not know what to cook.

Key Terms
MLOpsعمليات تعلّم الآلة
Practices and tools for building, deploying, monitoring and updating models in an automated, reliable and repeatable way.
Maturity levels (0/1/2)مستويات النضج
A classification of how automated a team is: 0 manual, 1 automated training pipeline, 2 full automation of continuous integration and delivery.
Continuous Delivery / Deployment — CDالتسليم المستمر
Releasing changes to production automatically, through successive stages, after they pass the tests.
Continuous Training — CTالتدريب المستمر
Retraining the model automatically on a schedule, when new data arrives, or when performance drops.
Triggerالمُطلِق
An event that starts a process automatically, such as "10,000 new labels arrived" or "recall fell below 0.85".

What the Exam Tests

CI / CD / CT

  • Continuous Integration: tests the code, the data and the model together — schema checks, unit tests and evaluation tests.
  • Continuous Delivery/Deployment: releases the service and the model through successive stages.
  • Continuous Training: retrains automatically on a schedule or on a trigger: drift, arrival of new labelled data, or a performance drop.
A team at level 0 does not start with a full CI/CD platform; it starts with version control and a reproducible training pipeline.

1.2 What Gets Versioned and Tracked

In Plain Terms

To be able to rebuild any model exactly, you must keep a numbered version of everything that produced it: the code, the data, the experiments and their results, the model itself, the environment it ran in (the libraries and their versions), and, for language models, the prompts and the RAG index version as well.

Lineage means being able to answer the question: "For the model running in production now: which data, which code and which settings produced it, and who approved it?". This is what the auditor asks for first.

Real-World Example

An auditor from the regulator asks: "Why did your model reject this customer's financing application in March?". To answer you need: the version of the model that was running in March, the data it was trained on, the settings, and who approved it. If these were not kept under version control, you have no answer and no evidence.

A common system for controlling code versions: it keeps every change, who made it and when, and allows returning to any version.

Recording every training experiment: the settings, the data, the results and the output files, so they can be compared later.
Lock fileملف القفل
A file that records the exact versions of every library used, so exactly the same environment is built every time.

A central store of prompt versions, like the model registry, to track which prompt was running and when.

1.3 Pipelines & Orchestration

In Plain Terms

A pipeline turns "a notebook that worked once" into a process that repeats the same way every time. Its stages in order: data ingestion → validating it → building features → splitting → training → evaluation → comparison with the current production model → registration → approval → deployment → monitoring.

An orchestrator is a program that schedules these steps, runs them in order and tracks them. The exam rewards particular properties: idempotent steps, caching of the steps that did not change, gates that stop promotion automatically if a check fails, environment separation, and infrastructure as code. A tip: a small team on a managed cloud platform uses its native tools rather than building a complex cluster itself.

Real-World Example

A factory production line: raw materials come in, then are inspected, then cut, then assembled, then tested, then packaged. If a product fails the test, it is not packaged or shipped (the gate). If only the colour of the packaging changed, we do not remanufacture the product from the start; we only repeat the packaging (step caching).

Key Terms
Pipelineخط العمل
An ordered chain of automated steps, each with specified inputs and outputs, executed the same way every time.
Orchestrator (Airflow, Kubeflow)المنسِّق
A system that schedules the pipeline's steps, runs them in the correct order, retries on failure and records what happened.
Gateالبوابة
An automated check that stops the pipeline's progress if a condition is not met, such as "performance is below the current model".
Infrastructure as Code (Terraform, Helm)البنية كشيفرة
Describing servers, networks and settings in versioned text files, instead of configuring them by hand, so they can be rebuilt and reviewed.
GitOpsGitOps
An approach in which the state of deployed systems always matches what is in the Git repository. A change is a pull request, and a rollback is a revert to the previous version.
Pull requestطلب الدمج
A proposed change to the code that colleagues review before it is merged into the main version.

What the Exam Tests

An ML pipeline turns "a notebook that worked once" into a repeatable, auditable process. The typical stages:

ingest → validate data → build features → split → train → evaluate → compare with production → register → (approve) → deploy → monitor

Each stage is a step inside a container with declared inputs and outputs, so any run can be reproduced from its code, data snapshot, parameters and environment, all of them versioned.

Orchestrators schedule and track these steps: general workflow engines (Airflow, Prefect, Dagster) and ML-specific pipeline systems (Kubeflow Pipelines, TFX, cloud pipeline services). Choose according to the team's skills and platform: a scenario of "a small team already working on a managed cloud ML platform" points to its native pipelines, not to a self-managed Kubeflow cluster.

Pipeline properties the exam rewards

  • Idempotent steps: re-running a step with the same inputs gives the same outputs, with no duplication.
  • Caching of unchanged steps: do not recompute features if only the training parameters changed.
  • Lineage capture: every asset records what produced it.
  • Gates: a failed data validation or evaluation stops promotion automatically.
  • Environment separation: development, test (staging) and production, with promotion between them and controlled access to production data.
  • Infrastructure as code (Terraform, Helm) and GitOps: the deployed state equals what is in version control; changes are reviewed pull requests, and rollback is simply a revert.

1.4 Production Launch Checklist

In Plain Terms

Before launching any model, here is the checklist in eight areas: data (documented, lawful, no leakage), evaluation (better than the current model with statistical confidence, no regression in critical cases), performance (speed under load), security, governance (documentation and approvals), operations (monitoring, runbook, on-call owner and rollback plan), rollout (a gradual plan with stop criteria), and communication (support knows, and the user is informed).

In "What was missing?" questions, look for the line that was not carried out. The important rule: stop criteria are set before the rollout, not during it, otherwise the decision becomes impressionistic under pressure.

Real-World Example

Before takeoff the pilot goes through a written checklist, however experienced: fuel, wings, instruments and the emergency plan. They do not decide "when we return to the airport" after the problem appears in the air; the rules are set in advance: "if this happens, we return immediately".

Key Terms
Runbookدليل التشغيل
A written document of steps for what to do in specific situations, such as "if this alert appears: check this, then roll back with that".
On-callالمناوبة
A person responsible by role for responding to system failures at a given time, even outside working hours.
Warm standby versionالإصدار الدافئ
The previous version is ready to run immediately when needed, with no loading time.
Stop criteriaمعايير الإيقاف
Conditions set in advance for stopping the rollout and rolling back, such as "if the error rate rises above 1% or complaints above 20%".

1.5 Feature Stores, Model Registries and Approval Paths

In Plain Terms

Three governance tools. The feature store (you saw it in Unit 02): each feature is defined once and used in training and serving in the same way. It earns its cost when several models share features, or real-time features are needed. The model registry: an official archive of every version, its state (staging, production, archived), and who approved it. Deployment points to the registry, and rollback = promoting the previous version. The model card: a document that describes the model.

approvals scale with risk: a low-risk model may be promoted automatically after passing the checks, whereas a high-risk model (credit, health, hiring or public services) needs human sign-off from named people, recorded in the registry.

Real-World Example

The model card is like a medicine leaflet: why it is used (the intended use), who it is not suitable for (the out-of-scope use), how it was tested and the results, the side effects (the limitations), and who the manufacturer is (the owner). This leaflet is the first thing the auditor asks for.

Key Terms
Registry stagesمراحل السجل
The model's states in the registry: none → staging → production → archived.
Model cardبطاقة النموذج
A document that describes the intended and out-of-scope uses, the training data, performance overall and by slice, fairness, limitations, and the owner.
Conformity assessmentتقييم المطابقة
A formal check that the system conforms to the requirements and principles, before and after deployment. The SDAIA principles require it for high-risk systems.

What the Exam Tests

Feature store

It defines each feature once (code + metadata + owner) and materialises it in an offline store that holds historical values for training with point-in-time-correct joins, so that a training row sees only the feature values that existed at that moment, and in an online store that holds the latest values for fast key lookups during serving.

  • It removes training–serving skew, enables reuse across teams, and documents lineage.
  • It earns its operating cost when several models share features or real-time features are needed; a single batch model may not need one.

Model registry

A catalogue of model versions that includes: artefact location, link to the training run, data version, metrics, evaluation report, model card, owner, stage (none → staging → production → archived), and approval history. Deployments point to registry versions, not to local files, and rollback = promoting the previous version.

Model cards

They document the intended and out-of-scope uses, a summary of the training data, evaluation results overall and by slice, fairness analysis, limitations, ethical considerations, and the owner and contact route. They are a practical implementation of the principles of Transparency & Explainability and Accountability & Responsibility, and the first thing an auditor asks for.

Approval paths scale with risk

Low-risk models may be promoted automatically after passing the gates; high-risk ones (credit, health, hiring, public services) require human sign-off from named roles, recorded in the registry — which matches the pre- and post-deployment conformity assessments required for high-risk systems in the AI Ethics Principles issued by SDAIA.

1.6 Model Risk Management

In Plain Terms

Banks treat models as a source of risk that must be governed throughout its life, and this applies to all AI. Six elements: the inventory (a list of every model in the organisation, because you cannot govern what you do not know), tiering (stricter controls for the riskier models), independent validation (a team other than the developers reviews high-risk models, and has the right to block deployment), monitoring and periodic review, use limits (a model built for one purpose is not used for another without new validation), and deliberate retirement.

Tiering depends on two things: impact (on people's money, health and rights) and autonomy (does the model support a human's decision, or decide alone?). The table gives four categories, the last of which is unacceptable outright, such as social scoring of individuals.

Real-World Example

Use limits: a bank built a model to assess the risk of "personal financing for employees" and validated it. Then another department decided to use it to assess "small-business financing" because it "works well". But businesses have entirely different data and behaviour, and the model was never tested on them. This is a recurring root cause in failure scenarios.

Classifying models into risk categories, where each category determines the depth of validation, the approval level and the monitoring frequency.

What the Exam Tests

Banks and regulated sectors have long treated models as a source of risk to be governed throughout their life. The same discipline applies to AI systems and maps directly onto the AI Ethics Principles and the National AI Risk Management Framework.

  • Model inventory: every model — including vendor models and applications built on language models — is registered with its purpose, owner, users, data used, risk level (the Ethics Principles levels and the Risk Framework score), dependencies, version, validation status and review date. You cannot govern what you cannot list.
  • Tiering: controls scale with risk. A classification based on impact (on individuals' rights, finances, health and safety, and on the organisation) and autonomy (decision support versus automated decision) determines the depth of validation, the approval level, the monitoring frequency and the review cycle.
  • Independent validation: for the higher tiers, a team independent of the developers reviews conceptual soundness, data quality, performance, stability, fairness, explainability, implementation correctness and limitations — and has the power to block deployment, and its findings are tracked to closure.
  • Ongoing monitoring and periodic review: performance, drift, fairness and usage against limits, with scheduled re-validation (annually for the high tier, for example) and event-driven reviews when the data, the use or the regulation changes.
  • Use limits: each model's card states the intended and prohibited uses. Using a model outside its validated scope (such as applying a model built for one product to another product) requires new validation — and it is a recurring root cause in failure scenarios.
  • Retirement: models are decommissioned deliberately: traffic moved, assets archived according to retention rules, documentation kept for audit, and dependent systems notified.

Chapter 2

Deployment, Monitoring, Drift and Retraining Strategies

2.1 Deployment Strategies — the Most Heavily Tested Table

In Plain Terms

The most important table in the unit. How do you launch a new model? Seven ways, each with its own risks and use. Shadow: the new model sees the real requests and predicts, but its results are shown to no one, only logged. No risk to anyone. Canary: a small percentage of users see the new one (1%, then 5%, then 25%, then 100%). Blue–green: two full environments, and the switch is all at once with the possibility of an instant return. A/B: a random split and a statistical comparison to find out which is better for the business. Multi-armed bandit: gradually shifting requests towards the winning variant during the experiment. Rolling: replacing replicas one after another. Big bang: the riskiest, and rarely the correct answer.

The skill required: reading the scenario and choosing the method. The cues are written at the end of the section, so memorise them.

Real-World Example

A restaurant wants to change the recipe of its most famous dish. Shadow: the cook prepares the new recipe next to the old one and only the staff taste it, while customers eat the old one. Canary: serve the new one to 1 table out of 20, then 5 tables, then everyone, and watch the reactions. A/B: half the customers, chosen randomly, get the new one, and we compare satisfaction statistically. Blue–green: two kitchens ready, and at exactly 12 o'clock everyone switches to the new one; if a problem appears, we return immediately to the old one.

Key Terms
Shadow / dark launchالنشر الظلي
Running the new model on a copy of the real requests and logging its results without showing them. It tests predictions and speed with no risk, but it does not measure its effect on user behaviour.
Canary releaseالإطلاق الكناري
Routing a small, growing percentage of users to the new version with monitoring and automatic rollback when the stop criteria are exceeded.
Blue–green deploymentالأزرق-الأخضر
Two identical full environments, with the switch between them all at once, and an instant return. It needs double the capacity for a period.
Multi-armed banditماكينة القمار متعددة الأذرع
An experimentation method that gradually moves requests towards the better variant during the experiment itself, which reduces loss.
Regretالندم
The loss caused by showing the worse variant during the experiment, compared with showing the better one from the start.
Rolling updateالتحديث المتدحرج
Replacing the old replicas with the new ones one after another, so the versions run together for a short time.
Statelessعديم الحالة
A service that does not keep information between one request and the next, so any replica of it can be replaced by another without a problem.

How to read the scenario

  • "We want to validate on real traffic with no effect on users" → shadow. But it does not measure the model's effect on user behaviour (conversion, for example) because its outputs are not shown.
  • "We want to reduce harm while measuring the real impact" → canary with stop criteria defined in advance. A canary is a safety mechanism, not a controlled experiment.
  • "A switch at a specific moment (a regulatory effective date) with a return within seconds and a budget that tolerates two environments" → blue–green.
  • "Which of the two models raises the business metric, with a defensible answer" → A/B with random split and statistical significance.
  • "Many variants and we want to minimise loss during the experiment" → multi-armed bandit.
Replacing everything at once is the highest-risk option; if it appears among the options it is usually the distractor.

2.2 Four-Layer Monitoring

In Plain Terms

Monitoring has four layers, and each layer exposes problems the others do not see. Infrastructure: is the server working? Is it fast? Does it have errors? Data: is the incoming data sound? Has its distribution changed? Model: are its predictions still correct? Business: has it achieved the goal? Have complaints risen?

The most important lesson: a model can be completely healthy at the infrastructure level (fast, error-free) and silently wrong in its predictions. A green infrastructure dashboard does not mean the model is correct. Note four points: the average hides slices, confidence is not performance, silent default values fool the dashboards, and saturation is an early warning.

Real-World Example

A car: infrastructure: the engine runs and the tyres are inflated. Data: the fuel is the right type. Model: the navigation system directs you to the right road. 2.3 Drift Detection — the Statistics

In Plain Terms

How do we measure "how much has the data changed"? This table gives different statistical tools for different data types. The best known is PSI: we divide the feature's values into bins, and compare each bin's proportion in the training data with its proportion in the live data. The reading: below 0.1 stable, from 0.1 to 0.25 a moderate change, and above 0.25 a major change.

interpretation discipline is the most important lesson: drift is a signal to investigate, not an automatic order to retrain. Ask: is it a data fault? A real change? Seasonality? Did it actually affect performance? A statistical trap: the KS test with millions of samples finds a "significant difference" even for trivial changes, so look at the size of the change, not at the p-value alone.

Real-World Example

PSI step by step for a feature split into two bins. In training: 50% and 50%. In reality now: 40% and 60%. First bin: (0.40 − 0.50) × ln(0.40 ÷ 0.50) = (−0.10) × (−0.223) = 0.022. Second bin: (0.60 − 0.50) × ln(0.60 ÷ 0.50) = 0.10 × 0.182 = 0.018. The total ≈ 0.04, which is less than 0.1, so stable. Note that every term is always positive, because the difference and the logarithm have the same sign.

A change in the distribution of the model's own outputs, such as the share of "fraud" rising from 1% to 3%. An early warning when labels are delayed.

Drift is a signal to investigate, not an automatic retraining trigger. Ask: is it a pipeline bug, a real change in the population, or seasonality? Does it affect important features? 2.4 Retraining Strategy

In Plain Terms

When do we retrain? Four types of triggers. Scheduled (every week or month): simple, but it may retrain unnecessarily or late. Performance-based: when actual performance drops, and this needs real answers quickly. Drift-based: when the real answers are delayed, but first confirm that the drift actually caused harm. Event-based: a new product, a new regulation, or a season.

The strict rule: every retrained model goes through the same checks and the same gradual rollout. Promoting a retrained model straight to 100% of users without checks is a classic wrong answer, even if its accuracy on paper is higher.

Real-World Example

Renewing a driving licence: it is not renewed automatically, but must pass the same medical examination every time. Likewise the updated model: "we retrained it on newer data" does not mean it is better. The new data may contain an error, or it may be worse with a particular group. This is known only by testing.

2.5 Feedback Loops and the Risks of Continuous Learning

3.1 Service Indicators, Objectives, Agreements and the Error Budget

In Plain Terms

Three similar terms. SLI: the thing we measure, such as "the percentage of requests that finish in under 300 ms". SLO: the internal target, such as "99% of requests under 300 ms over 30 days". SLA: the contract with the customer, with penalties if it is not met. The internal target (SLO) is set tighter than the contract (SLA) to leave a safety margin.

The error budget = the failure that is permitted. With 99.9% availability, 0.1% downtime is allowed. An important idea: components in series multiply. If your system needs three components all working, its availability = the product of their availabilities, which is lower than the weakest of them.

Real-World Example

First calculation: a 30-day month = 30 × 24 × 60 = 43,200 minutes. A 99.9% budget = 0.001 × 43,200 = 43.2 minutes of permitted downtime a month only. Second calculation: your system depends on a front end (99.9%), a vector database (99.9%) and a model provider (99.5%). Total availability = 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.995 ≈ 0.993, that is only 99.3%. Like a chain of three links: if any link breaks, the chain falls.

A measured quantity that describes service quality, such as the percentage of successful or fast requests.

An internal target for an indicator over a specified period, such as "99.9% availability over 30 days".

The permitted amount of failure = 1 − the target. When it is exhausted, risky releases are frozen and the focus goes to reliability.
Rolling windowالنافذة المتحركة
A measurement period that moves with time, such as "the last 30 days" calculated every day, not "the calendar month".
Hard dependencyالاعتمادية الصلبة
A component whose failure stops your whole service, with no alternative.

3.2 Health Checks, Graceful Operation and Disaster Recovery

In Plain Terms

Checks: the system periodically asks each replica about its health. Liveness probe: "Are you still working?" and if it does not answer, it is restarted. Readiness probe: "Are you ready to receive requests?" Large models need minutes to load, so requests are not sent to them before they are ready. Startup probe: "Give it time to start" so that it is not killed while loading.

disaster recovery is measured with two numbers. RPO: how much data can we lose? (for example the last 15 minutes). RTO: how long until the service is back? (for example one hour). Four strategies, from the cheapest and slowest to the most expensive and fastest. An important warning: replication is not a backup, because it copies deletion and corruption immediately too.

Real-World Example

Replication versus backup: you have an important file on two computers synchronised instantly (replication). If one burns, the other is intact, excellent. But if you delete the file by mistake, it is deleted from both immediately. Yesterday's backup, on the other hand, keeps it. The four strategies are like contingency plans for a restaurant that burns down: (1) we have the recipes in a safe and rebuild from scratch (hours and days). (2) Pilot light: a standby kitchen equipped but switched off. (3) Warm standby: a small kitchen that is always running. (4) Active–active: two branches that always run at full capacity.

Finishing in-flight requests before stopping a replica, instead of cutting them off abruptly.

The maximum acceptable data loss measured in time. RPO = 15 minutes means we accept losing the last 15 minutes of data at most.
Recovery Time Objective — RTOهدف زمن الاستعادة
The maximum acceptable time for the service to come back after the disaster.

Backup: a copy at a point in time that protects against deletion and corruption. Replication: continuous copying that protects against losing a machine or a zone, but it copies mistakes too.

Two or more sites running together and serving requests at the same time, so that if one falls the other carries on immediately.

3.3 Alert Design and On-Call Practice

In Plain Terms

An alert must fire on what the user feels (the service is slow, errors are high, the budget is being consumed), not on every internal detail. Every alert has an owner and written steps. Too many noisy alerts cause alert fatigue: the team ignores them, and so misses the real alert.

A smart method: burn-rate alerts. If the error budget is being consumed very fast (it will run out within hours or days) → wake the on-call person immediately. If it is being consumed slowly → open a ticket to work on later. After every incident: a blameless review, meaning we look for the system's causes, not for a person to blame.

Real-World Example

The calculation: the target is 99.9%, so 0.1% errors are permitted. Now the errors are 1%. Burn rate = 1% ÷ 0.1% = 10 times. A 30-day budget will run out in 30 ÷ 10 = 3 days, and this is urgent, so wake the on-call person. If the errors were 0.15%: the rate is 1.5 times, and the budget runs out in 20 days, and this is not urgent, so a ticket is enough. Like a car's fuel gauge: if it is dropping at an abnormal speed, stop now, and if it is dropping a little faster than usual, check the car this week.

Key Terms

So many unimportant alerts that the team starts ignoring all of them, including the real ones.
Burn rateمعدل الاستهلاك
The speed of consuming the error budget compared with the permitted rate. 10 times means the monthly budget will run out in a tenth of the month.
Page / ticketالاستدعاء الفوري والتذكرة
Page: an urgent alert that wakes the on-call person immediately. Ticket: a task that is logged to be worked on during working hours.
Flappingالتذبذب
An alert that fires, stops and returns repeatedly and quickly, so it annoys without benefit.
Blameless post-mortemمراجعة ما بعد الحادثة بلا لوم
An analysis of the incident that focuses on the system's causes, processes and how to prevent a recurrence, not on punishing a person.
Toilالعمل الروتيني
Repetitive manual work with no lasting value that grows with load, such as restarting failed jobs by hand. It should be automated.

What the Exam Tests

Alerts must fire on symptoms that users feel (SLO budget consumption, error spikes, latency breaches, drops in business metrics) and not on every internal cause, and every alert has an owner and an entry in the runbook. Too many noisy alerts cause alert fatigue, so real incidents are ignored.

  • Burn-rate alerts: an immediate page when the error budget is consumed quickly (a rate that would exhaust a month's budget in hours), and a ticket when consumption is slow.
  • Multi-window checks: require the condition to hold in both a short window and a longer one, to avoid flapping.
  • Severity levels: page only for urgent problems that affect users; send the rest to dashboards or tickets.
  • Runbooks: for every alert: what it means, the first checks, the mitigation (rollback, fallback, scale-out), and the escalation path.
  • Blameless post-mortems: the timeline, the root causes, the contributing factors, what worked, and actions that add tests, monitoring or safeguards.
  • Toil reduction: automate repetitive manual operations that grow with load (restarting failed jobs, manual upgrades).

3.4 Incident Response in ML Systems

In Plain Terms

When an incident occurs in a running model, the steps are six, in order: detect → mitigate first (stop the harm with a rollback or a fallback) → diagnose (look for the cause) → fix and verify through the normal pipeline and checks, not with a quick fix that bypasses them → review blamelessly → and if personal data leaked, follow the procedures of the Personal Data Protection Law.

The golden rule: mitigate before diagnosing. Do not look for the cause while the harm continues.

Real-World Example

Water leaking in the kitchen. The first step is not to look for the broken pipe, but to close the main valve to stop the damage. Then you look for the source calmly, then fix it properly, then ask: why did it break? How do we prevent that? In the model: complaints about rejected applications rose after the new release → bring back the old release immediately, then look for the cause.

Hotfixالإصلاح الساخن

  3. Diagnose: traces, data checks, and version comparison.
  4. Fix & verify: through the normal pipeline and gates — no hotfix that bypasses them.
  5. Blameless post-mortem: the root cause, and add a test or monitor so it cannot recur silently.
  6. If personal data leaked → breach-handling procedures under the Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL).

3.7 Operating GPU Fleets

In Plain Terms

GPUs are very expensive, and running a large group of them (a fleet) needs discipline. Seven aspects: health (monitoring faults and temperature, and isolating a faulty unit automatically), utilisation (measure the GPU's own utilisation, not the ordinary processor's), scheduling (a separate pool for training and another for serving), capacity, drivers, cost visibility (an alert for idle units), and security.

The rule to memorise: training tolerates interruption if it saves its progress periodically, so a cheap type of machine that may be reclaimed (Spot) suits it. But latency-sensitive serving does not tolerate interruption, so it needs reserved or guaranteed machines.

4.1 LLMOps — What Is Different

In Plain Terms

LLMOps is MLOps for language models, and it has important differences. In traditional models behaviour changes only when the weights change. In language models it changes when the prompt, the model version (which the provider may change without your knowledge), the RAG index, the tools and the settings change. That is why all of these are pinned together and logged with every answer.

The golden rules are six: pin the provider's model version, evaluate after every change to the prompt, log the versions with every answer, collect user ratings and add the failures to the test set, set token budgets, and put guardrails on inputs and outputs.

Real-World Example

An employee edited a single word in the system prompt: "Answer briefly" instead of "Answer clearly". The change looked innocent. But the answers started dropping important conditions from the policies. Nobody noticed for two weeks, because there is no automated evaluation that runs after every change to the prompt. The fix: the prompt in a versioned registry, and the test set running with every change.

4.2 The LLM Application Lifecycle and the LLM Judge

In Plain Terms

The LLM application lifecycle has nine steps: define the scope and risks → choose the model on your own data → design the prompt and context → build the evaluation harness → guardrails → staged launch (internal, then shadow, then canary, then everyone) → operation → continuous improvement → model upgrade and retirement. Note step 8: fine-tuning only when prompting and RAG reach their limit.

The LLM judge (a model that evaluates a model) is useful but biased: it prefers the longer answer, the one shown first, the answers of its own family, and a confident tone. The remedy is seven practices, the most important being: written evaluation criteria, asking for the justification before the score, swapping the order of the answers, and verifying its agreement with humans before relying on it.

Real-World Example

Internal use first (dogfooding): a company launches a customer assistant. Before that its employees use it for two weeks. The employees know the correct answers, so they discover the mistakes quickly. swapping the order with the judge: we ask it "which is better: A or B?", then ask "which is better: B or A?". If its opinion changes with the order alone, it is biased towards position.

What the Exam Tests

  1. Scope and risk: the use case, the users, data classification, risk level, success metrics, and the design of human oversight.
  2. Model selection: compare the candidate models on your own evaluation set (quality, performance in Arabic and its dialects, latency, cost, context length, licence, hosting location).
  3. Prompt/context design: the system prompt, retrieval, tools and the output schema; versioned in the prompt registry.
  4. Evaluation harness: a golden set (hundreds of real cases), an adversarial set (injection, jailbreaks, extraction of personal data), automated metrics + LLM judges calibrated against human labels, and human review of high-impact outputs.
  5. Guardrails: classification and filtering of inputs, validation of outputs (schema, personal data, toxicity, groundedness), and refusal and escalation paths.
  6. Staged launch: internal use (dogfooding) → shadow → canary → general availability, with stop criteria.
  7. Operation: tracing, cost and token dashboards, capturing user ratings, and incident handling.
  8. Continuous improvement: turn failures and negative ratings into the evaluation set; tune prompts and retrieval; and consider fine-tuning only when prompting and RAG reach their limit.
  9. Model upgrade and retirement: providers retire models; plan the migration with the evaluation harness as the acceptance test, and pin versions in the meantime.

The LLM Judge Done Right (LLM-as-judge)

Using a strong model to grade outputs scales up evaluation, but judges have biases: a preference for longer answers, for the option shown first (position bias), for their own model family, and for a confident tone.

  • Explicit evaluation criteria (rubrics) with clear criteria and scales.
  • Ask for the justification before the score.
  • Swap the order of the answers and take the average.
  • Pairwise comparison instead of absolute scores where possible.
  • Verify agreement with human raters on a labelled sample (for example Cohen's kappa) before relying on it.
  • Re-validate when the judge model changes.
  • Keep humans in the loop for safety-critical judgements and for the nuances of Arabic.

4.3 Guardrails Architecture

In Plain Terms

Guardrails are checks at every stage of the request's journey. On entry: authentication, limits, detecting personal data (and redacting it or routing to an in-Kingdom model), and detecting injection attempts. At retrieval: permissions. At tool use: parameter validation, an allow-list of actions, and human approval. On exit: format validation, scanning for personal data and secrets, toxicity, and grounding in sources. At logging: redaction and limited retention.

The repeated rule: permissions are enforced at the retrieval stage before the model sees the text. An instruction in the prompt ("do not reveal confidential information") is not an access control.

Real-World Example

An airport. On entry: checking the passport and the ticket. At security: checking the bags. Before the gate: verifying that your ticket is for this particular flight. At boarding: matching your name. Each point detects something the others do not. It is not enough to tell the traveller "please do not carry prohibited items"; you must actually inspect.

4.4 Managing the Cost of LLM Workloads

In Plain Terms

The cost of language models = tokens per request × number of requests × price per token. The levers for reducing it are ordered from the safest to the least: (1) remove token waste (shorter prompts, fewer chunks), (2) caching, (3) routing (simple requests to a small model), (4) batching for non-urgent work, (5) self-hosting for high, steady volume, and (6) quantised or distilled models.

An important cue in the exam: a sudden jump in cost while the number of requests is steady = usually a bug (an endless retry, or an agent stuck in a loop) or abuse, not natural growth.

Real-World Example

The month's bill rose from 10,000 to 35,000 SAR, and the number of users did not change. If it were natural growth, users would have increased. On investigation: an agent searching for information that does not exist, repeating the search 40 times per question instead of once. The fix is a maximum number of steps, not "increase the budget". If a jump of the same proportion in requests came from one unknown key, it is usually abuse.

Key Terms
Batchingالتجميع
Gathering non-urgent requests and processing them together, often at a cheaper price from providers.
Self-hostingالاستضافة الذاتية
Running the model on your own machines instead of paying a provider per request. It saves money at high, steady volume.

What the Exam Tests

Track cost per request, per user, per feature and per team from the gateway. The usual levers, in order of safety:

  1. Remove token waste: trim prompts, summarise history, fewer and better chunks, limits on output length.
  2. Caching: exact, semantic, prefix.
  3. Routing: simple requests to smaller models with escalation.
  4. Batching: for non-interactive work (often at discounted batch prices).
  5. Self-hosting: for high, steady volumes.
  6. Quantised or distilled models.

cost ≈ tokens per request × request volume × price per token

Set budgets and alerts for each team. A sudden jump in cost while traffic is steady is usually a bug (a retry loop, an agent stuck in a tool loop) or abuse — not natural growth.

4.6 Third-Party and Vendor Model Risk

In Plain Terms

Buying a model or an AI service transfers work, not accountability. If a model you bought makes a mistake and people are harmed, you are the one responsible to them and to the regulators. That is why due diligence is needed before purchase and during use.

Seven axes of due diligence: documentation (where their data comes from, and what their limitations are), data handling (where is your data processed? Do they train on it? Does it leave the Kingdom?), security, performance on your own data before signing, change management (will they tell you before changing the model?), the exit plan, and the allocation of responsibility in the contract.

Real-World Example

A hospital buys a system from a company to read X-ray images. The company says: "Our accuracy is 97%". But on whose images? Perhaps on different X-ray machines and different patients. The hospital tries it on 500 images from its own archive before signing, and finds the accuracy is 89%. It writes into the contract: "a minimum of 92% on our data, notice 60 days before any update, and export of our data on termination of the contract".

Systematic examination and verification of the vendor and its product before contracting and during the contract.

A third party the vendor uses to process your data, such as a cloud provider it uses.

What the Exam Tests

Buying a model or AI service transfers work, not accountability. Due diligence before and during use:

  • Documentation before procurement (Transparency principle): data sources, evaluation results by segment, limitations, intended use, and update policy.
  • Data handling: where prompts, outputs and files are processed and stored; retention; whether your data trains their models; sub-processors; residency and transfer basis.
  • Security: certifications, penetration-test results, incident-notification commitments, access controls, and isolation between customers.
  • Performance on your data: run your evaluation set before signing, and define acceptance criteria in the contract.
  • Change management: notice periods for model changes and retirements; version pinning; rollback options.
  • Exit plan: data export, portability of prompts and indexes, and alternative providers behind your gateway.
  • Contractual allocation of responsibility for errors and harms, remembering that under the Risk Framework transferring a risk does not remove the entity's responsibility.
The vendor's published metrics are not evidence of its performance in your environment; the evidence is your own evaluation set, before signing.

Applied Scenario

Apply the whole method of the unit to a single production incident: from choosing the rollout plan to monitoring, recovery and governance.

The Scenario

A financing company in Riyadh runs a model that scores personal-financing applications (a high-risk category) and a language assistant that answers customers' questions about financing policies through RAG. The data team manually retrained the scoring model on the last quarter's data and promoted it straight to 100% of traffic on Thursday evening because its offline accuracy was higher.

Two days later complaints rose, and the business dashboard showed that the rejection rate for the segment of newly hired private-sector employees had doubled, while the infrastructure dashboard was green: p99 = 220 ms and an error rate of 0.02%. The PSI for the "employment duration" feature between the training data and live traffic reached 0.31. In the same week the assistant's vector store was delayed for hours, so the assistant answered policy questions without sources, and the LLM provider changed the "latest" alias to a new model without notice.

The assistant service consists of a front end with 99.9% availability, a vector database at 99.9%, and an LLM provider at 99.5%, and management asks for an end-to-end availability SLO of 99.9%.

Solution Steps

  1. Mitigate first: promote the previous version from the model registry (rollback = promoting the previous version) before any diagnosis, and enable the "I can't access the documents right now" behaviour for the assistant instead of answering without sources.
  2. Diagnose with four layers: the infrastructure is healthy, but the data layer (PSI = 0.31 > 0.25, a major shift) and the business layer (complaints and rejections in one segment) reveal the fault; check whether the shift is a pipeline bug or a real change in the population, and measure performance and fairness on the segment.
  3. Name the missing processes: no automated evaluation gates (slices and fairness), no staged rollout, no human sign-off for a high-risk model, and no stop criteria defined in advance.
  4. Redesign: any retrained model goes through the same gates, then shadow (because the domain is high-risk), then a 1–5–25–100% canary with stop criteria that include the rejection rate by segment, together with independent validation and an approval record.
  5. Calculate availability: 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.995 ≈ 0.993, that is ≈ 99.3%, which does not meet the 99.9% target; raise it with replicas across availability zones, a secondary model from another provider behind the gateway, and cached answers to common questions. A 99.9% budget ≈ 43.2 minutes a month.
  6. Pin the provider's model version instead of "latest", make the evaluation harness the acceptance test for any migration, and add a notice-of-change clause and a notice period to the contract.
  7. Close the loop with a blameless review: add fairness monitoring next to accuracy, a drift alert on the key features that leads to an investigation and not to automatic retraining, a game-day exercise for the fallbacks, and update the model card, the risk assessment and the incident log.

Exam Traps

  • Promoting a retrained model to 100% of traffic because its offline metric is higher, with no gates or staged rollout.
  • Treating a green infrastructure dashboard as evidence that the model is sound; a model can be fast, error-free and silently wrong.
  • Treating drift (or a small p-value in KS on a huge sample) as an automatic retraining trigger instead of a signal to investigate.
  • Starting with diagnosis and the search for the root cause before rolling back or enabling the fallback.
  • Assuming that the availability of a chain equals that of the weakest component, or their average, instead of multiplying the availabilities.
  • Believing that contracting a vendor transfers accountability, or relying on the "latest" alias without pinning the version.
What You Must Master

A one-page written plan that specifies: the immediate mitigation step, the layer that exposed the fault, the corrected rollout strategy with its stop criteria, the availability calculation and the error budget, the fallback chain, and the documents that must be updated.

  1. 1A team trains its models in notebooks, hands them over manually to engineers and monitors nothing. What is the correct next step?

    Answer

  5. 5How many minutes of downtime a month does an SLO of 99.9% availability allow?
    Answer

    The error budget = 1 − 0.999 = 0.1% of 43,200 minutes (30 days) ≈ 43.2 minutes. When it is exhausted, risky releases are frozen and effort goes to reliability.

  6. 6A front end at 99.9%, a vector database at 99.9% and a model provider at 99.5% in series. What is the end-to-end availability?
    Answer

    Components in series multiply: 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.995 ≈ 0.993, about 99.3% — lower than the weakest component. Raise it with redundancy across zones, fallbacks and removing hard dependencies.

  7. 7Why does replication not replace backup?
    Answer

    Because it copies corruption and deletion immediately too; it protects against losing a node or zone and gives a near-zero RPO, whereas a point-in-time backup is what protects against corruption and deletion.

    Answer

  9. 9What are the main biases of an LLM judge and how do you mitigate them?
    Answer

    A preference for the longer answer, the one shown first, its own model family and a confident tone.   10. 10An entity bought a model from a vendor and harm occurred to beneficiaries. Who is accountable?

Blue–green's whole value is instant switch-back, and tearing down the old environment after two hours threw that away; some defects only appear with specific traffic hours later. Keeping it warm through an agreed bake period would have turned three hours into seconds. More tests are good but cannot guarantee every defect is caught; rolling updates are slower to reverse; a night cut-over does not change rollback time.

  5. 5Slice metrics hide in the averageHard

    A lender's loan-default model keeps an overall recall of 0.88 on newly matured labels, comfortably above its 0.85 alert threshold. Six months ago the lender added a network of rural branches, now 9% of applications. Complaints from that region are rising, and an ad-hoc analysis shows recall of 0.71 for rural applicants. The model-risk team wants monitoring that would have surfaced this early and will keep surfacing similar problems. What should be added?

    Explanation

    A 9% segment can fall sharply while the overall metric barely moves: if the other 91% sit near 0.90, overall recall is roughly 0.91 × 0.90 + 0.09 × 0.71 ≈ 0.88, still above 0.85. The model layer therefore includes slice metrics and fairness, monitored per region and segment against their own thresholds. A tighter overall threshold is still an average; PSI would show the population mix changed but not that recall fell for the slice; service metrics measure speed, not decisions. Watching slices also supports fairness obligations for credit.

  6. 6Retrained models still pass gatesHard

    A telecom's network-anomaly pipeline retrains nightly whenever drift is detected and automatically replaces the production model at 100% of traffic, so that it 'always has the freshest model'. One night a label-join bug produced a model whose recall was 20 points lower; it served all traffic for a day before engineers noticed. The team wants to keep automated retraining. What should change?

    Explanation

    Auto-promoting a retrained model straight to 100% without gates is a classic wrong answer. Every retrained model must pass the same automated evaluation gates — golden set, critical slices, fairness, latency — and a staged rollout, so the 20-point recall drop would have been blocked before serving anyone. Weekly retraining or going manual reduces automation without fixing the missing check; latency and error alerts cannot see a model that is fast but wrong.

  7. 7Pinned release bundles for LLM appsHard

    After complaints about wrong answers, a bank's investment-FAQ assistant was 'rolled back' by restoring last week's prompt, but the errors continued. It turns out that on the same day the retrieval index had been rebuilt with a new chunking strategy and the provider alias had moved to a newer model. Nobody could say which combination had been working last week. Which practice would have made a real rollback possible?

    Explanation

    In LLMOps the release unit is prompt version + model version + index version pinned together, because outputs depend on all three. With a pinned bundle, 'last week's release' is a single known combination, and rollback means re-deploying it — not guessing which piece changed. Index backups help restore one component but do not record which combination worked; extra approvals slow changes without making them reversible; fine-tuning does not remove the dependence on retrieval for changing knowledge.

  8. 8Mitigate before diagnosingHard

    At 02:00 an alert shows that a bank's card-fraud model, updated that afternoon, is declining about 4% of legitimate transactions instead of the usual 0.5%. The previous model version is still in the registry. The on-call engineer proposes keeping the new model live while investigating feature logs, estimating three to four hours to find the cause. What should happen first?

    Explanation

    The memo's incident sequence puts mitigation before diagnosis: roll back to the last good model, switch to a fallback, or disable the feature flag. Here a known-good version is in the registry, so rolling back stops customers being declined within minutes, and the faulty version can still be diagnosed offline from traces, data checks and version comparisons. Leaving it live for hours keeps harming customers. Changing the threshold in production is an untested change made outside the pipeline and gates, and it may let fraud through. An overnight retrain is an unvalidated fix rushed past the evaluation gates. Lesson: stop the harm first, then find the cause.

  9. 9Choosing an orchestrator by team and platformHard

    A three-person data-science team at a government agency already trains and serves its models on a managed cloud ML platform in an approved in-Kingdom region. None of them has Kubernetes experience. They now need scheduled, tracked retraining pipelines within a month. Which orchestration choice best fits?

    Explanation

    The memo says to choose an orchestrator by team skills and platform, and gives this exact signal: 'a small team already on a managed cloud ML platform' points to its native pipelines, not a self-managed Kubeflow cluster. Native pipelines give scheduled, tracked, containerised steps with no cluster to run. Self-managed Kubeflow is over-engineering for three people without Kubernetes skills. Cron and notebooks give a schedule but no step tracking, lineage or gates. An offshore service breaks the in-Kingdom placement the agency already uses. Lesson: the right tool is the one the team can operate.

  10. 10Point-in-time-correct joinsHard

    A lender built a training set by joining each historical loan application (2021–2024) with each customer's feature values as they stood on the day the data was extracted in 2025, including 'number of missed payments to date'. Offline AUC was 0.96; in production it fell to 0.74. What is the most likely cause and fix?

    Explanation

    Joining 2021–2024 applications to feature values as of 2025 lets each training row see information that did not exist at decision time — missed payments that happened after the loan was granted are an outcome, not a predictor. The memo's feature store prevents this with point-in-time-correct joins in the offline store, so training rows see only feature values that existed at that moment. The fall from 0.96 offline to 0.74 in production is the classic leakage signature. Drift and overfitting do not explain a gap created by the way the training set was built, and online-store speed has nothing to do with it. Lesson: an offline score that looks too good to be true usually is.

Unit Summary
