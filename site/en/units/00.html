Unit Introduction

This unit is written for readers who have never studied artificial intelligence. It assumes no technical background beyond using a computer and the internet. In it you will build the complete picture step by step: what we mean by artificial intelligence, how a model “learns” from data, how we tell whether it is good or bad, what language models such as AI assistants are, and what systems run them in companies and government bodies. Every term that appears here will appear later in the exam, but the exam assumes you already know it. Here you learn it from zero.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

  • Explain the difference between rule-based programming and learning from data, and between artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning and generative AI.
  • Distinguish data, features and labels, and explain why data is split into training, validation and test sets.
  • Describe how a model learns by trial and error, and what overfitting and underfitting are.
  • Calculate accuracy, precision and recall from a small example, and explain why accuracy can be misleading.
  • Explain tokens, the context window, the prompt, hallucination, vector embeddings and RAG in simple terms.
  • Interpret latency, throughput, replicas, caching and cost in a system running on a server.
  • Recognise personal and sensitive data, and the role of SDAIA and the Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL).

How to Study This Unit

Read this unit slowly, and do not memorise anything. The goal is to understand. After each section, close your eyes and explain the idea to yourself with an example from your own life. If you can, you have understood; if you cannot, re-read the “Example”. When you reach the calculations, pick up a pen and redo them by hand. You will come back to this unit often while reading the following units, so use it as a reference, and use the glossary as well.

Chapter 1

What Is Artificial Intelligence?

1.1 From Rule-Based Programming to Learning from Data

An ordinary program is a set of rules written by a programmer: “If the amount exceeds SAR 10,000, send an alert.” The computer executes the rule exactly as written and knows nothing beyond it.

This works when we know the rule. But for many tasks we cannot write the rules: how would you write a rule that tells a picture of a cat from a picture of a dog? Or that recognises a fraudulent email? Here we change the approach: we give the computer many examples together with their correct answers, and let an algorithm extract the pattern by itself. This is machine learning.

Traditional programmingMachine learning
What do we give the computer?Rules + dataData + correct answers (examples)
What is produced?AnswersExtracted rules (a model)
When does it fit?The rule is known and stableThe rule is complex, unknown or changing
ExampleCalculating 15% value-added taxDetecting a suspicious card transaction
Machine learning does not mean that the computer “thinks”. It means that it extracts a statistical pattern from past examples and applies it to new cases.

An important point for the exam: not every problem needs machine learning. If the rule is clear (an upper limit on an amount, or a regulatory condition), a written rule is cheaper, clearer and easier to audit. Many wrong exam options propose a complex model for a problem that a rule would solve.

1.2 Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning, Deep Learning and Generative AI

These are four terms that many people confuse. Picture them as circles nested inside one another:

  • Artificial intelligence (AI): the broadest term. Any system that performs a task we normally associate with human intelligence: speech recognition, translation, decision-making or planning. It may rely on written rules or on learning.
  • Machine learning (ML): a subset of artificial intelligence in which the system learns from data instead of from written rules.
  • Deep learning (DL): a subset of machine learning that uses neural networks with many layers. It excels at images, audio and text, and needs more data and more computing power.
  • Generative AI: systems that produce new content: text, images, audio or code. Most of them today are built on deep learning, such as large language models.

AI ⊃ ML ⊃ Deep Learning ⊃ (most) Generative AI

TypeWhat does it do?Example
Traditional machine learningPredicts a number or a category from a data tablePredicting whether a customer will be late repaying a loan
Deep learningUnderstands images, sounds and textReading the characters from a scanned ID image
Generative AIProduces new contentAn assistant that writes a reply to a customer enquiry

In the exam you will find that the simplest type that solves the problem is usually the correct one. A structured data table (columns and numbers) is normally well served by traditional machine learning and does not need a huge language model.

1.3 What Is a “Model”? Training and Inference

A model is the output we obtain after learning: a mathematical function that takes an input and produces an output. Give it a transaction's data and it gives you the probability that it is fraudulent. Give it a question and it gives you a text answer.

A model's life has two completely different phases, and they will recur in every unit:

TrainingInference
What happens?The model learns from the data and adjusts its internal numbersThe trained model is used to answer new requests
How often?Once or a few times (or periodically)With every request from every user, thousands or millions of times
CostVery high but temporarySmall per request, but continuous and cumulative
AnalogyThe student studiesThe student answers in the exam

This distinction matters greatly in the exam: many questions about cost and speed concern inference (the daily service), not training. A common mistake is an option that proposes “retraining” for a problem whose cause lies in inference, such as a slow server or an error in the input data.

Training creates the model. Inference uses it. Most of the monthly bill usually comes from inference.

1.4 Types of Learning: Supervised, Unsupervised and Reinforcement

Learning methods differ according to the type of data available:

TypeIdeaAnalogyExample
Supervised learningEvery example has a known correct answer (a label), and the model learns to map the input to the answerA student studying from a bank of solved questionsMessages labelled “fraud/legitimate” to train a fraud detector
Unsupervised learningThere are no answers; the model looks for clusters or patterns by itselfArranging a library of untitled books by similaritySegmenting customers into groups with similar behaviour
Reinforcement learningThe system tries actions and receives a reward or a penalty, so it learns what maximises the rewardTraining a pet with rewardsTuning a language model's behaviour using human ratings of its answers

Most exam questions about traditional models concern supervised learning, because it is the most widely used in business: we predict something whose answer we know in the historical data. Its hardest part is usually obtaining the correct labels, because they are assigned by humans, which is costly and may introduce errors.

1.5 Types of Tasks: Classification, Regression, Clustering and Generation

Before choosing any model, ask: what shape of answer is required? This alone determines the type of task:

TaskShape of the answerExamples
ClassificationOne of a set of defined categoriesFraud / legitimate; urgent / routine / complaint
RegressionA continuous numberProperty price; number of orders tomorrow
ClusteringGroups the model discovers by itselfCustomer segments
Ranking and recommendationAn ordered listThe top 10 products for this customer
GenerationNew contentA report summary; a reply to a customer

Classification with only two categories is called binary classification, such as fraud/legitimate. Classification with more than two categories is called multi-class classification.

Each type has different evaluation metrics. Do not measure a regression model by accuracy, or a classification model by mean error. You will learn the metrics in the third chapter of this unit.

Chapter 2

Data and How a Model Learns

2.1 Data: Rows, Columns, Features and Labels

The simplest form of data is a table, like an Excel sheet. Each row is one case (a customer, a transaction, a patient), and each column is one of its attributes.

Transaction IDAmount (SAR)HourCountryWas it fraud?
100112014:05Saudi ArabiaNo
10029,80003:12Another countryYes
10034509:30Saudi ArabiaNo
  • Features: the columns the model uses to make its prediction. Here: the amount, the hour and the country.
  • Label or target: the column we want to predict, here “Was it fraud?” In historical data we know its value; in new cases we do not, which is why we need the model.
  • Dataset: the whole table with all its rows.

Not all data is tabular. There is also unstructured data such as text, images, audio and scanned documents. Deep learning and language models usually handle these.

A model is only as good as its data. Incomplete, outdated or wrong data produces a bad model, however powerful the algorithm.

2.2 Why We Split the Data: Training, Validation and Test

Imagine a teacher who gives students a question bank and then sets the final exam from the same questions. Everyone will get high marks, but we will not know whether they understood or memorised. A model is like the student: if we test it on the data it learned from, it will look excellent, but that means nothing.

So we split the data into three parts:

PartCommon shareUseAnalogy
Training set≈ 70%The model learns from itThe bank of solved questions
Validation set≈ 15%We use it to compare settings and models and choose the bestThe mock exam
Test set≈ 15%We use it to measure final performance once, at the endThe sealed final exam

The shares above are a common example, not a fixed rule. The fixed rule is: the test set is seen neither by the model nor by the team during development. If we use it repeatedly to choose settings, it becomes like a leaked exam.

There is a more dangerous risk called data leakage: the training features contain information that will not be available at real prediction time. Example: a column “amount refunded” is known only after the fraud has been detected. The model will rely on it and look brilliant in testing, then fail in the real world. You will see it in detail in Unit 02.

2.3 Memorising versus Understanding: Overfitting and Underfitting

There are two ways in which a model fails to learn:

UnderfittingOverfitting
MeaningThe model is too simple to capture the patternThe model has memorised the training data with all its details and noise
AnalogyA student who did not study: gets the question bank wrong and the exam wrongA student who memorised the answers word for word: excellent on the question bank, poor on any new question
SymptomPoor performance on both training and testExcellent performance on training, poor on test
Common remedyA more powerful model, better features, or longer trainingMore data, a simpler model, regularisation, or early stopping

The sign that reveals overfitting is a large gap between training performance and validation performance. For example, 99% on training and 75% on validation.

Specialists call this idea the bias–variance trade-off. High bias means underfitting, and high variance means overfitting. Bias here means the error caused by oversimplifying the model, which is a different meaning from bias against a group of people.

2.4 How a Model Learns: Parameters, Loss and Adjustment

Inside every model are adjustable numbers called parameters or weights. All of learning is the search for values of these numbers that bring the predictions close to the correct answers.

  1. Guess: the model predicts with its current weights (initially almost random).
  2. Measure the error: we calculate the loss function, a number that measures how far the prediction is from the correct answer.
  3. Adjust: we change the weights slightly in the direction that reduces the loss. The common method is called gradient descent.
  4. Repeat: we repeat the steps thousands of times over the training examples until the loss stops improving.

An analogy: imagine you are in a valley covered in fog and want to reach the lowest point. You cannot see the bottom, but you can feel the slope of the ground under your feet, so you take a small step downhill, then repeat. The step size is called the learning rate. If it is too large you leap over the bottom; if it is too small it takes a very long time.

The number of parameters determines the size of the model. A small traditional model may contain hundreds of numbers. A large language model contains billions of parameters, and its size is written like this: 7B means 7 billion parameters, and 70B means 70 billion. Each parameter needs a place in memory, which is why large models need special hardware.

Weights memory ≈ number of parameters × bytes per parameter → 7B × 2 bytes ≈ 14 GB

2.5 Neural Networks by Intuition

A neural network is a model loosely inspired by the brain. It consists of small units called neurons arranged in layers:

  • Input layer: receives the data, such as image pixels or numbers representing words.
  • Hidden layers: each layer transforms what reaches it into a more abstract representation. In images, for example, the first layers pick up edges, then shapes, then parts such as the eye and the ear, then the whole object.
  • Output layer: gives the result, such as the probability that the image shows a cat.

Each neuron takes numbers, multiplies each by a weight, adds them up, then passes the result through a simple function called an activation function. The power comes not from a single neuron but from millions of neurons and weights that learn together.

The word “deep” in deep learning means having many layers. The best-known modern architecture is called the Transformer, and large language models are built on it. You will meet it in Unit 04.

A neural network does not understand the way a human does. It is a huge function whose weights were learned from examples.

2.6 Why We Need Graphics Processing Units (GPUs)

The central processing unit (CPU) in your computer is like a small number of experts: each one fast and clever, but there are few of them. A graphics processing unit (GPU), by contrast, is like thousands of simple workers carrying out simple operations at the same time.

Neural networks are built on an enormous number of multiplications and additions that can be executed in parallel, which is why GPUs outperform CPUs on them by a wide margin. These units were originally designed for game graphics and then became the main tool of artificial intelligence.

CPUGPU
ArchitectureA few powerful coresThousands of simple cores
Excels atVaried and sequential tasksRepetitive parallel operations
In artificial intelligenceSufficient for small traditional modelsEssential for training deep networks and running large language models
CostLowHigh, and usually billed by the hour in the cloud

Each GPU has a limited dedicated memory, such as 24, 48 or 80 GB. The model must fit in it. That is why you will meet calculations in the exam such as: does a model of 14 GB fit in 24 GB of memory? How much is left for concurrent users?

One of the exam's recurring rules: adding GPUs is not always the first solution. Sometimes the cheapest solution is a smaller model, caching, request batching, or shutting down idle machines.

Chapter 3

How We Judge a Model

3.1 The Confusion Matrix: Four Kinds of Answers

Take a fraud detector. Each transaction has two possibilities in reality (fraud or legitimate), and two possibilities in the model's prediction. The result is four cases, arranged in a table called the confusion matrix:

Model said: fraudModel said: legitimate
Reality: fraudTrue positive (TP): we caught the fraudsterFalse negative (FN): we missed the fraudster
Reality: legitimateFalse positive (FP): a false alarm that bothered an innocent customerTrue negative (TN): we let the legitimate transaction through

The word “positive” here does not mean “good”; it means the model said “yes, this is the case we are looking for.” In medicine it might mean “diseased”, and in fraud, “fraud”.

A numerical example we will use in the following sections

1,000 transactions, of which 10 are genuine fraud. The model flagged 20 transactions as fraud, 8 of them actually fraud.

Said: fraudSaid: legitimateTotal
Reality: fraudTP = 8FN = 210
Reality: legitimateFP = 12TN = 978990
Total209801,000

How to calculate: the model flagged 20, of which 8 were correct, so FP = 20 − 8 = 12. The genuine fraud is 10 and we caught 8, so FN = 10 − 8 = 2. The remainder is TN = 1,000 − 8 − 12 − 2 = 978.

3.2 Accuracy and Why It Misleads

Accuracy is the proportion of correct answers out of all answers:

Accuracy = (TP + TN) / Total = (8 + 978) / 1,000 = 98.6%

A figure that looks wonderful. But look at a “lazy” model that always says “legitimate” for every transaction without any thought:

Accuracy (lazy) = (0 + 990) / 1,000 = 99%

The lazy model has higher accuracy than our model, even though it did not catch a single fraudster. The reason is that the data is imbalanced: the cases we are looking for are rare (only 1%).

When the target case is rare (fraud, disease, failure), accuracy is a misleading metric. This is one of the most frequent distractors in the exam.

3.3 Precision, Recall and F1

Instead of accuracy we use two metrics, each answering a different question:

MetricThe question it answersCalculation in our example
PrecisionOf everything the model said was fraud, how much actually was fraud?TP / (TP + FP) = 8 / 20 = 40%
RecallOf all the genuine fraud, how much did the model catch?TP / (TP + FN) = 8 / 10 = 80%

Note the naming: Arabic uses two near-identical terms: الدقة الكلية (“overall accuracy”) for accuracy and الدقة (plain “accuracy”) for precision. In the exam the English term usually comes beside the Arabic, so rely on it.

Which of the two metrics matters more? It depends on the cost of the error:

  • If missing the case is very costly (a cancer patient, or major fraud), we care about recall, and accept some false alarms.
  • If a false alarm is costly (blocking an innocent customer's card, or an expensive manual review), we care about precision.

When we want a single number that balances the two, we use F1, which is the harmonic mean of the two metrics:

F1 = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall) = 2 × (0.4 × 0.8) / (0.4 + 0.8) = 0.64 / 1.2 ≈ 0.53

3.4 The Threshold: When Does the Model Say “Yes”?

Most models do not say “fraud” or “legitimate” directly. They give a probability between 0 and 1, such as 0.73. We choose the threshold: if the probability exceeds the threshold, we treat the transaction as fraud.

ThresholdWhat happens?PrecisionRecall
Low (e.g. 0.2)The model suspects many transactionsFalls (more false alarms)Rises (we catch more fraud)
High (e.g. 0.9)The model says fraud only when highly confidentRisesFalls (we miss more fraud)

An analogy: a fire alarm. If you make it very sensitive it will ring from cooking steam (false alarms), and if you make it insensitive it may not ring in a real fire.

The key point for the exam: changing the threshold does not require retraining the model. If the review team complains of too many alerts, the first solution is usually to raise the threshold, not to train a new model. The right way to choose the threshold is to calculate the expected cost of each error, which you will learn in Unit 01.

3.5 Essential Statistics: Mean, Median, Percentile and Probability

You will meet these concepts in almost every unit. Take the response times of 10 requests in milliseconds (ms, a thousandth of a second):

100, 110, 120, 120, 130, 140, 150, 160, 400, 900

ConceptDefinitionIn the example
MeanSum of the values ÷ their count2,330 ÷ 10 = 233 ms
MedianThe middle value after sorting(130 + 140) ÷ 2 = 135 ms
Percentile p90The value that 90% of the values are below or equal to≈ 400 ms
Percentile p95 / p99The same idea at 95% and 99%Measure the slowest requests

Note: the mean (233) does not represent most of the requests, because two very slow requests pushed it up. This is why speed requirements in real systems are written as percentiles: “p95 ≤ 300 ms” means that 95% of requests must finish within 300 milliseconds or less. You will see this form often in the exam.

  • Percentage (%): a part of 100. Note the difference between “increased by 10%” and “increased by 10 percentage points”: going from 50% to 60% is an increase of 10 points, but 20% in relative terms.
  • Probability: a number between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain). 0.05 means 5%.
  • Distribution: the shape in which values are spread: where they concentrate and how far apart they are. When the “distribution changes” between the training data and real-world data, we say there is drift; you will see it in Units 02 and 07.
Chapter 4

Language Models and Generative AI

4.1 What Is a Large Language Model (LLM)?

A large language model (LLM) is a huge neural network trained on vast quantities of text. Its task is, at heart, simple: to predict the next piece of text based on what came before.

When you type “The capital of Saudi Arabia is”, the model predicts that the most likely next word is “Riyadh”. When this prediction is repeated word after word, a complete answer is formed. From this simple skill, combined with enormous scale, capabilities emerge such as summarisation, translation, code writing and question answering.

What it can usually doWhat it does not guarantee by itself
Summarise and rephrase a textFactual accuracy
Answer general questionsKnowledge of events after its training date
Extract information from a documentKnowledge of your company's internal data
Write drafts and codeRespecting user permissions or regulations

Language models are built in stages: pre-training on huge general texts teaches them language and knowledge, then instruction tuning teaches them to follow requests, then alignment with human ratings makes them helpful and safe.

A language model produces text that is “linguistically convincing”, not necessarily “factually correct”. Fluency is not evidence of correctness.

4.2 Tokens and the Context Window

The model does not read letters or words the way we read them. It first splits the text into small pieces called tokens, then converts each token into a number. The tool that splits the text is called the tokenizer.

A token may be a whole word, part of a word, or a punctuation mark. A word such as the single word والمستشفيات (“and the hospitals”) may be split into several tokens. So a token does not equal one word, and the ratio differs between languages and models. Many tokenizers split Arabic text into more tokens than the equivalent English text, which means higher cost and longer latency.

Why does the number of tokens matter?

  • Cost: model providers usually charge by the number of tokens, both input (your question and the context) and output (the answer).
  • Speed: the model generates the answer token by token, so a longer answer takes longer.
  • The limit: every model has a context window, the maximum number of tokens it can handle in a single request, including the instructions, the conversation, the documents and the answer.

An analogy for the context window: an office desk. Everything you want the model to see must be placed on the desk, and the desk has limited space. Even if the desk is large, not everything on it is read with the same attention.

4.3 The Prompt and Temperature

The prompt is the text you give the model: your question, the instructions, and any examples or documents. How the prompt is worded strongly affects the quality of the answer, and the art of wording it is called prompt engineering.

In applications, messages are usually divided into roles:

  • System prompt: written by the developer, such as “You are a customer-service assistant for a telecom company; answer in Arabic and do not promise discounts.”
  • User message: the user's question.
  • Assistant response: what the model generates.

Temperature is a setting that controls the “randomness” of selection. At a low temperature (close to 0) the model picks the most probable words, so answers become more stable and repetitive. At a high temperature, answers are more varied and creative.

TaskUsually appropriate temperature
Extracting data from an invoice, classification, or answers that need consistencyLow
Brainstorming, or creative writingHigher

Exam warning: lowering the temperature does not make an answer correct. It only makes it more stable. The model may repeat the same mistake consistently.

4.4 Hallucination: Why the Model Errs with Confidence

Hallucination is when the model produces information that is incorrect or unsupported by any source and presents it in confident wording. It may invent an article number in a law, the name of a reference, or a statistic.

The reason is that the model is trained to produce text that is linguistically probable, not to verify facts. If it does not know the answer it will not automatically say “I don't know”; it will continue with whatever seems plausible.

RemedyHow it helps
Supplying it with the correct sources in the context (RAG)It answers from the documents, not from its memory
Requiring it to cite the sourceEvery claim can be verified
Allowing it to abstain (“I cannot find that in the documents”)Prevents it from inventing an answer
Human review of important decisionsA last line of defence

These remedies reduce hallucination but do not eliminate it entirely. Any exam option that says a technique “completely eliminates hallucination” is usually wrong.

4.5 Vector Embeddings and Semantic Search

How does a computer know that “car” and “vehicle” are close in meaning? We convert each text into a list of numbers called an embedding. A specialised model produces these numbers so that texts close in meaning are close in numbers.

An analogy: a map. Each text is a point on the map. Similar texts lie near one another: “How do I renew my passport?” is close to “Steps for renewing the passport”, and far from “Today's gold price”. The real map has hundreds of dimensions rather than two, but the idea is the same.

Lexical (keyword) searchSemantic search
Relies onLiteral word matchingCloseness of meaning (embeddings)
Excels atNumbers, codes and exact names, such as an invoice numberQuestions worded differently from the text
Weak atSynonyms and rephrasingExact identifiers and numbers

That is why many systems combine the two methods, which is called hybrid search. Embeddings are stored in a special type of database called a vector database, which can find the nearest points quickly.

4.6 Three Ways to Customise a Model: Prompting, RAG and Fine-Tuning

A general language model does not know your company's policies or documents. There are three main ways to make it useful in your work, and choosing among them is one of the exam's most important decisions:

MethodIdeaAnalogyWhen to choose it?
Prompt engineeringBetter instructions and examples in the requestClear directions to a smart employeeAlways first: the cheapest and fastest
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)We search our documents for the relevant passages and place them in the context with the questionAn “open-book” exam: the student looks up the reference, then answersThe knowledge changes, or you need citation of the source
Fine-tuningAdditional training of the model on examples from your task, so its weights changeA training course that changes the employee's styleA fixed style or format, or a narrow repetitive task at large volume

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation: retrieval, then generation. Its steps, simply: (1) we split the documents into passages and compute their embeddings, (2) when a question arrives we search for the nearest passages, (3) we give them to the model together with the question, (4) it answers from them and cites them.

A golden rule in the exam: if the documents change continuously, or the source must be cited, the answer is RAG, not fine-tuning. Fine-tuning does not make the model know what changed yesterday.

4.7 Agents

An ordinary language model only answers. An agent, by contrast, is a system that uses the language model to plan and take steps: it decides to search a database, then calls another system, then writes an email, and so on until it completes the task.

The agent connects to the world through tools: software functions that the developer allows it to call, such as “look up an order”, “create a ticket” and “send a notification”.

An analogy: the language model is a consultant who gives you an opinion. The agent is an employee to whom you have handed the keys to some systems. The more keys, the greater the risk, which is why we need controls:

  • Least privilege: the agent gets only the tools and data it actually needs.
  • Human approval before important actions or ones that cannot be undone, such as payment, deletion or sending outside the organisation.
  • An audit log of every step: what it did, when and why.
  • Limits: a maximum number of steps, a budget and a time limit.

One of the exam's rules: do not use an agent if the workflow is fixed and known. A predefined sequence of steps (a workflow) is cheaper, clearer and easier to test.

Chapter 5

The Systems That Run Artificial Intelligence

5.1 The Server, the Cloud and the Application Programming Interface (API)

In practice the model usually does not run on the user's device, but on a server: a powerful computer that runs continuously and receives requests from applications.

The cloud means renting servers from a provider instead of buying them: you pay for what you use, by the hour or by the request. The alternative is to own your machines in your own data centre, which is called on-premises hosting. In the Saudi context, the requirement that data be hosted inside the Kingdom, that is, in data centres located in it, recurs often.

How does an application talk to the model? Through an application programming interface (API). An analogy: the order window at a restaurant. You do not enter the kitchen; you submit a request in an agreed format, and a response comes back to you.

OptionIdeaAdvantageWatch out
External provider APIWe send the request to a model run by a provider, and pay per useQuick to start, no hardwareWhere does the data go? Does it leave the Kingdom?
Self-hostingWe run the model on our own servers or on rented serversFull control over the dataCost of hardware, operations and expertise

When you read in the exam “the application sends personal data to a model API abroad to save cost”, stop: this is technically a valid solution but it may violate regulations. You will learn why in Unit 10.

5.2 Latency, Throughput and Concurrency

Three speed metrics that many people confuse. Picture a fast-food restaurant:

MetricMeaningAt the restaurantUnit
LatencyThe time from sending the request until the response arrivesHow many minutes you waited for your mealMilliseconds (ms) or seconds
ThroughputThe number of requests completed per unit of timeHow many meals the restaurant hands out per hourRequests/second (QPS, RPS)
ConcurrencyThe number of requests in progress at the same momentHow many customers are waiting at the till right nowA count

QPS stands for Queries Per Second, that is, the number of requests per second. A simple relationship links the three metrics, called Little's Law:

Concurrency = arrival rate × average service time → 10 requests/second × 2 seconds = 20 requests in progress

Language models have two special metrics: time to first token (TTFT), that is, when the user starts to see the answer, and generation speed in tokens per second. The user feels the system is fast if the answer starts to appear quickly, even if it completes seconds later. This is why streaming is used, that is, displaying the answer while it is being generated.

Improving throughput may increase latency, and vice versa. Batching requests serves more of them, but each request waits a little. The exam asks you which of the two matters in the scenario.

5.3 Scaling, Replicas and Caching

When the number of users grows, the system must scale. There are two ways:

  • Scaling up (vertical): a more powerful machine, such as a larger GPU. It has a limit, and often requires taking the service down.
  • Scaling out (horizontal): more replicas of the same service, each on a machine, with a load balancer distributing requests among them. An analogy: opening extra tills instead of speeding up the single cashier.

Autoscaling: the system adds replicas at peak and removes them when quiet, so we do not pay for idle machines at night. Note the cold start: a new replica needs time to load the model, which can be minutes for large models, so it does not help if it starts late.

Cache: saving the result of a repeated question to return it immediately without running the model again. An analogy: the receptionist who memorises the answers to the most frequent questions instead of phoning the relevant department every time. It saves cost and time together, but beware: do not show a cached answer to a user who has no permission to see it, nor a stale answer after the information has changed.

SolutionHelps when…
More replicasThe load exceeds the capacity of the current machines
AutoscalingThe load varies through the day, such as a morning peak
CachingQuestions repeat often
A smaller modelThe required quality is achieved by a cheaper model

5.4 Deployment, Versioning and Monitoring

Deployment is putting the model into the production environment, that is, the real environment that people use. Before that, things are tried in the staging environment.

Models change, so we need versioning: each model has a version number, and we store with it the data it was trained on, its settings and its evaluation results. The aim is that we always know which version is running now, and can roll back to the previous version immediately if things go wrong.

A new model is usually not deployed to everyone at once. It is released gradually, for example to 5% of users first, while we monitor, and then expand. This is called a canary release, after the canary birds that miners used to take with them to warn them early of dangerous gases.

After deployment, monitoring begins. We monitor four things:

  • System health: is it running? What is the latency? How many errors?
  • Incoming data: has its shape changed from the training data? (drift)
  • Prediction quality: is the model still accurate?
  • Business impact: has it achieved the goal, such as reducing fraud or improving customer satisfaction?

All of this is recorded in the logs, a diary of the system: every request, when it arrived, how long it took, and the error if there was one.

5.5 Cost: Per Hour and Per Token

Cost questions are a fixed pattern in the exam. There are two main ways to pay:

Payment methodHow it is calculatedIllustrative exampleSuits
Per hour (machines)Price per hour × number of machines × number of hoursA GPU at USD 4/hour × 24 hours × 30 days = USD 2,880 per month for one machineLarge, continuous load
By usage (tokens or requests)Number of tokens × price per tokenIf the price is USD 2 per million tokens, then 50 million tokens = USD 100Small or intermittent load

The figures above are for illustration only. The important idea: a machine you pay for by the hour is paid for even while it is idle. A machine running at 15% utilisation means that most of what you pay is wasted.

Common sources of waste, which you will learn to detect in Unit 11:

  • Machines running 24 hours for a load that exists for only 8 hours.
  • A huge model for a task that a small model would handle.
  • Reprocessing all the documents daily although few of them have changed.
  • Forgotten development environments running over the weekend.
The exam rewards “the cheapest sufficient solution”: the lowest cost that meets all the hard requirements. Not the absolute cheapest, and not the absolute most powerful.

5.6 Personal Data, Encryption and Permissions

Personal data: any information that identifies a specific person, directly or when combined with other information: the name, the ID number, the mobile number, the address, and even a purchase history linked to a name.

Sensitive data: a type of personal data whose disclosure causes greater harm, such as health and genetic data. It needs stronger protection. You will learn its official list precisely in Unit 10.

ControlMeaningAnalogy
EncryptionConverting data into symbols that are unreadable except to whoever holds the keyA message written in a secret language

Encryption at restProtecting data while it is stored on diskA safe in the office

An important warning: encryption does not make the use of data lawful. If you collect data without a lawful basis, encrypting it does not correct the violation. Encryption protects against theft, but it does not answer the question “are we entitled to use this data at all?”

5.7 SDAIA and the Data Protection Law, and Why the Four Constraints Come Together

SDAIA is the Saudi Data and AI Authority, the national body responsible for data and artificial intelligence in the Kingdom. It issued the AI Ethics Principles and guidelines for generative AI, and it is the body that awards the AI professional badges you are preparing for.

The Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL) is a Saudi law that regulates the collection, processing and sharing of personal data, and gives data subjects rights such as being informed of what is collected about them and accessing it. In Unit 10 you will learn SDAIA's seven principles and the law's obligations by their exact names, because the exam asks about them verbatim.

Applied Scenario

Read a short business story and extract from it the components of any artificial intelligence project: the data, the type of task, the metric, the speed, the cost and the privacy.

The Scenario

A hospital in Riyadh suffers from patient no-shows: about 10% of appointments are missed by their owners, so doctors lose working hours. The hospital has a three-year record of appointments: patient age, clinic, appointment day and hour, number of days between booking and appointment, and whether the patient attended or not.

The hospital wants a system that predicts every morning which appointments are at risk of no-show, so as to send an extra reminder message to their owners. One employee suggested using a large language model from an external provider “because it is the smartest”, and sending it the complete patient records.

Solution Steps

  1. Data and label: the features are age, clinic, day, hour and days between booking and appointment. The label is “did they attend?” The data is a structured table, and its labels exist in the historical record.
  2. Type of learning and task: supervised learning, because the answers are known from the past. The task is binary classification: will not attend or will attend.
  3. Suitable model: a structured table is well served by a traditional machine learning model. A large language model is not necessary, and is more expensive and slower here.
  4. Metric: no-shows are only 10%, that is, imbalanced data, so we do not rely on accuracy. A model that always says “will attend” has an accuracy of 90% and is useless. The cost of an extra reminder is small (a message), and the cost of a miss is larger (a wasted doctor's hour), so we care about recall with reasonable precision.
  5. Speed: the prediction is made once each morning for all of the day's appointments, so no user is waiting. A daily batch run is enough, and there is no need for a fast server running all the time.
  6. Split: we train on the first two years and test on the last year, so that the model is tested on a “future” it has not seen.
  7. Privacy: patient records are sensitive health data. Sending them in full to an external provider contradicts the principle of data minimisation and may violate hosting conditions. The proposed model does not need the name or the ID number, and can be run inside the hospital.

Exam Traps

  • “The large language model is the smartest, so it is the most suitable”: the most suitable is the simplest option that meets the requirement.
  • “90% accuracy is excellent”: with 10% no-shows, this is the accuracy of a model that does nothing.
  • “We need a server with a response time under 100 ms”: no one is waiting, and batch processing is enough.
  • “Encryption is enough for sending the records to the provider”: encryption does not justify collecting or sending data we do not need.
  • “We test on a random sample from all the years”: when predicting the future, the test must be on a period later than the training period.
What You Must Master

For any new scenario, write six lines: the data and label, the type of task, the simplest suitable model, the metric and why, whether it needs an immediate response, and what the sensitive data is and how it is protected.

Exercises & Unit Review

Try to answer on your own first, then open the question to see the worked answer.

  1. 1What is the difference between traditional programming and machine learning in one sentence?
    Answer

    In traditional programming we write the rules ourselves, and in machine learning we give examples with their answers and the algorithm extracts the rules (the model) by itself.

  2. 2Is all artificial intelligence machine learning? Is all machine learning generative?
    Answer

    Neither. Artificial intelligence includes written-rule systems, and machine learning includes models that predict a number or a category without generating content.

  3. 3What is the difference between training and inference, and which one recurs with every user?
    Answer

    Training is making the model from data, and inference is using it to answer. Inference recurs with every request, which is why it usually accounts for most of the ongoing cost.

  4. 4Why do we not test the model on the same training data?
    Answer

    Because it has seen it and may have memorised it, so it will look excellent without our knowing its performance on new cases. We test it on data it has never seen.

  5. 5A model achieved 99% in training and 72% in validation. What is the problem?
    Answer

    Overfitting: it memorised the training data and did not generalise. Remedies include more data, a simpler model, regularisation, or early stopping.

  6. 6In 1,000 cases of which 10 are positive, a model always says “negative”. What are its accuracy and recall?
    Answer

    Its accuracy is 990/1000 = 99%, and its recall is 0/10 = 0%. This is why accuracy is unsuitable with imbalanced data.

  7. 7When do we prefer recall over precision?
    Answer

    When missing the case is more expensive than a false alarm, such as diagnosing a serious disease or major fraud.

  8. 8The review team complains of too many false alerts. Do we retrain the model?
    Answer

    Not first. We raise the threshold so false alarms fall, accepting a drop in recall. Changing the threshold does not require retraining.

  9. 9Why does a token not equal one word, and why does its count matter to us?
    Answer

    Because the tokenizer may split a word into parts depending on the language and the model. Its count matters because cost, latency and the context-window limit are all measured in tokens.

  10. 10The company's policy documents change weekly and the source must be cited in every answer. RAG or fine-tuning?
    Answer

    RAG, because it retrieves the latest version of the documents at question time and allows citation. Fine-tuning changes style and does not keep up with continuous change.
