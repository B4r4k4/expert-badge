Unit Introduction

The exam does not ask you to derive algorithms. It asks: which family suits this situation, and why? Is the difference between the two models real or noise? What is the value of precision, recall, F1, MAE and nDCG in this table? Which threshold is correct given the analyst capacity available? This unit continues what the previous unit began: the map of supervised, unsupervised and specialised algorithms; the statistics an engineer needs to read results correctly; hyper-parameter tuning, model selection and interpretability; then metrics worked through with complete numbers, the design of the evaluation set, data engineering, and search, recommendation and time-series systems in production.

Before You Start

Unit 02 taught you how to handle data. This unit completes Domain 1 with three things: the algorithm map (what kinds of model exist and when each one fits), practical statistics (how to tell that the difference between two models is real and not chance), and metrics with numbers (calculating precision, recall, forecasting errors and ranking quality step by step).

Do not be afraid of the word “statistics”. What you need here is small and practical: how to read a number computed on a sample, and when to trust it. All the calculations in Chapter 3 are multiplication and division.

You need these first: Unit 00 (Chapter 3: Metrics) and Unit 02 (metrics, the threshold and imbalance).

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

  • Distinguish the supervised and unsupervised algorithm families by their core idea, strengths, weaknesses and the scenario signals that point to each.
  • Calculate precision, recall, specificity, F1 and false-positive rate from a confusion matrix, and choose a feasible threshold according to capacity and cost.
  • Calculate Macro-F1, Weighted-F1, MAE, RMSE, WAPE, bias, Precision@k, Recall@k, MRR and nDCG, and interpret what each one reveals.
  • Estimate the uncertainty in metrics using the standard error and confidence intervals, and interpret the p-value, statistical power and the pitfalls of A/B tests.
  • Choose a hyper-parameter tuning method, compare models on criteria that go beyond the leaderboard, and choose the interpretability technique that suits the audience.
  • Design an evaluation set that is representative, stratified, isolated, versioned and tied to release gates.
  • Design data engineering components, the search and recommendation funnel, and time-series and streaming-learning practices in production.

How to Study This Unit

This is a unit of calculation and discrimination. In Chapter 3, redo every calculation by hand before reading the result, so that reading a confusion matrix and an error table becomes a habit that needs no thought on exam day. In the algorithm tables, memorise the “Choose when” column and the “Weaknesses” column, because distractors are usually built from a strong algorithm used in the wrong place. In statistics, practise stating the p-value in one correct sentence and calculating the approximate confidence interval; these two skills are enough to eliminate half the options in “which model is better?” questions.

Chapter 1

The Algorithm Map and Domain Patterns

1.1 Supervised Learning

In Plain Terms

This section is a “catalogue” of the best-known supervised learning models. Do not try to memorise the details of every model. For each one, remember three words: its idea, its strength and its weakness. The exam usually builds the wrong option from a model's weakness.

The summary that is enough for most questions: a table of numbers → boosted trees. Interpretability is a legal requirement → logistic regression. Images, audio or text in huge quantities → neural networks.

Real-World Example

Imagine each model as a different way of making a hiring decision. Logistic regression: you give each criterion a score (experience +3, degree +2) and add them up, so it is clear and easy to explain. Decision tree: successive questions (“Does he have more than 5 years of experience? Yes → Does he speak English?”). Random forest: a hundred committees, each of which saw part of the information, then a vote. Boosted trees: committee after committee, each focusing on the mistakes of the one before. Nearest neighbours: “Hire him if he resembles our best employees.”

Key Terms
Linear regressionالانحدار الخطي
A model that predicts a number by adding up the features after multiplying each by a weight. For example: price = 2,000 × area + 50,000 × number of rooms.
Sigmoid functionدالة sigmoid
A function that turns any number into a value between 0 and 1, so it is suitable for representing a probability. Logistic regression uses it.
Multicollinearityالارتباط الخطي المتعدد
When two or more features are very strongly correlated, such as weight in kilograms and weight in pounds. It makes the model's weights unstable and hard to interpret.
Bootstrap aggregatingBagging وBootstrap
Training many models, each on a random sample drawn from the data with replacement (bootstrap), then averaging their opinions (bagging). It reduces fluctuation.
Boostingالتعزيز
Building small models in sequence, each one learning from the errors of the one before, then combining them all into one strong model.
Support Vector Machine — SVMآلات المتجهات الداعمة
A model that looks for a dividing line between the two classes with the widest possible distance from the nearest examples. Strong on small data, and does not scale to huge data.
k-Nearest Neighbours — k-NNأقرب الجيران
It learns nothing in advance: for each question it looks up the k nearest stored examples and takes the majority opinion. Simple, but slow at prediction time.
Naive Bayesبايز الساذج
A very fast probabilistic model that assumes every feature is independent of the others. Often an unrealistic assumption, but a good baseline for text.

What the Exam Tests

For each family: the core idea, what it does well and what it does not, and the scenario signals that point to it. The general rule: on tabular data, boosted trees are the strong default; when interpretability for regulators is a requirement, logistic regression comes first; when the data is unstructured and very large, neural networks come first.

1.2 Unsupervised Learning and Anomaly Detection

In Plain Terms

In unsupervised learning there are no correct answers. The model searches by itself for groups of similar items, summarises the data, or detects what is abnormal.

There are three families here: clustering (dividing things into groups of similar items), dimensionality reduction (summarising dozens of columns into fewer), and anomaly detection (learning what “normal” looks like and alerting on what departs from it). A recurring trap: plotting tools such as t-SNE and UMAP are for looking only; their outputs are not used as features for a model.

Real-World Example

A shop has 100,000 customers and knows nothing about them except their purchase history. Clustering might reveal four groups: “buy only around end-of-month pay day”, “buy regularly every week”, “buy in the seasons”, and “a few big customers”. Nobody told the model about these groups; it discovered them itself.

Key Terms
Clusteringالعنقدة
Dividing the data into groups (clusters) whose members are similar, without prior answers.
k-meansk-means
A clustering method that divides the data into k groups, each point belonging to the nearest centre. You must set k in advance.
DBSCAN / HDBSCANالعنقدة بالكثافة
Methods that treat a cluster as a crowded region of points and treat isolated points as noise. They discover irregular shapes.
Principal Component Analysis — PCAتحليل المكونات الرئيسية
A method that summarises many columns into a smaller number of “components” that preserve most of the variance in the data. Useful for compression and for speeding up models.
Visualisation embeddingst-SNE وUMAP
Tools that draw high-dimensional data on a two-dimensional plane so that we can look at it. Distances in the plot are not accurate, so it is for looking only.
Isolation ForestIsolation Forest
An anomaly-detection method based on the idea that a strange case is easy to isolate from the others with few questions.
Autoencoderالمشفّر التلقائي
A neural network that learns to compress the data and then rebuild it. If it fails to rebuild a case, that case is probably abnormal.

1.3 Specialised Problem Types

In Plain Terms

Some problems have a special nature: recommendations (“you may also like”), time-series forecasting (next week's sales), ranking and search, computer vision, and speech.

Two ideas recur. The first: large systems work in stages, a fast, cheap stage that picks a few hundred from millions of options, then a more precise stage that ranks them. The second: the Saudi calendar (Ramadan, the Eids and salary day) must enter forecasting models explicitly, because each Gregorian year Ramadan arrives about 11 days earlier than it did in the previous one.

Real-World Example

A video platform with a million clips. You cannot run a heavy model over a million clips for every user every time. So you first quickly pick 500 clips close to their interests, then a precise model ranks them, then you apply rules such as “do not show three consecutive clips from the same channel”. A new user with no history? That is the cold start, so we show them the most popular in their region.

Key Terms
Collaborative filteringالترشيح التعاوني
A recommendation based on similarity of behaviour: “people who bought what you bought also bought…”.
Cold startالبداية الباردة (في التوصيات)
A new user or new product with no interaction history, so the system has nothing to base the recommendation on.
Classical forecastingالتمهيد الأسّي وARIMA
Traditional statistical models that forecast a time series from its past values, trend and seasonality.
Seasonal naive forecastالتنبؤ الموسمي الساذج
The simplest forecast: “next week is the same as last week”. A strong baseline that any model must beat.
Learning-to-rankتعلّم الترتيب
Models that learn to order the results (the most suitable first) instead of classifying each result on its own.
Detection / segmentationالكشف والتقطيع
Computer vision tasks. Detection: drawing a box around everything in the image with its name. Segmentation: determining which thing each pixel belongs to.
Automatic Speech Recognition — ASRالتعرف الآلي على الكلام
Converting spoken speech into written text.
Diarisationتمييز المتحدثين
Determining who spoke and when in a recording that has more than one person.

What the Exam Tests

Recommender systems combine collaborative filtering (users who behave alike like similar items — matrix factorisation, two-tower neural models) with content-based signals (item attributes, text and image embeddings). The cold-start problem — new users or items with no history — is solved with content features, popularity priors and onboarding questions. Production recommenders usually work in two stages: fast candidate retrieval (approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) search over the embeddings), then a heavier ranking model, then business rules (diversity, freshness, compliance). Offline metrics (Recall@k, nDCG) must be confirmed by live A/B tests because of feedback loops: the system only learns about what it chose to show.

Time-series forecasting ranges from classical statistical models (exponential smoothing, ARIMA/SARIMA) to boosted trees with lag and calendar features, to deep models for many related series. The strongest practical baseline is often the seasonal naive forecast (“same as last week”) — any model must beat it. Saudi calendar effects (Ramadan, which moves forward by about 11 days each Gregorian year, the Eids, salary day, school terms and the summer season) must be encoded explicitly, usually with Hijri-calendar features. Always validate forward in time; the metrics are MAE, RMSE, MAPE (unsafe near zero) and sMAPE/MASE.

Ranking and search optimise the order, not the labels: learning-to-rank with pointwise, pairwise or listwise approaches, evaluated with nDCG, MRR and Precision@k — the same metrics used for RAG retrievers.

1.4 Domain Patterns That Recur in Scenarios

In Plain Terms

This table is a set of “identity cards” for the domains that recur most often in scenarios: fraud, credit, churn, demand forecasting, maintenance, documents, customer service, and health. Each domain has four cells: the nature of its data, the suitable model and metric, and what must be done operationally.

Use it like this: when the question mentions “loans”, immediately remember “selection bias” and “interpretability”. When it mentions “equipment maintenance”, remember “failures are rare” and “the edge”. The correct option is usually the one that respects this specificity.

Real-World Example

Selection bias in credit: the bank knows whether the customer repaid or not only for those whose financing it approved. For those who were rejected it knows nothing; many of them might have repaid. So a model trained on the historical record sees an incomplete picture, and it may repeat the mistakes of old rejections.

Key Terms

When the available data is unrepresentative because the way it was collected selected a particular group, such as knowing the outcomes of approved loans only.
Monotonic constraintsالقيود الرتيبة
Forcing the model so that the effect of a feature is always in one direction. For example: an increase in income cannot raise the risk of default. It makes the model logical and justifiable.
Uplift modellingنمذجة الأثر الصافي
Predicting who will change their behaviour because of the campaign, not who would stay anyway. It prevents wasting incentives on customers who would have stayed regardless.
Weighted Absolute Percentage ErrorWAPE
The sum of absolute errors divided by the sum of actual values. A robust error percentage that is easy to explain.
Censored dataالبيانات المبتورة
Data whose story is not yet complete. For example, a machine that has not failed so far: we do not know when it will fail, only that it has held up to today.
Edgeالحافة
Running the model on a device close to where the data is (a camera, a machine or a phone) instead of sending it to the cloud.
Intentالنية
What the user wants from their message: an enquiry about an invoice, a complaint, or a cancellation request. Classifying intents is a common task in customer service.

A study carried out before processing high-risk personal data, to identify the risks to individuals and ways of reducing them.

Statistics, Tuning, Model Selection and Interpretability

2.1 Sampling and Uncertainty

In Plain Terms

If you measure a model's accuracy on only 100 examples, the number you get is “approximate”. If you tried another 100 examples you might get a slightly different number. Statistics tells you how much the number may change, and this is the margin of error.

The simple rule: the larger the sample, the smaller the margin of error and the greater the confidence. The practical consequence: if the difference between two models is smaller than the margin of error, you cannot say that one of them is better.

Real-World Example

An opinion poll asked only 100 people, and 90 of them said “satisfied”. The true proportion might be between 84% and 96%, that is ±6 points. If it had asked 1,000 people the margin would have been only ±2. By calculation: standard error = √(0.9 × 0.1 ÷ 100) = √0.0009 = 0.03. Then 1.96 × 0.03 ≈ 0.059, that is about ±6 points.

Key Terms
Sampleالعينة
A part of the whole that we measure, because measuring the whole is impossible or costly. The test set is a sample of all future cases.
Standard error — SEالخطأ المعياري
A measure of how much the computed number would fluctuate if we repeated the measurement on different samples. It shrinks as the sample grows.
95% confidence intervalفترة الثقة 95%
A range around the measured number in which we expect, with 95% confidence, the true value to fall. Calculated approximately as: the number ± 1.96 × the standard error.
Bootstrapإعادة المعاينة
A way of estimating uncertainty: we draw many random samples with replacement from the test set, compute the metric on each, and see how much it varies.
McNemar's testاختبار ماكنيمار
A statistical test for comparing two classifiers on the same examples: do their errors really differ?

What the Exam Tests

Every metric computed on a sample is an estimate that carries uncertainty. For a proportion such as accuracy p measured on n examples, the standard error is approximately:

SE ≈ √( p(1 − p) / n ) 95% CI ≈ p ± 1.96 × SE

p = 0.90, n = 1,000 → SE ≈ 0.0095 → 95% CI ≈ ±1.9 points

p = 0.90, n = 100 → SE ≈ 0.030 → 95% CI ≈ ±5.9 points

So the statement “model B is 1 point better than A on 1,000 test cases” is usually not evidence of a real difference. Before declaring a winner, use confidence intervals, bootstrap resampling, or paired tests (McNemar's test for two classifiers on the same examples).

A difference smaller than the confidence interval is not a win; it is noise until proven otherwise.

2.2 Hypothesis Testing in A/B Tests

In Plain Terms

A/B test: we split users randomly into two groups. Group A sees the old system and B sees the new one. Then we compare the results. But even if the two versions were exactly identical, the results would differ slightly by chance. So how do we know that the difference is real?

We start from the assumption of “no difference” (the null hypothesis), then ask: if there really were no difference, what is the probability of seeing a difference this large by chance? That probability is the p-value. If it is small (less than 5%) we say: “It is hard for this to be chance.” Note: p is not the probability that the new version is better.

Real-World Example

You toss a coin 10 times and heads comes up 8 times. Is the coin biased? We first assume it is fair (the null hypothesis), and calculate: what is the probability of 8 heads or more from a fair coin? About 5.5%. That is p. It does not mean “a 94.5% probability that the coin is biased”; it means “if it were fair, this result is somewhat rare”.

Key Terms
A/B testتجربة A/B
A randomised experiment that compares two versions on real users: half of them see A and half see B, and then we compare a metric agreed in advance.
Null hypothesisالفرضية الصفرية
The initial assumption that “there is no difference”. We try to see whether the data contradicts it strongly enough.
p-valueالقيمة الاحتمالية
The probability of seeing a result this large or larger if the null hypothesis were true. It is not the probability that the hypothesis is true, nor the probability that the new version is better.
Significance level (α)مستوى الدلالة
The limit at which we decide that the result is “statistically significant”, usually 0.05. It means we accept being wrong by declaring a non-existent difference in 5% of experiments.
Statistical powerالقوة الإحصائية
The probability that the experiment detects a difference that really exists. Usually set at 80%, and it determines how many users we need.
Armالذراع
Each group in the experiment. A is one arm and B is one arm.

What the Exam Tests

  • Null hypothesis: “no difference”.
  • p-value: the probability of seeing a difference at least this large if the null hypothesis were true — not the probability that the new variant is better.
  • Significance level of 0.05: accepts a 5% false-positive rate per test.
  • Statistical power — commonly 80%: the probability of detecting a real effect of a given size; it determines the sample size.
  • Small effects need very large samples: detecting a rise in conversion from 5.0% to 5.5% needs tens of thousands of users in each arm.

A correct statement for p = 0.03: “If there were no real difference, a gap this large or larger would appear in about 3% of runs.” Options that say “there is a 97% probability that the new model is better” or “there is a 3% probability that the null hypothesis is true” are wrong.

2.3 Classic A/B Test Pitfalls

In Plain Terms

A/B tests are easy to spoil. This table lists the best-known mistakes.

The most important: peeking, that is, looking at the results every day and stopping when they “look” good. This is like tossing a coin until you get the result you want. multiple comparisons: if you measure 20 things, one of them will seem to “succeed” by chance. sample ratio mismatch: you designed the split as 50/50 and it came out 52/48, so the splitting mechanism itself is broken, and the result cannot be trusted.

Real-World Example

Multiple comparisons: if you toss 20 fair coins 10 times each, you will probably find that one of them gave 8 heads or more, and you think it is biased. It is not biased; you simply tried many times and chance appeared. The novelty effect: the colour of the buy button changes and people press it more in the first days out of curiosity, then return to their habit. That is why the experiment is run for at least one full week.

Key Terms
Peekingالتلصص
Checking the results of the experiment repeatedly while it runs and stopping it at the first “significant” result. It multiplies the probability of announcing a false win.
Sequential testingالاختبار المتتابع
Statistical methods designed to allow repeated checking during the experiment without inflating errors.
Bonferroni correctionتصحيح بونفيروني
Tightening the significance threshold when testing many things: with 20 metrics we use 0.05 ÷ 20 = 0.0025 instead of 0.05.
Sample Ratio Mismatch — SRMعدم تطابق نسبة العينة
When the actual split between the arms differs from the designed one (such as 52/48 instead of 50/50). A sign of a fault in the assignment mechanism.
Novelty effectأثر الجِدّة
Users' interaction with the new thing merely because it is new, after which the effect fades over time.
Interferenceالتداخل
When users in one arm affect those in the other, as in marketplaces or social networks, so the results get mixed.

2.4 Correlation, Causation and Base Rates

In Plain Terms

Correlation is not causation. Two things rising together does not mean one causes the other. There may be a third cause driving both. To find the cause we need a randomised experiment or special methods, not just a predictive model.

The second idea is the base rate: when something is very rare, even a very accurate test will produce mostly false positive results. This surprises everyone, but it is exactly the reason precision is low in fraud detection.

Real-World Example

A disease affects 1 person in 100, and the test is 95% accurate. We test 10,000 people. There are 100 sick people, and the test detects 95 of them. There are 9,900 healthy people, but the test errs on 5% of them, that is 495 false positive results. So the total number of positive results is 95 + 495 = 590, and only 95 of them are actually sick. The probability that someone with a positive result is sick = 95 ÷ 590 ≈ 16%.

Key Terms
Correlationالارتباط
Two variables changing together (rising together, or one rising while the other falls). It does not prove that one causes the other.
Causationالسببية
Changing one thing is what actually brings about the change in the other. Usually established by a randomised experiment.
Confounderالمتغير المُربك
A third factor that affects both at once and so creates a correlation between them. Summer raises ice-cream sales and raises drowning cases.
Simpson's paradoxمفارقة سيمبسون
A trend that appears in each subgroup separately, then reverses when they are combined, because the sizes of the groups differ.
Base rateالمعدل الأساسي
The proportion of the case in the population before any test, such as a 1% disease prevalence. Ignoring it leads to a wrong interpretation of the results.
Sensitivity / specificityالحساسية والنوعية
Sensitivity: the proportion of sick people the test detects, which is the same as recall. Specificity: the proportion of healthy people of whom the test says “healthy”.
Difference-in-differencesفرق الفروق
A method of estimating the causal effect: we compare the change in a group to which the intervention was applied with the change in a similar group to which it was not applied, over the same period.

What the Exam Tests

Correlation is not causation. Observational data show associations confounded by other variables (ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer). Simpson's paradox: a trend present in every subgroup can reverse when the groups are combined, because their sizes differ — so always look at the slices.

Causal questions such as “did the retention campaign reduce churn?” need randomised experiments or causal-inference methods (difference-in-differences, propensity matching, uplift modelling), not just a predictive model.

Base Rates

With a disease prevalence of 1% and a test with 95% sensitivity and 95% specificity, a positive result means only about a 16% probability of disease, because the false positives from the healthy majority (99%) outnumber the true positives. Out of 10,000 people:

sick = 100 → TP = 0.95 × 100 = 95

healthy = 9,900 → FP = 0.05 × 9,900 = 495

P(disease | positive) = 95 / (95 + 495) ≈ 0.16

This is the arithmetic behind low precision in rare-event detection such as fraud.

2.5 Hyper-parameter Tuning

In Plain Terms

Hyper-parameters are settings we choose before training, such as tree depth or learning rate. How do we find the best ones? We try. The question is: how do we try intelligently without wasting time?

Grid search tries every combination, and is suitable only if there are few. Random search tries random combinations, and it is a good default choice. Bayesian optimisation learns from previous trials where to try next, and suits costly training. Hyperband starts with many short trials and continues only the promising ones.

Real-World Example

You are searching for the best biscuit recipe. Grid search: you try every amount of sugar (5 amounts) with every temperature (5 temperatures) with every time (5 times), that is 125 biscuits. Random search: 25 biscuits with random combinations, and you will probably find a good recipe. Bayesian: after each biscuit you taste and decide intelligently what to try next. Hyperband: you bake 30 small biscuits for a few minutes, and carry on baking only the best 5.

2.6 Model Selection Beyond the Leaderboard

In Plain Terms

A leaderboard is a table that ranks models by their score alone. But the highest score is not always the right choice. The expert engineer compares on eight points: performance on the important classes, correctness of the probabilities, speed, cost, robustness, interpretability, maintenance, and fairness.

Remember the same logic from Unit 01: a model that is half a point better but ten times more expensive and does not achieve the required response time is the wrong choice.

Real-World Example

You are choosing an employee. Candidate A scored 95 on the test, but asks for double the salary, cannot explain how he works, and needs 3 months to start. Candidate B scored 94, has a reasonable salary, starts tomorrow, and explains every decision. In most cases B is the better choice.

Key Terms
Leaderboardلوحة الصدارة
A table that ranks models by their score on a single metric. Useful as a start, but not enough for a production decision.
Robustnessالمتانة
The model's ability to keep its performance in the face of noise or small changes in the data.
Ensemblesالمجمّعات
Combining several models into one by averaging their opinions or by a model that learns how to combine them (stacking). They add a little accuracy at the expense of speed and complexity.

What the Exam Tests

The best offline score is only one criterion. An expert engineer compares candidates on:

  • Performance on critical slices.
  • Calibration.
  • Latency and memory at the required throughput.
  • Cost to train and serve.
  • Robustness to drift and noise.
  • The interpretability that regulation requires.
  • Maintenance burden (dependencies, retraining time).
  • Fairness.
A model that is half a point better but ten times more expensive, opaque and slower than the SLA is the wrong choice — the same logic as in spec-matching questions.

Ensembles — averaging, stacking and blending — usually add a little accuracy at the price of latency, cost and complexity. They are worth it in competitions, and often not worth it in production unless the gain has business value.

2.7 Interpretability for Classical Models

In Plain Terms

Interpretability is being able to answer “why did the model say this?” There are two kinds: global explanation (what the model depends on in general) and local explanation (why it made this particular decision for this particular person).

For whom do we explain? For three audiences: developers to discover errors (a suspiciously dominant feature is often leakage), regulators for auditing, and the person affected by the decision so that they know the reason and can object.

Real-World Example

Mohammed's financing application was rejected. Global explanation: “The model generally depends on income, debt ratio and repayment history.” Local explanation (SHAP) for Mohammed: “Your current debt ratio of 65% lowered the score a great deal.” Counterfactual explanation: “Had your debt ratio been 40% or less, your application would have been accepted.” The last is very useful to Mohammed because it tells him what to do.

In Plain Terms

This section revisits what you learnt in Unit 00 with bigger numbers, closer to the exam. What is required is to fill in the confusion matrix from the question's data, then calculate all the metrics from it.

The method: start from the three given facts (the total number, the number of truly positive cases, and how many the model flagged and how many of those were correct), then fill in the four cells by subtraction.

3.3 Averaging in Multi-Class Classification

In Plain Terms

When the model classifies into more than two classes (billing, network, legal threat, safety incident), we calculate F1 for each class. But how do we combine them into a single number? There are two ways, and they give two very different pictures.

Macro: a simple average; every class has the same weight however small it is, so it exposes small, weak classes. Weighted: an average weighted by the size of each class, so large classes dominate and the problems of the small ones disappear. If the rare classes are the most dangerous, Macro is the honest one.

Real-World Example

Macro: we add the scores of the four classes and divide by 4: (0.955 + 0.910 + 0.400 + 0.286) ÷ 4 ≈ 0.64. Weighted: we multiply each score by the number of its cases: the billing class has 6,000 cases and the safety incident only 50, so the weakness of the small class drowns in the strength of the large one, and it comes out ≈ 0.93. By the second the model looks excellent, and it misses 80% of the safety incidents!

Key Terms
SupportSupport
The number of true cases of each class in the test set.
Micro averagingMicro-F1
Pooling all the decisions from all the classes into one matrix and then calculating. It behaves like overall accuracy, so large classes dominate it.

3.4 Regression Metrics Side by Side

In Plain Terms

When the model predicts a number (such as sales), we measure the error in different ways, and each way reveals something. MAE is the average of the errors regardless of their sign. RMSE magnifies large errors. WAPE is the error as a proportion of the total. Bias is whether the model tends to over-estimate or under-estimate.

MAPE calculates the error as a percentage for each day, so it fails when actual sales are zero (division by zero). That is why it is not suitable for products that go days without sales.

Real-World Example

The five errors: 10, 5, 5, 60, 5. MAE = their sum ÷ 5 = 85 ÷ 5 = 17. RMSE: we square each error (100, 25, 25, 3,600, 25), take the mean (755), then the root ≈ 27.5. Larger than MAE because the error of 60 was magnified by squaring. Bias: we add the errors with their signs (−10 + 5 + 5 − 60 + 5 = −55) and divide by 5 = −11, meaning the model under-estimates, and this means stock-outs.

Key Terms
Mean Absolute ErrorMAE
The mean of the absolute error values (without sign). In the same units as the target, and easy to understand.
Root Mean Squared ErrorRMSE
The square root of the mean of the squared errors. It penalises large errors far more than small ones.
Mean Absolute Percentage ErrorMAPE
The mean error as a percentage of the actual value. Undefined when the actual value is zero.
Forecast biasالانحياز (في التنبؤ)
The mean of the errors with their signs. Negative means the model consistently under-estimates, and positive means it over-estimates.
Intermittent demandالطلب المتقطع
Demand for a product that goes many days without sales, such as rare spare parts.

3.5 Ranking and Retrieval Metrics

In Plain Terms

In search (and in a RAG system) it is not enough to find the right documents; their ranking matters. These metrics measure both.

Precision@5: of the first 5 results, how many are correct? Recall@5: of all the correct documents, how many appeared in the first 5? MRR: does the first correct result appear early? nDCG: a score that rewards correct results more the higher they are in the list.

Real-World Example

You searched for “leave policy”, and there are 3 correct documents. 5 results appeared, and the correct ones are in positions 2 and 4. Precision@5 = 2 ÷ 5 = 0.40. Recall@5 = 2 ÷ 3 ≈ 0.67, meaning we missed one document. MRR = 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5, because the first correct result came in second position. Had it come first it would have been 1.

Key Terms
Precision at kPrecision@k
The proportion of correct results among the first k results.
Mean Reciprocal RankMRR
The mean of (1 ÷ the rank of the first correct result) across many queries. 1 means the correct one always comes first.
normalised Discounted Cumulative GainnDCG
A score between 0 and 1 for the quality of the ranking. It gives a correct result fewer points the further down the list it falls, then compares with the ideal ranking.
Retrieverالمسترجع
The part of a RAG system that searches for passages relevant to the question before sending them to the model.

What the Exam Tests

A query has 3 relevant documents in the corpus. The system returns 5 results, and the relevant ones are at ranks 2 and 4.

Precision@5 = 2 / 5 = 0.40

Recall@5 = 2 / 3 ≈ 0.67 (one relevant document never retrieved)

MRR = 1 / rank of first relevant = 1 / 2 = 0.50 (averaged over many queries)

DCG@5 = 1/log₂(3) + 1/log₂(5) ≈ 0.631 + 0.431 = 1.062

IDCG@5 (relevant at ranks 1–3) = 1 + 0.631 + 0.5 = 2.131 → nDCG@5 ≈ 1.062 / 2.131 ≈ 0.50

nDCG rewards relevant results more the higher they are ranked, using a logarithmic discount (the document at rank i is divided by log₂(i+1)).

In RAG, the retriever's Recall@k is usually the first metric to fix: if the right passage never reaches the model, no prompt can save the answer. Precision and ranking come after that, to reduce noise and tokens.

3.6 Calibration in Numbers

In Plain Terms

A model that ranks customers correctly (the riskiest at the top) may nevertheless give wrong probabilities. It says of a customer “40% probability of default” when the reality is 20%. The ranking is right, and the numbers are exaggerated.

Does this matter? If we use only the ranking (we review the 100 riskiest customers), no. But if we use the probability itself to set the interest rate or financial provisions, wrong probabilities mean wrong prices. The remedy is to “calibrate” the probabilities without retraining.

Real-World Example

A weather presenter says “70% chance of rain” every day. If it really rains on 70% of those days, he is “calibrated”. If it rains on only 35%, he exaggerates, even though he may be right that “today is more likely to rain than yesterday”. The table here is like that: of the “40%+” bin, only 31% actually defaulted.

3.7 Designing the Evaluation Set

In Plain Terms

The evaluation set is the “exam” by which we judge the model. If the exam is badly designed, we will judge wrongly. This section gives six qualities of a good evaluation set: representative of reality, stratified with enough examples of the rare important cases, clean labels, isolated and never used in training, versioned, and linked to a decision (each metric has a release threshold).

For LLM systems: we do not write “the correct literal answer”, because a good answer can be phrased in many ways. Instead we write expected properties: “must cite document X”, “must refuse”, “must ask a clarifying question”.

Real-World Example

A school designs a final exam. Representative: it covers all the chapters of the syllabus. Stratified: it guarantees enough questions from the hard chapter even if it is small. Clean: the model answer was reviewed by more than one teacher. Isolated: students do not see it before the exam. Versioned: “Exam 2026 version B”, so we do not compare the score of one version with another. Linked to a decision: 60 and above is a pass.

Key Terms
Release gateبوابة الإطلاق
A condition set in advance that the model must pass before release, such as “recall ≥ 90% on the golden set”.
Development setمجموعة التطوير
A set the team uses often during improvement, separate from the test set, which is used rarely.
Multi-turn conversationالمحادثة متعددة الأدوار
A conversation with more than one consecutive question and answer, where the later question depends on what came before.
Adversarial promptالأمر العدائي
A message designed to deceive the model or push it into violating its instructions.

What the Exam Tests

An evaluation set is a specification written in data: it defines what “good” means, so it must be designed, not merely sampled.

  1. Representative: it matches the production population in channels, regions, dialects, devices and time; refresh it when the population changes.
  2. Stratified for what matters: enough examples of rare but critical cases (fraud types, safety incidents, minority classes, unanswerable questions) to measure them with useful confidence, even if that means over-representing them relative to production; report both the overall figures and the per-slice figures.
  3. Clean labels: labelled by experts following guidelines, with agreement measured; ambiguous cases are resolved or flagged.
  4. Isolated: never used for training or tuning; access-controlled so that it does not leak into prompts or fine-tuning data; keep a development set for iteration separate from a test set that is rarely used.
  5. Versioned: results are comparable only on the same version; record the version on which each model was evaluated.
  6. Linked to decisions: each metric has a threshold agreed in advance (a release gate) and an owner.

Golden Sets for LLM Systems

These add: real user queries (anonymised), multi-turn conversations, adversarial prompts, questions that require refusal, dialect and mixed-language variants, and expected properties (must cite source X, must refuse, must ask a clarifying question) instead of single exact answers.

4.2 Batch versus Streaming, and Reproducibility

In Plain Terms

Batch or streaming? Streaming gives data that is fresh to the second, but it is harder and more expensive to build and maintain. So we use it only when freshness changes the decision, as in fraud. As for “the customer's sales over the last 90 days”, nightly processing is enough.

reproducibility: we must always be able to say “this model was trained on exactly this version of the data, with this code, with these settings”. Without that there can be no auditing, no investigation of an incident, and no carrying out of a request to delete a person's data.

Real-World Example

A restaurant kitchen: some ingredients are prepared once in the morning (batch), such as sauces. Some are prepared at the moment of the order (streaming), such as fried eggs. Preparing everything at the moment of the order is costly and slow for no benefit. Reproducibility: if a customer complains about yesterday's dish, you must know which batch of sauce and which recipe were used.

Key Terms
Backfillإعادة الملء
Recomputing a feature or data for past periods, for example when we add a new feature and need its historical values for training.
Reproducibilityقابلية إعادة الإنتاج
The ability to rebuild exactly the same model, by knowing the data, code and settings that were used.
Code commitCommit
A saved, numbered version of the source code in a version-control system such as Git.

What the Exam Tests

Batch versus streaming for features: streaming pipelines offer freshness in seconds but are harder to build, test and backfill. Use them only when freshness changes the decision (fraud, real-time personalisation); otherwise batch is cheaper and more reliable. The common hybrid pattern: long-window features computed in batch alongside a few short-window streaming features, all served from the connected feature store.

Reproducibility: every training run must point to a fixed data snapshot (table version, file fingerprints) together with the code version (commit) and the parameters.

“We cannot reproduce last quarter's model” is a governance failure: auditors, incident investigations and PDPL deletion requests all depend on knowing exactly which data the model saw.

4.3 The Multi-Stage Search and Recommendation Funnel

In Plain Terms

Search and recommendation work like a funnel: from millions of items we quickly pick a few hundred (candidate generation), then rank them with a more precise model (ranking), then apply business rules (re-ranking), and log everything.

Each stage has a time budget: the first stage does not exceed 10–30 ms because it works on millions of items, and ranking takes 20–50 ms because it works on only a few hundred.

Real-World Example

Hiring at a large company that received 10,000 applications. Stage 1: a fast automated screening by keywords selects 300 CVs. Stage 2: an HR employee reads the 300 carefully and ranks the best 20. Stage 3: rules such as “diversity of specialisations” and “priority for Saudi nationals in certain jobs”. It is not possible to read 10,000 CVs carefully.

The number of times an item was shown to users, whether or not they clicked on it.

4.4 Feedback, Cold Start, Arabic Search and Metrics

In Plain Terms

Recommendation systems fall into a circular trap: they show something and people click on it, so they learn that it is liked, so they show it more. Items that were not shown get no chance. Likewise, people click the first result more because it is first, not because it is the best (position bias).

The remedy: log where you showed everything, set aside a small share of the display for exploration, and measure success by real business outcomes (purchase and satisfaction), not by clicks alone. Arabic search has special challenges: the forms of alef, yaa and taa marbuta, diacritics, and product names that differ between regions.

Real-World Example

أحمد and احمد (the name “Ahmad” with and without the hamza), مستشفى and مستشفي (“hospital”, spelled with alef maqsura or with yaa), المدرسة and المدرسه (“the school”, spelled with taa marbuta or with haa): people write them in different forms. If the search system does not unify these forms (normalisation), the user will find results for some and not others. An example of dialects: جوال, موبايل and هاتف (“mobile phone” in Saudi usage, the transliterated “mobile”, and the formal “phone”) may mean the same product.

Key Terms

Users' tendency to click on the higher results because of their position, not because of their quality.

Setting aside a small part of the display for untried items, to collect information about them.

Unifying the forms of similar letters (أ/إ/آ → ا, ى → ي, ة → ه) and removing diacritics, so that the search matches despite differences in spelling.
Stemmingالتجذيع
Returning a word to a common root (المعلمون “the teachers” and معلم “teacher” → علم), so that the search finds the different forms of the word.
Zero-result rateنسبة البحث بلا نتائج
The proportion of searches that returned no result. A rise in it is an indicator of a problem in the index or in normalisation.

What the Exam Tests

Feedback and bias: users click only on what was shown, and they click the top positions more (position bias). Naive training on clicks reinforces whatever the current system displays. The remedy:

  • Log impressions together with their positions.
  • Use counterfactual training or a position-bias corrector.
  • Reserve a small share of traffic for exploration.
  • Validate with A/B tests on business outcomes (conversion, satisfaction, retention), not on click-through rate alone.

Cold start: new items gain exposure through content embeddings and exploration slots; new users get “most popular in their segment” recommendations and the preferences they give at onboarding.

Arabic search specifics: normalisation (the forms of alef, yaa and taa marbuta, and diacritics), light stemming or morphological analysis for keyword search, synonyms and dialectal forms (different names for the same product between regions), literal transliteration of brand names, and queries that mix Arabic and English. Hybrid search is usually the strongest baseline.

4.5 Time Series and Streaming Learning in Production

In Plain Terms

Time-series forecasting in production has special rules. The most important are three. Hierarchical consistency: the forecasts for the products must sum to the store forecast, and the store forecasts to the region forecast. Uncertainty: we do not give a single number but a range. forward testing: we test as if we were in the past and did not know the future.

The most important idea for the exam: safety stock is built on the quantile, not on the mean. If you expect to sell 100 on average and prepare only 100, you will run out of goods on roughly half the days.

Real-World Example

You expect your shop to sell 100 cartons of milk tomorrow on average, but actual sales may be between 80 and 130. If you prepare 100 you may run out. But if you say “I want the milk not to run out on 95% of days”, you prepare the 95% quantile, say 125 cartons. This is using the quantile instead of the mean.

Key Terms
Quantileالكمّية
The value below which a given proportion of the possible outcomes fall. The 95% quantile is a value that sales do not exceed in 95% of expected cases. Like the 95th percentile.
Prediction intervalفترة التنبؤ
A range in which we expect the actual value to fall with a stated probability, such as “between 80 and 130 with 90% probability”.
Backtestingالاختبار الرجعي
Evaluating the model by replaying the past: at each date we forecast using only what was known up to that date, then compare with what happened.
Watermarksالعلامات المائية
A mechanism in streaming systems that determines how long we wait for late events before closing a time window and computing it.
Exactly-onceمرة واحدة بالضبط
A guarantee that every event is processed once only, neither lost nor repeated, even during failures.
Alert fatigueإرهاق التنبيهات
When alerts become so frequent that the team starts ignoring them, including the important ones.

Applied Scenario

Apply calculation, statistics and model selection to a real decision: should we replace the current credit-scoring model with a new one? At what threshold, and with what safeguards?

The Scenario

A Saudi financing company uses logistic regression to score personal-finance applications. The data science team proposed a stacking ensemble of three boosted-tree models that achieved an AUC of 0.842, against 0.836 for the current model, on a test set of 1,000 applications; its latency is 180 ms against an SLA of 100 ms. The team trained the new model with up-weighting of the defaulter class.

When calibration was checked, the observed default rate in the 20–40% probability bin was about 14%, and in the 40%+ bin about 31%. The probabilities are used directly to set the profit margin. The team also ran a small A/B test on the app channel; it checked the results daily and stopped on day 4, when p = 0.04 appeared on one metric out of the 15 being monitored, and the actual split between the two arms was 53/47 although the design was 50/50.

Solution Steps

  1. Estimate the uncertainty: a difference of 0.006 in AUC on 1,000 examples is not sufficient evidence of superiority — for comparison, a proportion near 0.90 measured on 1,000 examples carries a confidence interval of about ±1.9 points on its own. Calculate a confidence interval for the AUC difference by bootstrap resampling on the same examples before making any claim.
  2. Evaluate the candidates on criteria beyond the leaderboard: the new model violates the latency SLA, is more opaque and more complex, and its gain is unproven — so it is the wrong choice by the logic of spec matching.
  3. Read the calibration: the probabilities are roughly double the observed rates above 10%, which is expected after up-weighting the minority class. If either model is used for pricing, apply Platt or isotonic calibration on a recent held-out period and monitor the Brier score and a reliability curve.
  4. Reject the A/B test result: peeking and early stopping inflate false positives; 15 metrics at 0.05 produce an expected false win; the sample ratio mismatch (53/47) means a broken assignment that must be investigated before reading anything; and 4 days is less than one full weekly cycle.
  5. Redesign the experiment: a primary metric fixed in advance, a sample size calculated for 80% power, a duration of at least one or two weekly cycles, and a correction for multiple comparisons on the secondary metrics.
  6. Check regulatory compliance: credit is a high-impact decision, so keep an interpretable model, or boosted trees with monotonic constraints and faithful reason codes (SHAP), fairness by group and human review; and remember that the training data contains selection bias (outcomes are known only for approved loans).
  7. Fix the evaluation set: a specific version, critical slices with sufficient representation, and a release gate agreed in advance for each metric, with an owner.

Exam Traps

  • Treating a small difference in AUC or accuracy on 1,000 examples as evidence of superiority without confidence intervals.
  • Interpreting p = 0.04 as “a 96% probability that the new model is better”.
  • Choosing the highest-scoring ensemble despite its violation of latency, interpretability and cost.
  • Confusing ranking quality (AUC) with the correctness of the probabilities (calibration) when the probabilities are used for pricing.
  • Calculating MAPE for intermittent demand that contains zeros, or reporting Weighted-F1 when the rare classes are the ones that matter.
  • Choosing a threshold that produces more alerts than the available review capacity.
What You Must Master

A decision documented with numbers: keep the current model with calibration, or rerun a correctly designed experiment, with statistical, operational and regulatory justification for every step.

Exercises & Unit Review

Try to answer on your own first, then open the question to see the worked answer.

  1. 1Tabular churn data with half a million rows. What is the strong default choice, and why?
    Answer

    Boosted trees (XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost): the best available for tabular data, they handle missing values, and they have fast inference on CPU. Deep networks do not usually win on this type of data.

  2. 2A model has 90% accuracy on 100 test examples. What is the approximate 95% confidence interval?
    Answer

    The standard error is √(0.9×0.1/100) = 0.03, so the confidence interval is about ±5.9 points. Any difference smaller than that between two models on this set is not evidence.

  3. 3What is the correct interpretation of the p-value p = 0.03 in an A/B test?
    Answer

    If there were no real difference, a gap this large or larger would appear in about 3% of runs. It is not the probability that the new variant is better, nor the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

  4. 4A disease has a prevalence of 1%, and the test has 95% sensitivity and 95% specificity. What is the probability of disease given a positive result?
    Answer

    Only about 16%: out of 10,000 people, there are 95 true positives against 495 false positives from the healthy majority. This is why precision is low in rare-event detection.

  5. 5From a confusion matrix: TP=400, FP=850, FN=100, TN=98,650. Calculate precision, recall and F1.
    Answer

    Precision is 400/1,250 = 32%, recall is 400/500 = 80%, and F1 = 2×0.32×0.80/1.12 ≈ 0.457. The overall accuracy of 99.05% is lower than the “never fraud” baseline (99.5%).

  6. 6The review team's capacity is 700 alerts, and the available thresholds produce 300, 600 and 1,250 alerts at 0.90, 0.70 and 0.50. Which threshold?
    Answer

    0.70: the lowest threshold that does not exceed capacity, giving 66% recall against 48% at 0.90. The 0.50 threshold produces 1,250 alerts, which exceeds capacity.

  7. 7Why was Macro-F1 ≈ 0.638 while Weighted-F1 ≈ 0.930 in the customer-service ticket example?
    Answer

    Weighted-F1 is dominated by the large classes (billing and network), so it looks healthy, whereas Macro-F1 gives every class the same weight and so exposes the weakness of the legal-threat class (0.400) and the safety-incident class (0.286).

  8. 8Why is WAPE preferred to MAPE when forecasting demand that has days with no sales?
    Answer

    MAPE is undefined when the actual value is zero and explodes near it, whereas WAPE = sum of absolute errors ÷ sum of actuals (85/500 = 17% in the example) is robust and easy to explain to the business.

  9. 9In a RAG system, which retriever metric do you fix first?
    Answer

    Recall@k: if the right passage never reaches the model, no prompt can save the answer. After that, precision and ranking (nDCG, MRR) are improved to reduce noise and tokens.

  10. 10An A/B test designed as 50/50 produced a 52/48 split. What do you do?
    Answer

    This is a sample ratio mismatch, which indicates a broken assignment mechanism; investigate and fix it before reading any result, because the comparison itself is no longer reliable.

Exam-Style Questions

Choose an answer to see the correct one and its explanation. These 10 questions are drawn from the 71 questions assigned to this unit in the exam simulator.

  1. 1Density-based clustering for irregular clustersHard

    A last-mile delivery company wants to discover natural drop-off zones from 2 million GPS delivery points in Jeddah to place parcel lockers. The points form dense, irregularly shaped clusters along streets and malls, there are many isolated one-off deliveries that should not create zones, and nobody knows how many zones exist. The zones will be recomputed every month, and the planning team will review each one on a map before committing to locker leases. Which approach is most appropriate?

    Explanation

    Three cues decide it: irregular shapes, outliers that must not form zones, and an unknown number of clusters. Density-based methods (DBSCAN/HDBSCAN) find arbitrarily shaped dense regions and mark sparse points as noise without fixing k. k-means needs k and assumes round clusters, so it splits elongated street clusters and forces every outlier into a zone; Gaussian mixtures assume elliptical components; t-SNE is for visualisation — its distances are not globally meaningful. Watch HDBSCAN's sensitivity to its density parameters.

  2. 2Peeking in A/B testsHard

    A bank's digital team runs an A/B test of a new credit-card offer page, planned for 28 days with a pre-computed sample size. The product manager checks the dashboard every morning; on day 5 the variant shows a 9% lift with p = 0.04, and she asks to stop the test and roll the variant out to all users today. Traffic is steady and the split is 50/50 as designed. What is the most appropriate response?

    Explanation

    Checking results daily and stopping the first time p < 0.05 is peeking: each look is another chance for noise to cross the line, so the real false-positive rate is far above the nominal 5%. The memo's fix is to fix the sample size in advance or use sequential testing methods designed for repeated looks. Day 5 also has not covered a full weekly cycle, so novelty effects are uncontrolled. A stricter ad-hoc threshold with continued peeking is still unprincipled, and running far past the planned size wastes traffic.

  3. 3Nested cross-validation for small datasetsHard

    A hospital research team has 640 labelled patient records for a readmission model. They ran 5-fold cross-validation over 300 hyper-parameter combinations, picked the best combination, and report its cross-validated AUC of 0.84 as the expected performance in a paper. A reviewer says the estimate is optimistic. Collecting more data would take a year. What is the most appropriate way to obtain an honest estimate?

    Explanation

    Choosing the best of 300 configurations on the same folds that produce the score rewards configurations that happened to fit those folds' noise, so the maximum is biased upwards. The memo's tool for small datasets where an unbiased estimate matters is nested cross-validation: the inner loop tunes, the outer loop estimates performance on data never used for selection, and all 640 records still contribute. A 64-patient holdout is unbiased but so small that its AUC has a very wide interval; averaging the maxima across seeds keeps the selection bias.

  4. 4Bias of impurity-based importanceHard

    A retailer's return-prediction random forest ranks 'order_id_hash' — a near-unique hashed identifier added by mistake — and 'postcode' (4,000 values) as its top two features by built-in impurity importance. Validation performance barely changes when either is removed. The compliance team needs a feature-importance section for the model documentation. An external auditor will review the documentation next month. What should the data scientist use?

    Explanation

    Impurity-based importance is biased towards high-cardinality features: near-unique values offer many split points that reduce training impurity even when they carry no generalisable signal — exactly why the hash ranks first while removing it changes nothing on validation. The memo calls permutation importance on a validation set the most honest generic measure, because it measures the real score drop on unseen data. The identifier should be removed as a defect, not merely documented. More trees stabilise impurity importance but do not remove its bias.

  5. 5Censored data in predictive maintenanceHard

    A logistics firm wants to predict when the gearboxes of its 1,500 delivery trucks will fail. In five years 140 gearboxes failed; the other trucks are still running, and many were bought only last year. The current approach trains a classifier only on the 140 failed trucks plus trucks retired before failure, dropping the 1,200 still-running trucks because 'their outcome is unknown'. Predictions badly overestimate how soon newer trucks will fail. What is the best change?

    Explanation

    Still-running trucks are not unknown — they are censored: we know they survived at least until today. Dropping them leaves a sample made only of trucks that failed or were retired, so the model learns that trucks fail early. The memo's domain pattern for predictive maintenance: few failures and censored data call for anomaly detection or survival models, measured by recall on failures, with alert-fatigue management. A survival model uses the partial information from all 1,500 trucks. Labelling them 'never fails' invents outcomes; SMOTE does not fix the biased sample.

  6. 6Rates hide volumesHard

    A payments processor plans to launch a fraud model on 2 million transactions a day. At the proposed threshold the model's false-positive rate is 0.5% and its recall 85%; fraud is 0.1% of transactions. The fraud team, which can review 3,000 alerts a day, signs off because 'a 0.5% false-positive rate is tiny'. What is the most important issue to raise before launch?

    Explanation

    Convert rates to daily volumes. Frauds: 0.1% × 2,000,000 = 2,000; caught: 85% = 1,700. Legitimate: 1,998,000; false alerts: 0.5% ≈ 9,990. Total ≈ 11,690 alerts a day, almost four times the 3,000 capacity, with precision ≈ 1,700 / 11,690 ≈ 15%. The memo: rates hide volumes, and capacity questions are about volumes. Choose the operating threshold that fits capacity and minimises expected cost, or rank alerts by score. Lowering the threshold makes the overload worse; accuracy is meaningless at 0.1% prevalence.

  7. 7A representative evaluation setHard

    A government Arabic assistant was evaluated on 1,500 typed Modern Standard Arabic questions collected from the web portal in 2024, where it scores 92% answer correctness. Since a mobile voice channel launched, 55% of traffic arrives as transcribed Gulf-dialect speech, often mixed with English terms, and complaints have tripled. The release board relies on the evaluation set to approve every model update. What should the team do first?

    Explanation

    The memo's first property of an evaluation set: representative — it must match the production population in channels, regions, dialects, devices and time, and be refreshed as the population changes. The current set describes 2024's typed MSA traffic, so its 92% says little about the majority voice/dialect channel, and every approval based on it is blind to the complaints. Rebuild it with anonymised real queries from each channel, including dialect and code-switched variants, and report overall plus per-slice results. Fixing the model before the measuring stick leaves the release gate blind.

  8. 8Reproducible training dataHard

    After a customer complaint, auditors ask a bank exactly which data trained the credit model deployed last quarter. Training read from a warehouse table that is overwritten daily, and no snapshot or hash was recorded. In the same week, a customer exercises the PDPL right to deletion and asks whether their data was used in training. What should the team put in place for every future training run?

    Explanation

    The memo: every training run should reference an immutable data snapshot (table version, file hashes) plus the code commit and parameters; 'we cannot reproduce last quarter's model' is a governance failure, because auditors, incident investigations and PDPL deletion requests all depend on knowing exactly what data a model saw. A lakehouse version or hashed snapshot recorded in the model registry answers both questions. Indefinite full copies create cost and conflict with PDPL retention limits; an approximate date range is not exact enough for audit or deletion.

  9. 9Position bias and feedback loopsHard

    A news app retrains its article ranker every day on clicks, logging only the articles that were clicked, not what was shown or where. Over six months the top slot's share of clicks has risen steadily, the variety of sources shown has fallen, and newly added publishers almost never surface. Offline nDCG on click logs keeps improving. What is the most appropriate fix?

    Explanation

    Users can only click what was shown and click higher positions more, so training on clicks alone reinforces whatever the current ranker already shows — a feedback loop that rising offline nDCG on the same biased logs cannot detect. The memo's remedies: log impressions with positions, use counterfactual or position-debiased training, reserve a small share of traffic for exploration (which also gives new publishers exposure), and validate with A/B tests on business outcomes such as retention and satisfaction, not just click-through.

  10. 10Hierarchical forecast reconciliationHard

    A grocery chain produces store-level SKU forecasts for replenishment and, separately, region-level forecasts that the distribution centre uses for supplier purchasing. Summed store forecasts differ from the region forecast by about 9% in some weeks, so the distribution centre is over-stocked one week and short the next, and the two teams argue about whose numbers are right. What is the best fix?

    Explanation

    The memo: forecasts at product → store → region must add up; reconcile them with bottom-up, top-down or optimal reconciliation, and monitor error by tier. Reconciliation produces one coherent set of numbers, so the distribution centre buys what the stores will order. Running two inconsistent forecasts is the cause of the swings; averaging the totals leaves the store-level numbers inconsistent with the new total; adding a feature does not force the sums to match.
