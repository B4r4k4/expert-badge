Unit Introduction

The exam devotes roughly 25 of its 150 questions to this domain. It does not ask you to derive attention or write training code; instead it puts you in a situation and asks: which technique fits it? A prompt, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) or fine-tuning? Which model size and which numerical precision? Which generation settings? Why does Arabic cost more tokens? How is hallucination reduced? This unit builds the mechanisms behind those decisions: how a network learns, how the transformer works, how large language models are built, and then how they are adapted, served and evaluated. The theory here is not an end in itself; it is the “why” that makes one answer correct and another a distractor.

Before You Start

This unit opens the “box” from the inside: how neural networks learn, what the transformer is that language models are built on, how these models are made, and how we choose between the ways of adapting them. It is the first of the two units of Domain 2 (about 25 questions out of 150).

It has more mathematics than the others, but the exam does not ask for derivations. It asks you to understand why and to calculate how much: how much memory does a model need? Why does it slow down with long context? Why does quantisation speed up generation? All the calculations are simple multiplication, and we will walk through them step by step.

Before it, you need from Unit 00: Sections 2.4 and 2.5 (how a model learns, and neural networks), and Chapter 4 (language models, tokens, prompts, hallucination, vector representations and RAG).

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

  • Diagnose common training symptoms (NaN loss, flat loss, overfitting, out-of-memory) and link each symptom to its remedy.
  • Calculate weight memory by numerical precision, training memory with AdamW, and KV cache memory for a given number of users.
  • Explain the attention mechanism, the MHA, GQA and MQA variants, positional information and mixture of experts, and their effect on serving cost.
  • Distinguish the stages of building a language model: pre-training, supervised fine-tuning, preference alignment (RLHF and DPO), and reasoning models.
  • Choose between prompting, RAG, fine-tuning and distillation, and choose the tuning method (full, LoRA, QLoRA, DPO) to suit the situation.
  • Set generation parameters to suit the task, and identify the cause of hallucination and its appropriate remedy.
  • Design an evaluation for a generative system that covers faithfulness to sources, retrieval quality, Arabic, safety and cost.

Chapter 1

How Neural Networks Learn and How to Diagnose Them

1.1 The Training Loop in Four Steps

In Plain Terms

In Unit 00 you saw that learning is “guess, then measure the error, then adjust”. Here are the formal names of the four steps: the forward pass (the model guesses), the loss (we measure the error), backpropagation (we work out each weight's responsibility for the error), and the optimiser step (we adjust the weights a little).

Why do we need a non-linear activation function? Because multiplying and adding numbers again and again is still, in the end, simple multiplication and addition, that is, a straight line however many layers there are. A non-linear function lets the network learn complex, curved shapes.

Real-World Example

A football coach is teaching a player to shoot. Forward: the player shoots. Loss: the ball went a metre to the right of the goal. Backpropagation: the coach analyses: how much of the error was due to the angle of the foot, how much to the body position, and how much to the power of the strike. Optimiser: adjusts each of these a little. Then the player shoots again. The player going through all of the day's drills once is called an epoch.

Key Terms
Activation function (ReLU, GELU, SiLU)دالة التنشيط
A simple non-linear function applied to the output of every neuron. ReLU, for example, turns every negative number into zero and leaves positive ones as they are.
Forward passالتمرير الأمامي
Feeding the data into the network and computing layer after layer until we get the prediction.
Backpropagationالانتشار العكسي
Calculating how much each weight contributed to the error, working back from the output to the input layer by layer.
Gradientالتدرّج
A number for each weight that tells us: if we increase this weight slightly, does the error go up or down, and by how much? We move the weight in the direction that reduces the error.
Optimiserالمُحسِّن
The algorithm that decides how the weights are adjusted using the gradients, such as SGD and Adam.
Epochالحقبة
One pass of the model over the whole training set. Training usually takes several epochs.
Mini-batchالدفعة الصغيرة
A small group of examples (32 or 256, for instance) used together in a single adjustment step, instead of one example or all the data.

What the Exam Tests

A neural network is a sequence of layers, each of which computes a weighted sum of its inputs and then passes it through a non-linear activation function such as ReLU, GELU or SiLU. Without the non-linearity, all the layers would collapse into a single linear transformation.

  1. Forward pass: compute predictions from the inputs.
  2. Loss: measure the error between the prediction and the correct value.
  3. Backpropagation: apply the chain rule to compute the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight.
  4. Optimiser step: move every weight a small amount in the direction opposite to its gradient.

One pass over the whole training set is called an epoch, and each optimiser step uses a mini-batch. Understanding this mechanism is what lets you diagnose training scenarios: a loss that explodes, a loss that does not move, a model that memorises the data, and a run that runs out of memory.

1.2 Loss Functions: Which for Which Task

In Plain Terms

The loss function is the way we “penalise” the model for its error. Each type of task has a suitable function: for numbers we measure how far the prediction is from the correct number, and for classification we measure how confident the model is in the correct answer.

In language models the loss is: how surprised was the model by the real next word? We convert it into an easier number called perplexity. Lower perplexity means a model that is better at predicting the next word, but it does not necessarily mean it is more useful to the user.

Real-World Example

Perplexity is like “the number of options the model is torn between”. A perplexity of 10 means roughly that at every word the model hesitates as if it had 10 equally likely words in front of it. A perplexity of 3 means it is far more confident. But a model that is very confident at completing internet text may be poor at following your instructions.

Key Terms
Cross-entropy / log lossالإنتروبيا المتقاطعة
A classification loss function that penalises the model more the lower the probability it gives to the correct answer.
Softmaxsoftmax
A function that turns a set of numbers into positive probabilities that sum to 1. It is used at the end of the network to choose a class or a word.
Perplexityالحيرة
A metric for language models equal to exp(loss). Lower is better at predicting the next word, but it does not measure real usefulness.
Focal lossFocal loss
A loss function that reduces the weight of easy examples and focuses on hard ones. Useful when the class you are looking for is rare.
Contrastive loss (InfoNCE)الخسارة التباينية
A function that trains embedding models: it pulls similar pairs (a question and its answer) together and pushes dissimilar ones apart.
Label smoothingتنعيم التسميات
Instead of telling the model “the answer is 100% correct”, we tell it “95%”, so it does not become over-confident.

1.3 Optimisers and the Learning Rate

In Plain Terms

The optimiser is the “driver” of the adjustment. The simplest is SGD: a step opposite to the direction of the error. Momentum makes it like a rolling ball, which gains speed and does not stop at every small bump. Adam is smarter: it gives each weight its own step size. It is the common choice with transformers (in the AdamW version).

But Adam needs to remember two extra numbers for every weight, which is why training needs far more memory than use. The most important setting in all of training is the learning rate: the size of the step.

Real-World Example

You are going down a mountain in fog. A learning rate that is too large: you take huge leaps, overshoot the valley and climb the opposite mountain, and this is what makes the loss oscillate or explode. Too small: you walk in ant steps and never arrive. Warm-up: you start with small steps until you get used to the ground, then lengthen them. Gradient clipping: if you see a sudden steep slope, you do not leap into it; you set a maximum step.

Key Terms
Stochastic Gradient Descent / momentumSGD والزخم
SGD: adjusting the weights opposite to the direction of the gradient computed on a mini-batch. Momentum adds a “memory” of the previous movement, so the adjustment becomes smoother.
Adam optimiserAdam / AdamW
An optimiser that adapts the step size for each weight based on the history of its gradients. AdamW is a version that decouples weight decay, and it is the default for transformers.
Weight decayتضاؤل الأوزان
Shrinking the weights slightly at every step so that they do not grow too large. A kind of regularisation against overfitting.
Not a NumberNaN
A non-numeric value that appears when the computation blows up (division by zero or an extremely large number). Seeing it in the loss means training has collapsed.
Warm-upالإحماء
Starting training with a very small learning rate and then raising it gradually over the first steps to avoid instability.
Gradient clippingقص التدرّج
Putting a ceiling on the size of the gradient, so a single bad batch cannot push the weights with a huge shove and ruin training.
Gradient accumulationتراكم التدرّج
Summing the gradients of several small batches and then adjusting once, so we simulate a large batch without needing large memory.

What the Exam Tests

SGD with momentum accumulates a “velocity” that keeps updates moving across flat or noisy regions, and it generalises well in vision models. Adam adapts the step size independently for each parameter from moving averages of the gradients and their squares, and AdamW decouples weight decay from the adaptive update; it is the default choice for transformers. Adam-family optimisers store two extra values per parameter, which is a major reason why training needs far more memory than inference.

The learning rate is the most important hyperparameter: too high makes the loss oscillate or become NaN, and too low makes training crawl or stall.

  • Warm-up: increase the rate linearly over the first steps to avoid instability while the optimiser statistics settle, followed by cosine or linear decay.
  • Batch size: larger batches usually allow proportionally larger rates, up to a limit.
  • Gradient clipping: limits the gradient norm and stops a single bad batch from blowing up training.
  • Gradient accumulation: sums gradients over several mini-batches before the step, simulating a large batch when memory is limited.

1.4 Normalisation, Regularisation and Initialisation

In Plain Terms

This table lists “stability tools” that make deep networks trainable. Three of them matter most: normalisation keeps the numbers inside the network within a reasonable range. Residual connections open a “shortcut” along which the signal and the gradient pass through many layers without weakening. Regularisation (dropout and weight decay) prevents memorisation.

Why do we need residual connections? Because the signal in a network of 100 layers weakens or swells layer after layer, just as a message distorts when 100 people pass it on by whispering. The shortcut carries the original message alongside the modifications.

Real-World Example

Dropout: a work team from which random people are absent every day. Each member learns to know a little of the others' work, so the whole team does not depend on one person. This makes the network more robust. A residual connection: each layer adds an “adjustment” to what reached it instead of rewriting it from scratch.

Key Terms
Batch normalisationتطبيع الدفعة
Rescaling a layer's outputs to have a fixed mean and spread, computed across the examples of the batch. Common in image networks.
Layer normalisation / RMSNormتطبيع الطبقة / RMSNorm
Like batch normalisation but computed for each token separately, so it does not depend on batch size. It is the one used in transformers.
Residual connectionالوصلة المتبقية
Adding a layer's input directly to its output, so the layer learns an “adjustment” to what reached it. It makes it possible to train very deep networks.
Co-adaptationالتكيّف المشترك
Neurons depending on each other so heavily that they only work together. Dropout breaks this.
Initialisation (He / Xavier)التهيئة
Choosing the initial values of the weights before training in a considered way, so the signal does not vanish or explode from the start.
Back-translationالترجمة العكسية
Augmenting text data by translating a sentence into another language and back, giving a new wording with the same meaning.

1.5 Numerical Precision and Training Memory

In Plain Terms

Every weight in the model is a number, and a number needs space in memory. FP32 uses 4 bytes per number, and FP16/BF16 use 2 bytes, that is, half the memory and more speed. The difference between FP16 and BF16: FP16 cannot represent very small numbers and so needs a trick, whereas BF16 covers a wider range, which is why it became the default.

Now the most important calculation: training needs not only the weights, but also the gradients, a precise copy of the weights, and the two numbers Adam remembers for every weight. The total is about 16 bytes per parameter, whereas use in FP16 needs only 2 bytes. That is, training needs eight times the memory of use.

Real-World Example

The calculation for a 7-billion-parameter model. For training: 2 (weights) + 2 (gradients) + 4 (precise copy) + 8 (Adam's two numbers) = 16 bytes per parameter. So 7 billion × 16 = 112 billion bytes ≈ 112 GB, and that is before counting anything else. The largest common GPU has 80 GB, so one machine is not enough for full training. For use: 7 billion × 2 = 14 GB, so a single machine is easily enough.

Key Terms
Floating-point formatsFP32 / FP16 / BF16 / FP8
Formats for storing decimal numbers at different sizes: 4, 2, 2 and 1 bytes. The smaller is faster, lighter and less precise.
Tensor coresأنوية الموترات
Special parts in modern GPUs designed to multiply matrices very fast, especially at low precision.
Loss scalingتحجيم الخسارة
Multiplying the loss by a large number before computing gradients in FP16, so that very small gradients do not become zeros, and then dividing afterwards.
Mixed precisionالدقة المختلطة
Running the computations at low precision (BF16) for speed, while keeping a master copy of the weights in FP32 to preserve accuracy.
Activationsالتنشيطات
The intermediate results each layer computes during the forward pass. They are stored for backpropagation, so they need extra memory that grows with batch size and text length.
Sharded trainingZeRO / FSDP
Methods that distribute the weights, gradients and optimiser states across several GPUs instead of copying them in full onto each one. You will see them in Unit 08.

What the Exam Tests

FP32 is the reference. FP16 halves memory and speeds up tensor cores, but its range is narrow, so training needs loss scaling so that small gradients do not underflow to zero. BF16 keeps the same exponent range as FP32 with fewer mantissa bits, so it needs no scaling and is the default on modern GPUs. FP8 (on the newest GPUs) speeds up training and inference further, with careful scaling. Mixed precision keeps a master copy of the weights in FP32 while the computations run in BF16/FP16.

Training memory per parameter (AdamW, mixed precision)

2 (BF16 weights) + 2 (gradients) + 4 (FP32 master weights) + 8 (two Adam moments) ≈ 16 bytes/parameter

7B × 16 bytes ≈ 112 GB (model + optimiser states only, before activations)

This is why full fine-tuning of a 7-billion-parameter model needs several GPUs or state sharding (ZeRO / FSDP), whereas LoRA / QLoRA fits on a single GPU. Note that this figure is before accounting for activations.

Training ≈ 16 bytes per parameter; serving in FP16 ≈ 2 bytes per parameter. The difference is the optimiser, the gradients and the master copy.

1.6 Diagnosing Training Runs

In Plain Terms

This is the “training doctor” table: for each symptom, its cause and remedy. The two most important symptoms: the loss explodes (becomes NaN), usually because of a step that is far too large; and the loss never moves, usually because of a fault in the data or the setup.

The smartest test for this second symptom is the sanity test: give the model only 10 examples and train it on them. Any healthy model can memorise 10 examples. If it cannot, the problem is not that it is “weak”, but in the data or the code.

Real-World Example

A student whose grades never improve. Before you say “not intelligent”, ask them to memorise just 5 definitions. If they cannot, perhaps the book they have is for a different subject, that is, the fault is in the “data”. Another symptom: a GPU used at only 20%. The worker is fast but is waiting for materials, and the problem is in delivering the data to it, not in its speed.

Key Terms
Overflowفيض الأرقام
When a number becomes larger than the maximum the format can represent, so it turns into infinity or NaN. Common with FP16.
Frozen layersالطبقات المجمّدة
Layers whose weights we have prevented from being adjusted during training. Useful in transfer learning, but freezing them by mistake prevents learning.
Dead activationsالتنشيطات الميتة
Neurons whose output has become permanently zero (with ReLU, for example), so they learn nothing afterwards.
Data loaderمحمّل البيانات
The part of the code that reads the data, prepares it in batches and sends it to the model. If it is slow, the GPU waits for it.
Gradient checkpointingنقاط تفتيش التدرّج
Saving memory by not storing all the activations, and recomputing some of them when needed. Less memory in exchange for slightly more time.
Prefetchingالجلب المسبق
Preparing the next batch of data while the model works on the current one, so it does not wait.

Architectures: From Convolution to the Transformer

2.1 Convolutional Networks and Vision Transformers

In Plain Terms

The convolutional network (CNN) is designed for images. It slides small “lenses” over the image, each lens looking for a simple pattern: an edge, a corner or a colour. The first layers see simple things, and the deeper ones assemble them into shapes, then parts (an eye, a wheel), then whole objects.

The vision transformer (ViT) is a newer approach: it cuts the image into small squares and treats each square as if it were a “word”, then applies to them the same transformer used in language. The most important practical lesson: do not train from scratch. Start from a model trained on millions of images, and adapt it to your few images.

Real-World Example

A factory wants to detect defects in its products, and has only 2,000 images. Training from scratch will fail, because 2,000 images are not enough to learn “what an edge is and what a shape is”. But a model that has learned from millions of images already knows that. We take it and change its last layer to say “sound/defective”, train it on our images, and add images that are flipped and lit differently (data augmentation).

Key Terms
Convolutional Neural Network — CNNالشبكة الالتفافية
A neural network for images that slides small filters over the image to detect patterns, using the same weights at every position in the image.
Filter / kernelالمرشّح
A small matrix of weights (3×3, for example) slid over the image to detect a particular pattern.
Poolingالتجميع
Shrinking the intermediate image by taking the maximum or the average in each small region, so computation falls and the model tolerates small shifts.
Vision Transformer — ViTمحوّل الرؤية
A model that splits the image into small patches and processes them as a sequence of tokens with the attention mechanism, like text.
Contrastive Language–Image Pre-trainingCLIP
A model that learned to place images and their descriptive texts in a single vector space, so it can search for an image with a text description.
MobileNetMobileNet
A family of lightweight image networks designed to run on phones and small devices.

What the Exam Tests

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) slide small learned filters over the image, sharing weights across positions. Early layers detect edges and textures, deeper ones detect parts and objects, and pooling or strides reduce spatial resolution. This built-in locality and translation invariance make them data-efficient.

  • ResNet: residual connections make very deep networks possible.
  • EfficientNet: balanced scaling of depth, width and resolution.
  • MobileNet: depthwise-separable convolutions for phones.

Vision transformers (ViT) split the image into patches, embed each patch as a token, and then apply transformer attention. With large pre-training data they match or outperform CNNs, and they unify vision with language models: CLIP aligns image and text embeddings, and vision-language models pass image tokens to a language model.

Transfer learning is the default

Start from a pre-trained backbone, replace the head, tune the head first, and then optionally unfreeze the deeper layers with a smaller learning rate.

The scenario “we have only 2,000 labelled images” points to transfer learning with data augmentation, not to training from scratch.

2.2 Sequence Models Before and After Transformers

In Plain Terms

Before transformers, text was processed with recurrent networks (RNNs): they read the sentence word after word, and carry a small “memory” that they update with every word. The first problem: slowness, because the second word waits for the first. The second: forgetting, so by the end of a long paragraph the memory of its beginning has weakened.

The transformer solved both problems: it looks at all the words at the same time, and every word can “look” directly at any other word however far away. But it has a price: the longer the text, the far more comparisons there are.

Real-World Example

Reading a novel. The recurrent network is like a reader who reads page by page and keeps only a short summary in mind, so by the tenth chapter they have forgotten the name of a character from the first. The transformer is like a reader who spreads all the pages in front of them on a huge table and can look at any page at any moment. But the table grows enormously the longer the novel.

Key Terms
Recurrent Neural Network — RNNالشبكة المتكررة
A network that processes a sequence element by element, carrying a hidden state that passes information from one step to the next.
Gated recurrent unitsLSTM / GRU
Improved types of recurrent networks that have “gates” that decide what is kept and what is forgotten, so they remember over a longer span.
Hidden stateالحالة المخفية
A vector of numbers that carries a summary of what the recurrent network has seen so far.
State-space models (Mamba)نماذج فضاء الحالة
A newer family that processes a sequence in time proportional to its length, so it is more efficient on very long texts, with a relative weakness in exact recall.

What the Exam Tests

Recurrent networks (RNN, LSTM, GRU) read the sequence step by step, carrying a hidden state. LSTM and GRU add gates that control what is remembered and what is forgotten, easing the vanishing-gradient problem. Their weaknesses: no parallelism across time steps during training (slow on GPUs), and difficulty with very long dependencies. They remain useful, however, for small streaming problems on constrained devices.

Transformers replaced them in language because attention links any two positions directly and processes all positions in parallel during training. The price: the cost of attention grows with sequence length, which is why long-context models need efficient attention kernels, and also why the KV cache dominates serving.

State-space models such as Mamba, and hybrid models, are a newer family that processes sequences in linear time and appears in some long-context and efficient models. For the exam it is enough to know that they trade some in-context accuracy (such as exact recall) for efficiency on very long inputs.

2.3 Scaled Dot-Product Attention and the Transformer Block

In Plain Terms

Attention is the heart of the transformer. Its idea: to understand a word, look at the other words and decide which of them matter to it. In the sentence “Ahmed drank the tea because he was thirsty”, for the model to understand the “he” (in Arabic, the suffix ـه attached to “because”, لأنه), it must attend to “Ahmed”, not to “the tea”.

How does this happen mathematically? Each word makes three things: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I offer?), and a value (what is my information?). We compare the word's query with the keys of the other words, which tells us their degree of relevance, and then we take from their values in proportion to these scores. Dividing by √d_k stops the scores from growing so large that a single word dominates all the attention.

Real-World Example

A library. You walk in with a query: “I want a book about Saudi cooking”. Each book has a key, which is its title and classification. You compare your query with the titles and give each book a relevance score: 0.7 for the book “Najdi Cuisine”, 0.25 for “Gulf Dishes”, and 0.05 for “The History of Riyadh”. Then you take from the value (content) of each book in proportion to its score. This is what every word does with all the other words.

Key Terms
Attentionالانتباه
A mechanism that computes, for each word, how relevant it is to every other word, and then builds its representation from the other words according to that relevance.
Query / Key / Valueالاستعلام والمفتاح والقيمة
Three vectors that each word makes: the query for what it is looking for, the key for what it offers, and the value for the information it carries.
Causal maskالقناع السببي
Preventing each word from looking at the words after it during training, because the model is learning to predict the next word and must not see the answer.
Multi-head attentionالانتباه متعدد الرؤوس
Running several attention operations in parallel, each “head” specialising in a type of relationship (syntactic, coreference, position), and then merging their results.
Feed-forward layerالطبقة الأمامية
A small network in every transformer block that processes each word separately after attention, and carries a large part of the model's knowledge.
Encoder / decoderالمشفِّر والمفكِّك
The encoder reads the whole text and understands it (for classification and embeddings). The decoder generates text word after word (for conversation and writing).
Quadraticتربيعي
Growth proportional to the square of the length. A text twice as long needs 4 times as many comparisons in standard attention.

What the Exam Tests

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q·Kᵀ / √d_k ) · V

Each token is projected into three vectors: a query representing what it is looking for, a key representing what it offers, and a value representing the information it carries. Scores are computed between the query and every key and then divided by √d_k; without this scaling, dot-product outputs grow with the dimension, so softmax saturates towards a one-hot value and gradients vanish. The softmax weights then mix the values.

In GPT-style decoder models, the causal mask prevents every position from attending to later positions, so the model trains on predicting the next token for all positions at once.

Multi-head attention runs several attention operations in parallel on lower-dimensional projections, so the heads specialise (syntax, coreference, position), and then the outputs are concatenated and projected again.

Block: attention → residual + norm → feed-forward (2 linear layers, ~4× wider; SwiGLU in modern LLMs) → residual + norm

2.4 MHA, GQA, MQA and the KV Cache

In Plain Terms

When the model generates an answer, it writes it word after word, and each new word needs to look at everything before it. Instead of recomputing the “keys and values” for all the previous words with every new word, we keep them in memory. This is the KV cache.

The problem is that it grows with every word and with every user. With long conversations and many users it becomes bigger than the model itself. That is why researchers invented GQA: instead of each “attention head” having its own keys and values, a group of heads shares one set of keys and values, so memory shrinks a lot.

Real-World Example

The calculation step by step for an 8-billion-parameter model. For each word we store: keys and values (×2), across 32 layers (×32), for 8 shared heads (×8), each head 128 numbers (×128), and each number 2 bytes (×2). The result: 2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 131,072 bytes ≈ 128 KB per word. A conversation of 8,192 tokens: 8,192 × 128 KB ≈ 1 GB. 30 users together = 30 GB, whereas the model's own weights are only 16 GB!

Key Terms
KV cacheذاكرة KV
Storing the keys and values computed for the previous words during generation, to avoid recomputing them with every new word. It grows with conversation length and the number of users.
Attention head-sharing variantsMHA / GQA / MQA
MHA: each head has its own keys and values. GQA: groups of heads share. MQA: all heads share a single pair. The more sharing, the smaller the memory, with a slight loss in quality.
PagedAttentionالانتباه المقسّم إلى صفحات
A technique that manages the KV cache in small pieces like the pages of a book instead of reserving a large contiguous block for each user, so memory is not wasted.
KV-cache quantisationتكميم ذاكرة KV
Storing the keys and values at lower precision (1 byte instead of 2, for example) to shrink memory and serve more users.

What the Exam Tests

The KV cache stores the previous keys and values so they are not recomputed when each new token is generated. It grows linearly with context length × batch size, and it is often what limits the number of concurrent users a GPU can serve. The number of key and value heads determines its size.

This is why GQA, KV cache quantisation, paged attention (PagedAttention) and context limits are what determine how many users a GPU serves.

GPU sizing is not computed from the weights alone: weights + KV cache (context × users) + activations and runtime overhead.

2.5 Positional Information and Context Extension

In Plain Terms

Attention alone does not know the order of words. “The boy ate the apple” and “The apple ate the boy” contain nearly the same words. So we add “position” information to every word, and the common modern method is RoPE: it rotates each word's vector by an angle that depends on its position.

context extension: a model trained on texts of 8,000 tokens can be adjusted to accept 128,000. But watch out for the lost in the middle phenomenon: the model remembers the beginning and end of a text better than its middle. That is why retrieving only the important passages (RAG) is often better and cheaper than throwing everything into a huge context.

Real-World Example

You read a 300-page report in one sitting. You will remember the introduction and the conclusion well, but an important detail on page 150 may escape you. But if someone gave you only the three pages relevant to your question, you would answer more accurately and in less time.

Techniques that make a model trained on short texts work on much longer ones, by re-tuning the positional information and a short period of training.
Lost in the middleالضياع في المنتصف
The tendency of models to use information at the beginning and end of a long context better than the middle.

2.6 Efficient Attention and Mixture of Experts

In Plain Terms

FlashAttention computes exactly the same attention, with no approximation, but is clever in its use of memory: it works on small pieces that stay in the small, fast memory inside the chip, instead of moving huge tables back and forth to the large, slow memory. The result: much faster, with the same output.

mixture of experts (MoE): instead of one huge layer that works in full for every word, there are several “experts” and a router that sends each word to only two experts. The computation is small, but all the experts must be in memory, because you do not know in advance which expert the next word will need.

Real-World Example

FlashAttention: a cook preparing a dish. The slow way: going to the distant storeroom for each ingredient separately. The clever way: bringing enough for a whole step to the table, finishing it, then bringing the next. The same dish, in less time. MoE: a hospital with 8 specialist doctors, where each patient is seen by only two. The work per patient is small, but the salaries are for eight doctors.

Key Terms
FlashAttentionFlashAttention
A way of computing the same attention at full accuracy while reducing data movement in memory, so it becomes faster and uses less memory.
On-chip vs device memorySRAM وHBM
SRAM is a very small, very fast memory inside the GPU chip. HBM is the GPU's large memory (80 GB, for example), which is relatively slower.
Sliding-window attentionانتباه النافذة المنزلقة
Each word attends only to a fixed number of words before it, not to the whole text. Lower cost in exchange for seeing less.
Mixture of Experts — MoEخليط الخبراء
An architecture with several “experts” and a router that sends each token to a few of them. Little computation, and large memory because all the experts are loaded.
Active parametersالمعاملات النشطة
The parameters actually used for each token in an MoE model, which are a fraction of the total parameters.

What the Exam Tests

FlashAttention computes the exact same attention in tiles that stay in the fast on-chip SRAM, avoiding writing the full n × n score matrix to the slower GPU memory (HBM). It does not change the result; it reduces memory traffic and speeds up training and long-context inference considerably. Sparse attention and sliding-window attention (used by Mistral), by contrast, trade full accuracy for linear cost.

Mixture of Experts (MoE): the feed-forward layer is replaced by several “experts” and a router that sends each token to a small number of them (for example 2 of 8). The total parameters are large, but the active parameters per token, and therefore the compute, are far fewer.

MoE is cheap in compute but heavy in memory when serving: memory must hold all the experts, not just the active ones.

2.7 Rules of Thumb for Compute

In Plain Terms

Four rules of thumb for computation. Training needs about 6 × the number of parameters × the number of training tokens operations. Use needs about 2 × the number of parameters per generated token. The Chinchilla rule: the compute-optimal amount is about 20 training tokens per parameter.

The most important rule in practice: generating words is limited by the speed of reading memory, not by the speed of computation. With every word, the whole model must be read from memory. That is why shrinking the model by quantisation speeds up generation, rather than just saving memory.

Real-World Example

A GPU chip reads 2,000 GB per second from its memory. A model of 8 GB: for each word it must be read in full, so the maximum is about 2,000 ÷ 8 = 250 words per second for one user. If we quantised it to 4 GB: 2,000 ÷ 4 = 500 words per second. Quantisation doubled the speed. There is a difference between two phases: reading your whole question in one go (prefill, which is compute-bound) and then writing the answer word by word (decode, which is memory-bound).

The number of arithmetic operations (multiplication and addition on decimal numbers). Used to measure the size of the computational work.
Chinchilla scalingقاعدة Chinchilla
A research result saying that the compute-optimal amount for training a model is about 20 training tokens per parameter. Many modern models deliberately exceed it so they are smaller and cheaper to use.
Memory bandwidthعرض نطاق الذاكرة
The amount of data a GPU can read from its memory per second. It determines generation speed.
Prefill vs decodeالتعبئة والتوليد
Prefill: processing the whole question text in one go, which determines time to first token. Decode: writing the answer token after token, which determines how fast the answer appears.

How Language Models Are Built and How They Represent Text

3.1 The Stages of Building a Large Language Model

In Plain Terms

The language model you talk to goes through several stages. Pre-training: it reads trillions of words and learns to predict the next word, producing a “base model” that knows a lot but only continues texts and does not follow commands. Then instruction tuning: it trains on “question → good answer” examples and learns to be an assistant. Then preference alignment: humans compare two answers and choose the better one, so the model learns what people prefer. Then safety testing.

The two best-known alignment methods are: RLHF (we build a model that imitates human ratings, then train the language model to please it; this is powerful but complex), and DPO (we learn directly from pairs of “this is better than that”; this is simpler).

Real-World Example

Write to a base model: “What is the capital of France?”. It may continue: “What is the capital of Germany? What is the capital of Italy?”, because it has seen lists of questions like that on the internet. The instruction-tuned model, by contrast, answers: “Paris”. That is why you do not build an assistant on a base model.

Key Terms
Pre-trainingالتدريب المسبق
The first and most expensive stage: training the model on a huge amount of general text so it learns language and knowledge.
Base modelنموذج الأساس
The product of pre-training. It knows a lot but continues texts and does not follow instructions.
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) / instruction tuningالضبط بالتعليمات
Training the model on examples of an instruction and its good answer, so it learns to follow commands and the assistant style.
Reinforcement Learning from Human FeedbackRLHF
Training a reward model on human preferences, and then optimising the language model with reinforcement learning to maximise that reward.
Direct Preference OptimisationDPO
Learning preferences directly from pairs of “preferred answer / rejected answer” with no separate reward model. Simpler and more stable.
Constitutional AI / RL from AI Feedbackالنهج الدستوري / RLAIF
An AI model that evaluates answers according to written principles instead of humans evaluating every example.
Reasoning modelsنماذج الاستدلال
Models trained to “think” through longer internal steps before answering. More accurate on complex problems, and slower and more expensive.

What the Exam Tests

  1. Pre-training: the model learns to predict the next token over trillions of tokens of web text, books, code and curated sources. Data work dominates: deduplication, quality filtering, removing personal data and abusive content, and balancing languages. Arabic is a small share of web data, which is why Arabic models curate their data and up-weight Arabic. The output is a base model: broad in knowledge, but it continues text rather than following instructions.
  2. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT / instruction tuning): training on curated pairs (instruction → good answer) teaches the format of useful dialogue, tool calling and refusal. Thousands to hundreds of thousands of high-quality examples matter more than millions of noisy ones.
  3. Preference alignment: humans (or a strong model) compare two answers.
  4. Safety tuning and red-teaming: adversarial testing for harmful content, jailbreak attempts and bias, with targeted data to fix the failures.
  5. Reasoning models: models trained (often with reinforcement learning on verifiable tasks) to produce longer internal reasoning before answering.

3.2 Reasoning Models and What Building Means for the Engineer

In Plain Terms

Reasoning models are more accurate on complex problems because they “think” longer, but thinking means more words, that is, more time and cost. The rule: send them only the hard questions.

The second lesson: as an engineer you rarely train a model from scratch. You choose a ready-made model and then adapt it in order from cheapest to most expensive: a good prompt first, then RAG, then LoRA tuning, then preference tuning. Do not forget the licence terms, the hosting location, and support for Arabic and its dialects.

Real-World Example

A company builds an assistant for its employees. “What is my leave balance?” is a simple question that a fast model can handle. But “Analyse this contract and compare its clauses with our internal policy” may deserve a reasoning model. Sending every question to the reasoning model is like calling in a senior consultant to answer every question about lunchtime.

Key Terms
Checkpointنقطة الحفظ
A saved copy of the model's weights at a particular stage. A “base checkpoint” means the model's weights before instruction tuning.
Open weightsالأوزان المفتوحة
A model whose weights are published so it can be downloaded and run on your own machines, as opposed to a model available only through a provider's API.
Licenceالترخيص
The terms of use of the model: is commercial use allowed? Are there restrictions on the number of users or on domains?

What the Exam Tests

Reasoning models improve accuracy on multi-step problems, but at the cost of far more output tokens, that is, higher latency and cost per request. The rule: route only the hard queries to them.

You rarely train a model from scratch. You choose a base model or an instruction-tuned model, and then adapt it in ascending order of cost and risk:

prompting → RAG → SFT via LoRA → preference tuning

Licence terms (open weights or API only, restrictions on commercial use) and hosting location are part of model selection, as is its coverage of Arabic and its dialects.

A base model completes text; an instruction-tuned model follows commands. Do not build an assistant on a base checkpoint.

3.3 Tokenizers

In Plain Terms

You met tokens in Unit 00. Here are the details that matter for the exam. The most important is fertility: how many tokens a single word needs. Tokenizers designed for English cut Arabic words into more pieces, because of prefixes and suffixes (for example the single word وبمستشفياتهم (“and in their hospitals”)) and diacritics. The result: Arabic text costs more, runs slower, and takes up more space in the context window.

An important rule: the tokenizer is an inseparable part of the model. It cannot be swapped. When comparing two models, do not compare only the “price per token”, but the actual cost for your texts after tokenising them with each model's tokenizer.

Real-World Example

Two models: the first costs 1 dollar per million tokens, but its tokenizer turns an Arabic word into 3 tokens on average. The second costs 1.5 dollars, and its Arabic tokenizer needs 1.5 tokens per word. For a million Arabic words: the first is 3 million tokens × 1 = 3 dollars, and the second is 1.5 million × 1.5 = 2.25 dollars. The “more expensive” one on paper is the cheaper one in reality.

Key Terms
Subword tokenisation algorithmsBPE / WordPiece / SentencePiece
Algorithms that build a vocabulary from common word pieces. Common words stay a single token, and rare ones are cut into smaller pieces.
Vocabularyالمفردات
The list of all the tokens the tokenizer knows, which may be tens or hundreds of thousands. Each token has a number and a vector of its own in the model.
ALLaMعلّام
A large language model focused on Arabic, developed by SDAIA. Other Arabic models include Jais.
Tatweel / kashida (ـ)التطويل
A character that stretches a word cosmetically (for example stretching الريـــاض (“Riyadh”) by inserting elongation characters between its letters). It must be removed in normalisation so it does not spoil search.

What the Exam Tests

The tokenizer splits text into sub-word units (BPE, WordPiece, SentencePiece/Unigram) and then converts them to numbers. Everything a language model is billed, limited and measured by is tokens, not words and not characters.

  1. Fertility = tokens per word, and it differs between languages. Tokenizers trained mainly on English usually split an Arabic word into more pieces, made worse by morphology (prefixes and suffixes such as و “and”, ال “the”, ها “her/its” and the plural ون) and diacritics. Higher fertility means higher API cost, higher latency, and a smaller effective context window for the same Arabic text. Arabic or multilingual tokenizers (as in Arabic-focused models such as ALLaM and Jais) reduce this.
  2. The tokenizer is part of the model: you must use the same tokenizer the model was trained with. Swapping it breaks the model, and extending the vocabulary requires training new embedding rows.
  3. Context limits in tokens: the prompt + retrieved passages + conversation history + output must all fit together.
  4. Document chunking in RAG is measured in tokens and must match the embedding model's limit.
  5. Normalisation matters for Arabic: the forms of alef (أ إ آ ا: with hamza above, with hamza below, with madda, and bare), teh marbuta versus heh (ة/ه), alef maqsura versus yeh (ى/ي), tatweel (ـ, the elongation character) and diacritics. Inconsistent normalisation between indexing and querying harms retrieval.
  6. Tokenisation quirks explain strange behaviour: counting letters, spelling, and arithmetic on long numbers.
Choose the Arabic model by the actual token cost on your texts, not by the advertised price per token alone.

3.4 Embedding Models in Detail

In Plain Terms

An embedding model converts text into a list of numbers so that texts similar in meaning lie close to each other. How does it learn this? By training on pairs: “this question and its answer” should be close, and “this question and the answer to another question” should be far apart.

There are two important types. The bi-encoder: converts the question alone and the document alone, and then we compare the numbers. Very fast, because documents are converted once in advance. The cross-encoder: reads the question and the document together. Much more accurate but slow, so it is used only to rerank the best results. The most important operational rule: do not mix the vectors of two models in one index.

Real-World Example

The bi-encoder analogy: every book in the library has a “summary card” written in advance, and you write a card for your question and compare it quickly with the cards. The cross-encoder: the librarian reads your question together with the first page of each candidate book, and judges precisely. More accurate, but they cannot do that with a million books. If you changed the way the cards are written, you would have to rewrite all the library's cards, not mix old ones with new.

Embedding models convert text (or images) into fixed-length vectors so that semantically similar inputs lie close to each other.   • Record the embedding model version with every vector.
  • When changing the model, re-embed the entire repository and switch indexes atomically.
  • Cache embeddings by content hash.
  • Treat embeddings of personal data as personal data, since they can be partly reversed.

4.1 Prompt, RAG or Fine-Tuning: The Pivotal Decision

In Plain Terms

This is the most important decision table in the unit, and it recurs often in the exam. The question: how do I make the model useful for my work? Three ways, each with its cases.

The rule in short: if the problem is knowledge (information that changes, or needs a source, or permissions, or a right to deletion), the solution is RAG. If the problem is behaviour (a fixed style, a specific format, or a narrow task at huge volume), the solution is fine-tuning. If it is a quick, simple experiment, the prompt is enough. Sometimes you need both together.

Real-World Example

A smart new employee. The prompt: you give them clear instructions for every task. RAG: you give them access to the company's up-to-date files, so they search them, answer and name the file. If a policy changes, they read the new version immediately. Fine-tuning: an intensive training course that changes their writing style. But the course does not make them know what changed in the policies last week, and they cannot “forget” what they learned if asked to delete it.

Tuning methods that adjust a very small part of the model instead of all its weights, such as LoRA. Much cheaper and needing less memory.

4.2 Fine-Tuning Options

In Plain Terms

If you decide on fine-tuning, there are options. Full fine-tuning adjusts all the weights, and is the most expensive. LoRA freezes the original model and adds beside it very small layers that learn the adjustment, so the size of the adjustment is megabytes instead of gigabytes. QLoRA is like LoRA but the original model is compressed to 4 bits, so a large model fits on a single GPU.

Watch out for the risk of catastrophic forgetting: if you train the model intensively on a narrow task, it may forget general abilities it had mastered. LoRA reduces this risk because the original model stays frozen.

Real-World Example

LoRA is like putting transparent “correction stickers” over the pages of a book instead of reprinting it. The original book has not changed, the stickers are light and cheap, and you can prepare a set of stickers for each department (task), all of them using the same book. catastrophic forgetting: a general practitioner who specialised for 5 years in eye surgery only, and forgot much of general medicine.

4.3 Quantisation and Model Size

In Plain Terms

Calculating a model's size is easy: number of parameters × bytes per parameter. Every step down in precision halves the size: from FP16 (2 bytes) to INT8 (1 byte) to INT4 (half a byte).

But the weights are not everything. Add to them the KV cache for the conversations, the intermediate computations, and what the runtime needs: usually 20–40% extra, and far more with long conversations and many users. After any quantisation, re-test on your Arabic data, because quality may be affected more in Arabic than in English.

Real-World Example

A model of 70 billion parameters and a GPU with 80 GB of memory. FP16: 70 × 2 = 140 GB, does not fit. INT8: 70 × 1 = 70 GB, barely fits, but only 10 is left for conversations. INT4: 70 × 0.5 = 35 GB, fits and leaves 45 GB for the KV cache and users.

4.4 Generation Parameters

In Plain Terms

During generation the model chooses the next word from a list of probabilities. The generation settings control how this choice is made. A low temperature always picks the most likely, and a high one allows variety. Top-p and top-k cut the low-probability words from the list before choosing.

Other practical settings: the output limit (the maximum length of the answer, to control cost), stop sequences, and structured outputs (forcing the model into valid JSON so another program can read it). Remember: low temperature does not prevent hallucination.

Real-World Example

The next word after “The best time to visit Riyadh is”, with probabilities: “winter” 60%, “spring” 25%, “November” 10%, “summer” 5%. Temperature 0: always picks “winter”. Temperature 1: picks at random according to the proportions, so it may say “summer” once in 20 times. Top-p = 0.9: keeps the smallest set that reaches 90% (winter, spring and November = 95%), and drops “summer”.

4.5 Hallucination: Causes and Remedies

In Plain Terms

Hallucination has different causes, and each cause has a different remedy. This is the lesson: diagnose the cause first. If the knowledge is absent or out of date, the remedy is RAG. If retrieval returns wrong passages, fix retrieval. If the model was not asked to say “I do not know”, add the instruction. If the information is buried in the middle of a long context, reduce the passages and rank them.

The usual distractors in the exam: “use a bigger model” and “lower the temperature”. Neither remedies faulty retrieval.

Real-World Example

A customer-service employee gives wrong information. If the cause is that their price guide is out of date, the solution is to give them the new guide, not to hire a smarter person. If they are looking in the wrong section of the guide, the solution is to improve the guide's index. If they are embarrassed to say “I do not know” and so invent an answer, the solution is to tell them plainly: “If you do not find the answer in the guide, say you do not know”.

4.6 Evaluating Generative Systems

In Plain Terms

How do we evaluate a system that generates different text every time? There is no “single correct answer” to compare with word for word. So we measure several separate things: is the answer supported by sources? Is it relevant to the question? Did the system retrieve the right documents? Is it safe? Is the Arabic sound? What are the cost and time?

A common tool is the judge model, that is, a language model that evaluates the answers of another model. Cheap and fast, but it has biases: it favours the longer answer, or the first in the order, or the answers of a model from its own family. So we first verify it against human evaluation. The golden rule: separate evaluating retrieval from evaluating writing.

Real-World Example

A wrong answer from a RAG system. Was the cause that the retriever did not find the right document, or that it found it and the model misunderstood it? If you measure only the final answer you will not know. So you measure both: “is the right document among those retrieved?” (Recall@k), and “does the answer match what is in the document?” (faithfulness). The remedy is completely different in the two cases.

A strict metric: the answer is correct only if it matches the reference answer literally. Suitable for extracting specific fields.

The tendency of a judge model to prefer longer answers even when they are not better.

Caveats on the judge model

  • Position bias: favours the first or second answer depending on presentation order.
  • Length bias: favours longer answers.
  • Self-preference: favours the outputs of its own model or family.

Calibrate the judge on a human-rated sample before relying on it. Remember that automatic n-gram overlap metrics (BLEU / ROUGE) are weak indicators of open-ended quality.

Separate evaluating the retriever from evaluating the generator: a wrong answer may be caused by poor retrieval rather than a poor model.

The Scenario

A government entity in Riyadh wants an Arabic assistant that answers employees' questions about internal regulations and rules. The regulations are updated monthly, some documents are restricted to specific departments, and the legal department requires every answer to be supported by a reference.

A vendor has proposed “fully fine-tuning a 70B model on all the documents so that it memorises them”, with temperature 0 “to prevent hallucination”. The team has a single GPU with 80 GB of memory, and about 30 concurrent users are expected, with conversations of up to 8,000 tokens. Management also wants answers in a fixed formal tone and a uniform format.

Solution Steps

  1. Classify the needs: changing knowledge + citation + permissions → RAG with access filters; fixed tone and format → prompt engineering first, then LoRA tuning if that is not enough. Full fine-tuning on the documents is rejected: it cannot easily be updated monthly, it does not cite, it does not enforce permissions, and it makes destruction of data harder.
  2. Calculate the weights: 70B in FP16 = 140 GB (does not fit), in INT8 = 70 GB (barely fits, leaving no room for KV), and in INT4 = about 35 GB.
  3. Calculate KV cache memory using the worked example for an 8B-class model: 128 KB per token × 8,192 ≈ 1 GB per conversation × 30 users = 30 GB, which is more than the weights of an 8B model in FP16 (≈ 16 GB). Conclude that KV cache and context are a core part of sizing, and that GQA, KV quantisation, PagedAttention and context limits are the decision tools.
  4. Compare a smaller alternative: an Arabic model in the 7–8B class with RAG may be sufficient for a grounded enterprise-answering task and leaves room for the KV cache; decide only after evaluating on your own Arabic set, and re-run the evaluation after any quantisation.
  5. Set generation: low temperature (0–0.3) for RAG answers, an output limit, and structured outputs for citations. Explain to the vendor that temperature 0 does not prevent hallucination; what prevents it is grounding, the “answer from the context only, otherwise say I do not know” instruction, and verification.
  6. Design the evaluation: faithfulness to sources with a human-calibrated judge model, Recall@k and MRR for the retriever, review by native Arabic speakers, safety and personal-data checks, and TTFT, p95 and cost per answer.

Exam Traps

  • “Fine-tune the model on our documents so that it knows them” when the documents change or need citation or permissions.
  • “Temperature zero removes hallucination”: it makes outputs deterministic, not correct.
  • Sizing the GPU from the weights alone while ignoring the KV cache and runtime overhead (20–40% or more).
  • Confusing serving memory (2 bytes/parameter in FP16) with full training memory (≈ 16 bytes/parameter with AdamW).
  • Believing that FlashAttention approximates attention or changes its result, or that MoE is cheap in memory because it is cheap in compute.
  • Relying on a judge model or the BLEU/ROUGE metric without human calibration, especially with Arabic paraphrases.

  1. 1Why are attention scores divided by √d_k?
    Answer

    Because the dot product grows with the dimension, so softmax saturates towards a one-hot value and gradients vanish; the scaling keeps the scores in a range that preserves gradients.

  2. 2How much memory does full training of a 7B model with AdamW in mixed precision need?
    Answer

    About 16 bytes per parameter (2 weights + 2 gradients + 4 FP32 master copy + 8 Adam moments), that is ≈ 112 GB before activations; so it needs several GPUs or ZeRO/FSDP, whereas LoRA/QLoRA fits on a single GPU.

  3. 3Calculate the KV cache per token for a model with 32 layers, 8 KV heads and a head dimension of 128 in FP16.
    Answer

    2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 131,072 bytes = 128 KB per token; a conversation of 8,192 tokens ≈ 1 GB, and 30 users ≈ 30 GB.

  4. 4The loss is flat from the very first step; what is the first action?
    Answer

    A sanity test by trying to overfit a very small batch, and checking the labels and the data loader; the likely causes are a learning rate that is too low, frozen layers, a bug in the data, or dead activations.

  5. 5What is the difference between RLHF and DPO?
    Answer

    RLHF trains a reward model and then optimises the policy with PPO and a KL penalty; it is powerful but complex and unstable. DPO optimises directly on preference pairs with a classification-like loss, with no reward model and no reinforcement-learning loop.

  6. 6Why does Arabic text cost more in some models?
    Answer

    Because of tokenizer fertility: tokenizers trained on English split an Arabic word into more tokens because of morphology and diacritics, so cost and latency rise and the effective context window shrinks.

  7. 7The team changed the embedding model; what must it do with the index?
    Answer

    Re-embed the entire repository with the new model and switch the indexes atomically, not mix the vectors of two models in one index, and record the model version with every vector.

  8. 8A 70B model is required on a single GPU; which numerical precision brings the weights to ≈ 35 GB?
    Answer

    INT4 (0.5 bytes per parameter) via GPTQ, AWQ or GGUF, re-running the evaluation on an Arabic set because degradation may be uneven across languages.

  9. 9Why are MoE models described as “cheap in compute, heavy in memory”?
    Answer

    Because the router sends each token to a few experts, so the active parameters and the compute are fewer, but memory must hold all the experts.

  10. 10What are the caveats of using a language model as a judge?
    Answer

    Position bias, length bias and self-preference; therefore it must be calibrated on a human-rated sample before relying on it.

    A team compares two prompt versions with an LLM judge that sees each pair of answers side by side and picks the better one. Version 2's answer was always shown second, and it won 64% of 1,000 comparisons, so the team plans to ship it. A reviewer reruns 100 pairs with the order swapped: the judge now prefers whichever answer is shown second 61% of the time, so version 2 wins only 39% when shown first. What should the team do before deciding?

    Explanation

    The memo lists LLM-as-judge caveats — position bias, verbosity bias and self-preference — and says to calibrate the judge against a human-labelled sample before trusting it. Averaging the two orders gives version 2 roughly (64% + 39%) ÷ 2 ≈ 51.5%, so the apparent win is mostly the 'shown second' advantage. Evaluating both orders and keeping only consistent verdicts cancels the position effect, and human calibration shows whether the judge agrees with people at all. Significance tests cannot fix a biased measurement, ROUGE is a weak proxy for open-ended quality, and asking for reasoning does not remove the bias.

  9. 9Embeddings of personal dataHard

    A health insurer embeds members' claim notes, which contain names, diagnoses and contact details, into a vector database for a RAG assistant. Each vector is stored with its source chunk and member ID. A vendor offers cheaper vector hosting outside the Kingdom and argues that 'embeddings are just numbers, not personal data', so no residency or deletion controls apply to them. What is the correct position?

    Explanation

    The memo states plainly that embeddings of personal data are still personal data. Here each vector is stored with its source chunk and member ID, so it is directly linked to an identifiable person, and embeddings can also leak information about the text they encode. Health information calls for the strictest handling under the Personal Data Protection Law, including its rules on transfer outside the Kingdom, and a destruction request must reach the vector store as well as the source notes. Reducing dimensions does not anonymise, and moving only the vectors is still a transfer of personal data.

  10. 10Loss spikes and NaN at the peak learning rateHard

    A team pre-trains a 1B-parameter Arabic language model in BF16 with AdamW, jumping straight to a peak learning rate of 6e-4 with no warm-up. The loss falls for a few hundred steps, then spikes and becomes NaN; the gradient norm shoots up about 100-fold just before each failure. Restarting from a checkpoint fails again at different steps, so no single bad batch is to blame. What is the most appropriate fix?

    Explanation

    The memo's diagnosis row for 'loss becomes NaN after some steps' lists three causes — learning rate too high, FP16 overflow, a bad data row — and the actions: lower LR, warm-up, BF16 or loss scaling, gradient clipping, find the batch. BF16 is already in use (no overflow-range issue), and failures at different steps rule out a bad row, leaving the learning-rate shock. Warm-up ramps the rate while the optimiser's statistics are unreliable, then decay brings it down; clipping caps the gradient norm so one bad step cannot blow up training. FP16 has a narrower range and would be worse; regularisation is not the cause; a bigger batch without warm-up leaves the shock in place.
