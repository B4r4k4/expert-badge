Unit Introduction

A warning from past candidates: “Memorise the principles by their exact official names; the options are close, the difference between two principles can be a single word, and a quick read is not enough.” The typical question in this part of the domain describes an entity’s behaviour and asks which principle, or which obligation, was breached. This unit builds four layers: first, the official names of SDAIA’s AI Ethics Principles, the risk levels, the lifecycle and the roles; second, the official controls for each principle at each stage, and the map from scenario to principle; third, the Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL) as engineering controls, with its deadlines and figures; and fourth, the national data governance system, the generative AI principles, deepfakes and the media sector. Set aside a separate study session for it.

Before You Start

This unit is about the Saudi rules: SDAIA’s AI Ethics Principles, the National AI Risk Management Framework, the Personal Data Protection Law, data classification, and the principles for generative AI, deepfakes and the media. It is the second half of Domain 6.

This unit is different from the others: the exam asks about the official names word for word. الخصوصية والأمن (“Privacy and Security”) is correct, and الأمن والخصوصية (“Security and Privacy”) is wrong. So memorise the names as they are written in the tables, and do not rely on your own wording. The plain-language explanation here helps you understand what they mean, but it does not change their names.

You need these first: Unit 00 (Sections 5.6 and 5.7: personal data and SDAIA) and Unit 09 (Section 3.3: encryption does not cure a missing legal basis).

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

  • Recall the official names of the seven principles in Arabic and English and their word order, and distinguish the near-identical pairs.
  • Distinguish the Ethics Principles document from the National AI Risk Management Framework in stages, categories and levels, and calculate the risk score.
  • Identify the stage in which an official control falls within the matrix of seven principles × four stages.
  • Link a violation scenario to the official principle and to the accompanying obligation in the law or the security control.
  • Apply the PDPL obligations with their deadlines: 30 days for requests, extendable by 30; 72 hours to notify SDAIA; and the penalties after the 2023 amendments.
  • Classify data according to the National Data Management Office (NDMO) levels and infer the effect on hosting and on the use of generative AI tools.
  • Distinguish the voluntary tools (badges, certificates and self-assessment) from the mandatory obligations in the law.

Chapter 1

SDAIA’s AI Ethics Principles and Risk Frameworks

1.1 Scope and the Seven Principles by Their Official Names

In Plain Terms

SDAIA (the Saudi Data and AI Authority) issued the AI Ethics Principles. They apply to anyone who develops, deploys or uses AI in the Kingdom: government, private and non-profit entities, and individuals. There are seven principles.

What they mean, in plain terms. Integrity and Fairness: the system does not wrong any group. Privacy and Security: it protects the data and the system itself. Humanity: it serves people and does not deceive or manipulate them. Social and Environmental Benefits: it benefits society and the environment. Reliability and Safety: it works as designed and poses no danger. Transparency and Explainability: people understand its decisions and know they are dealing with AI. Accountability and Responsibility: there are known people who are responsible.

Real-World Example

A way to memorise them: imagine a new employee in a government entity, and ask seven questions about him. Does he treat everyone fairly? (Integrity and Fairness). Does he keep visitors’ secrets? (Privacy and Security). Does he respect them and not deceive them? (Humanity). Is his work useful to society? (Social and Environmental Benefits). Does he do his job well, without dangerous mistakes? (Reliability and Safety). Does he explain his decisions? (Transparency and Explainability). Is there a manager responsible for him whom you can complain to? (Accountability and Responsibility).

1.2 Close Pairs and One-Word Traps

In Plain Terms

Some principles are close, and the exam puts both in the options. There are three pairs, and each pair is settled by one question. Transparency or Accountability? If the problem is “the affected person does not understand, or does not know they are dealing with a machine”, it is Transparency. If it is “there is no responsible person, no logs, and no redress”, it is Accountability.

Privacy and Security or Reliability and Safety? Protecting the data, the model and lawful use → Privacy and Security. The system behaving correctly and safely, robustness and oversight → Reliability and Safety. Integrity and Fairness or Humanity? Unequal outcomes between groups → Integrity and Fairness. Manipulation, deception, or harm to dignity or culture → Humanity.

Real-World Example

Two cases that look alike. (1) “My application was rejected and no one told me why”: the affected person does not understand, so it is Transparency and Explainability. (2) “My application was rejected, and when I tried to object I found no body to object to and no one responsible”: no accountability and no redress, so it is Accountability and Responsibility. A third example: “a games app uses fake countdown timers to push children to buy”: this is not unfairness between groups but manipulation, so it is Humanity.

Key Terms
One-word trapفخ الكلمة الواحدة
An exam option that differs from the correct one by a single word or by the order of two words, to test how precisely you memorised the official name.
Redressالتعويض
Repairing the harm done to the affected person, such as correcting the decision or restoring the right. Part of the Accountability and Responsibility principle.

1.3 Risk Levels, Lifecycle and Roles

In Plain Terms

The four risk levels in the Ethics Principles: little or no risk (such as a spam filter: compliance with the principles is recommended), limited risk (apply the principles), high risk (a risk to fundamental human rights: conformity assessment before and after deployment), and unacceptable risk (such as social scoring and exploiting children: not permitted at all).

the lifecycle has four stages under their official names: Planning and Design, then Data Preparation, then Building and Performance Measurement, then Deployment and Monitoring. The roles within the entity: the head of the entity or the Data Management Officer approves and oversees, the Compliance Officer is the strategic lead, the AI Officer is the operational lead, and the AI System Assessor audits. A warning: registration with SDAIA is voluntary, unlike the binding Personal Data Protection Law.

Real-World Example

The levels with examples. Little or no: a system that suggests a subject line for your email. Limited: a system that orders employees’ daily tasks. High: a system that decides who receives social support (it touches a fundamental right), so it needs a conformity assessment before and after deployment. Unacceptable: a system that gives every citizen a “social behaviour score” that affects their rights, which is entirely forbidden.

1.4 The National AI Risk Management Framework (April 2026)

In Plain Terms

The National AI Risk Management Framework (April 2026) is a different document from the Ethics Principles, and that is exactly what makes it a source of many traps. It is a tool for identifying, assessing and treating risks. It has five stages: Context and Scope Definition → Risk Identification → Risk Assessment → Risk Treatment → Monitoring and Review. seven risk categories.

The method of assessment: likelihood (1 to 4) × impact (1 to 4) = risk score. 1–2 low, 3–6 medium, 8–12 high, 16 catastrophic. Four treatment options: avoid, mitigate, transfer (a contract or insurance, which does not remove the entity’s responsibility), and accept (residual risk within the limit, documented).

Real-World Example

The calculation. A risk with likelihood “likely” (3) and impact “catastrophic” (4): 3 × 4 = 12, high. A risk with likelihood “almost certain” (4) and impact “low” (1): 4 × 1 = 4, only medium. Note that high likelihood alone does not make a risk high. Why do the ranges jump from 6 to 8? Because the product of two numbers from 1 to 4 can never be 5 or 7: the possible results are only 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12 and 16.

An SDAIA framework (April 2026) for identifying, assessing, treating and monitoring AI risks, with five stages, seven risk categories, and scores from 1 to 16.

Likelihood: rare, unlikely, likely, almost certain (1 to 4). Impact: low, medium, high, catastrophic (1 to 4). Their product is the risk score.

  • The Ethics lifecycle has 4 stages, while the Framework’s process has 5 stages.
  • Ethics has risk levels (little or no · limited · high · unacceptable), while the Framework has risk scores (low · medium · high · catastrophic).
  • Read whichever document the question names before you choose.

1.5 International Frameworks That May Be Named

In Plain Terms

A question may name international frameworks. OECD principles: general international principles for AI. NIST AI RMF: an American risk management framework with four functions (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage). EU AI Act: the European Union law with risk tiers. ISO/IEC 42001: a standard for an AI management system that can be certified. ISO/IEC 23894: risk management guidance.

But the decisive rule: if the question asks about “SDAIA’s principles”, the Saudi names and levels are the ones that count, not the OECD or European Union labels, even if they look alike.

Real-World Example

A question: “According to SDAIA’s principles, what is the risk level of a social scoring system?”. The option “Prohibited” uses the European law’s term. The correct option is “Unacceptable Risk”, which is the official Saudi term. The meanings are close, and the correct answer is the one with the Saudi name.

1.6 Registration, Badges and Self-Assessment

In Plain Terms

The AI Market Governance Tools document (SDAIA, 2025) describes three instruments. AI Ethics incentive badges: five levels showing the maturity of an entity’s or a product’s ethical practice. AI service-provider accreditation certificates: for private-sector entities that hold a badge, through the National Data Governance Platform. AI Ethics Self-Assessment: a tool with which you measure your product against the seven principles in order to learn and improve.

The three tools are voluntary and incentive-based. A badge does not replace any legal duty. Expected traps: the badge is a prerequisite for the certificate, not the certificate itself; the certificate is not issued by the National Cybersecurity Authority; and the self-assessment is for learning, not for accreditation.

Real-World Example

A restaurant analogy. Self-assessment: the owner fills in a questionnaire himself to see where he stands and improve. The badge: a star rating that reflects the restaurant’s level. The certificate: an official accreditation granted only to someone who has first obtained a rating. But all of this does not replace the mandatory municipal licence (which here is the Personal Data Protection Law). A five-star restaurant without a licence is still in violation.

What the Exam Tests

SDAIA’s AI Market Governance Tools document (2025) describes three instruments built on the Ethics Principles:

  1. AI Ethics incentive badges: five ascending levels reflecting the maturity of an entity’s or a product’s ethical practice, from adopting the principles to a role-model level. The assessment has four parts: product information (description, data sources), information about the product’s customers (target groups, number of customers), risk assessment (potential risks and their impact on society), and the documents required for audit.
  2. AI service-provider accreditation certificates: for private-sector entities that hold a badge; obtained through the National Data Governance Platform: register the entity → appoint an AI Officer → add the product → complete the questionnaire with documents → committee review.
  3. AI Ethics Self-Assessment: a tool that lets entities and individuals measure their products against all seven principles through a cycle: know your level of compliance → learn from the results → improve → re-assess.
All three tools are voluntary / incentive-based — unlike the mandatory, enforceable obligations of the PDPL. A badge does not replace any legal duty.

Expected traps: the badge is a prerequisite for the certificate, not the certificate itself; the certificate is not issued by the National Cybersecurity Authority; and the self-assessment tool is for learning and improvement, not for granting accreditation.

1.7 SDAIA’s AI Adoption Framework

In Plain Terms

The Framework for AI Adoption (SDAIA, 2025) measures how far entities have adopted AI, along three dimensions. Directions: are there a clear vision, goals, governance policies and priorities? Enablers: are there infrastructure, data, human talent and partnerships? Outputs: have measurable results and benefits been achieved?

The framework stresses graduated adoption: start with priority use cases, then build the enablers, then measure the outputs, then scale. In the exam: a shortage of talent or infrastructure → a gap in Enablers. The absence of a vision or policies → a gap in Directions. No measured benefits → a gap in Outputs.

Real-World Example

A government entity has a splendid written AI strategy (Directions ✓) and successful pilot projects. But it has only two engineers, no suitable servers, and its data is scattered. Where is the gap? In Enablers. It does not help to write a second strategy (Directions) or to launch more projects. What it needs is talent, infrastructure and data.

Chapter 2

The Seven Principles Stage by Stage, and from Scenario to Control

2.1 How to Read the Matrix, and the First Principle: Integrity and Fairness

In Plain Terms

The Ethics Principles are not just general talk: each principle has specific controls at each stage of the four stages. That is a table of 7 principles × 4 stages. Knowing where each control sits answers questions such as “At which stage should the team have…?”.

Integrity and Fairness across the four stages, in brief. In Planning and Design: identify the affected groups, choose the fairness metric, and define the threshold at which the system is fair or unfair, with the participation of non-technical people. In Data Preparation: do not exclude the least represented groups, and do not include sensitive data among the model’s features without a justification. In Building: take fairness metrics into account when selecting the model. In Deployment and Monitoring: monitor the metric against the threshold you set.

Real-World Example

A question: “A team built a student admissions model, and after launch discovered a gap between regions, and then began to ask: what gap is acceptable?”. The mistake is that the question came too late. The fairness metric and its threshold (“the gap in the acceptance rate must not exceed such-and-such”) should have been defined in the Planning and Design stage, before collecting the data and training.

The controls of each of the seven principles at each stage of the four-stage lifecycle.

The value at which a system’s assessment is considered fair or unfair; it is set in Planning and Design and monitored in Deployment and Monitoring.
Sensitive attributes defined at designالبيانات الحساسة المحددة في التصميم

2.2 The Second Principle: Privacy and Security

In Plain Terms

Privacy and Security across the four stages. In Planning and Design: personal data at the minimum, systems that profile individuals or groups are developed only with the approval of the Chief Data Officer or under a sector code of conduct, a security design that protects the data and the model and its weights, and an impact assessment before processing personal or sensitive data. In Data Preparation: compliance at collection, authorised access only, classification, and backup and encryption.

In Building: protect the model’s components from corruption or modification, and test that it does not reveal personal data. In Deployment and Monitoring: continuous monitoring, and the system owner is responsible for protecting personal data throughout the lifecycle. A recurring signal: protecting model weights falls under Privacy and Security, not under Reliability and Safety.

Real-World Example

A question: “A model weights file was stolen from an unprotected server. Which principle was touched?”. You might think “Reliability and Safety” because it concerns the model. But the principle’s text is explicit: security design against attacks on “the data, the model or the model weights” falls under Privacy and Security.

2.3 The Third and Fourth Principles: Humanity, and Social and Environmental Benefits

In Plain Terms

Humanity: AI is built on human rights and cultural values, does not deceive or manipulate, does not impose behaviour that does not enhance human skills, and leaves the choice and the decision to the human. In planning: how does the system comply with Saudi human rights and social and cultural values? In building: priority to systems that facilitate human decision-making. In monitoring: periodic assessments and corrective actions.

Social and Environmental Benefits: it achieves a sustained positive impact and causes no harm. In Deployment and Monitoring: continuous assessment of social, cultural, economic and environmental impact. Its signal in a scenario is usually waste: a huge model where a small one would suffice, while ignoring energy.

Real-World Example

Humanity: an educational app for children uses “streaks” (“You will lose your stars if you do not log in today!”) and fake offers that expire after a minute to push them to buy. There is no unfairness between groups here, but manipulation of the child’s behaviour, so the principle is Humanity. Social and Environmental Benefits: an entity trained a giant model for weeks to classify requests into 5 categories, when a small model would suffice.

2.4 The Fifth Principle: Reliability and Safety

In Plain Terms

Reliability and Safety: the system works to its specification and as its designers intended. Reliability = does it keep working as it should? safety = is it free of danger to people? It must have mechanisms that prevent harm.

The most important controls. In planning: robustness against new situations, and approval of important decision points by stakeholders. In Data Preparation: verifying that the system works in emergency events. In Building: rigorous testing, human oversight of irreversible decisions or decisions related to life, and it is not used for social scoring or mass surveillance. In monitoring: periodic monitoring of risks, internal and external.

Real-World Example

A hospital system decides a medication dose and sends the order directly to the pharmacy without a physician reviewing it. This is a decision related to preserving life and cannot easily be reversed if it is wrong. The violated control: human oversight under Reliability and Safety. The official text’s example: a self-driving car not trained to recognise pedestrians in some conditions, which is a safety risk.

2.5 The Sixth Principle: Transparency and Explainability

In Plain Terms

Transparency and Explainability: decisions are clear and their steps can be traced, the data, algorithms and purpose are transparent to those affected, and developers can justify what they did. The degree of transparency depends on the context.

The most important controls. In planning: those affected understand how outputs are produced, and a mechanism to record and handle complaints. In Data Preparation: documenting the datasets. In Building and Performance Measurement: informing those affected that the outcome was processed by AI and enabling them to request an explanation, and if a third party built it, make sure all the documentation is available and traceable before procurement or adoption. In monitoring: documenting performance, recording failures and leaks, and notifying the competent authorities.

Real-World Example

An entity wants to buy a request-classification system from a company. The company says: “The system is excellent, but how it works and its training data are a trade secret.” The Transparency and Explainability control in the Building and Performance Measurement stage says: you must make sure that all the documentation is available and traceable before procurement. If it is not made available, that is a violation that must be resolved before contracting.

2.6 The Seventh Principle: Accountability and Responsibility

In Plain Terms

Accountability and Responsibility: designers, developers, officers and assessors bear responsibility for decisions and actions, they are named and reachable, and there are human oversight and governance throughout the lifecycle, and measures to assess and mitigate risks.

The most important controls. In planning: a governance structure that defines authorities and responsibilities, tools such as impact assessment, audit and recovery plans, and decisions that are monitored and open to intervention by authorised users. In Data Preparation: approval of datasets before development, and no biometric attributes in the model’s data without a documented justification. In Building: technical staff are accountable for feature selection, tuning and the model, and review and approval before deployment. In monitoring: clear responsibilities and continuous monitoring.

Real-World Example

A question: “A model in production gave wrong decisions, and when the team was asked, no one knew who had approved its launch, and there is no record.” The violated principle: Accountability and Responsibility. There is no named owner, no approval path and no audit trail. The engineering control: a model registry recording who approved each version and when.

The Personal Data Protection Law: From Text to Operation

3.1 Source and Scope

In Plain Terms

The Personal Data Protection Law is a Saudi law that is binding, with penalties, unlike the voluntary principles. It was issued by Royal Decree M/19 (1443 AH, 2021) and amended by Royal Decree M/148 (1444 AH, 2023). It has been in force since 14 September 2023, with a grace period for entities until 14 September 2024. It is supervised by SDAIA. It is detailed by the Implementing Regulations and the Regulation on Personal Data Transfer outside the Kingdom.

Its scope: all processing inside the Kingdom, and also processing of the data of residents of the Kingdom that takes place from outside it. That is, a company abroad that processes the data of residents of Saudi Arabia is subject to the Law.

Real-World Example

A start-up in Dubai offers a fitness app used by 50,000 people in Riyadh and Jeddah, and its servers are in Europe. Is it subject to the Saudi Personal Data Protection Law? Yes. Because the Law covers the processing of the data of residents of the Kingdom even if it takes place from outside.

The binding Saudi law that regulates the collection, processing, sharing and protection of personal data, gives data subjects rights, and is supervised by SDAIA.
Royal Decreeالمرسوم الملكي
The legal instrument through which laws are issued in the Kingdom. The PDPL was issued by Decree M/19 and amended by Decree M/148.
Implementing Regulationsاللائحة التنفيذية
A document that details how the Law is applied, such as the breach-reporting deadline in its Article 24.
Transfer Regulationsلائحة نقل البيانات الشخصية خارج المملكة

3.2 Core Terms

In Plain Terms

The Law’s core terms. Personal data: any data that identifies a person, directly or indirectly, including pseudonymised data and vector representations as long as linking is possible. Sensitive data: a precisely defined list (racial or tribal origin, belief, security and criminal data, biometric, genetic, health, and data indicating unknown parentage). Note: credit data and location data were removed from the list by the 2023 amendment.

The parties: the data subject (the person themselves), the controller (determines the purpose and the means, and is the responsible party), and the processor (processes on its behalf). The important difference: final, irreversible anonymisation takes data out of the Law, whereas reversible pseudonymisation leaves it personal data.

Real-World Example

Controller and processor: a bank (the controller) decided to use its customers’ data to detect fraud, and hired a cloud company (the processor) to run the system on its behalf. If a leak occurs at the cloud company, the bank remains responsible for compliance, and it must have verified the company’s compliance. An example on sensitivity: “loan and default history” is no longer sensitive data after 2023, but it is still personal data that needs protection.

3.5 Before Launching an AI System That Processes Personal Data

In Plain Terms

Before launching any AI system that processes personal data, here are ten steps in order. The order itself answers “What is the first step?” questions. (1) Data map: what data, from where, and where it is stored (including prompts, representations, logs and copies). (2) Purpose and legal basis. (3) Minimisation. (4) Impact assessment. (5) Privacy notice. (6) Retention schedule. (7) Processor and transfer checks. (8) Security controls. (9) The ability to carry out rights everywhere. (10) Record of activities.

Note that the first step is always the data map. You cannot protect what you do not know the location of, nor assess the impact of what you do not know the extent of.

Real-World Example

A hospital wants to launch an assistant that summarises patient files for doctors. The manager says: “Start with encryption and security.” But which data exactly? Does the assistant need the patient’s name, or is their number enough? Will it be sent to an external provider? Where are the summaries stored? For how long? Without the data map first, you will put controls in the wrong places, and forget important places such as logs and backups.

A text that informs data subjects of the purpose, the basis, the data collected, the retention period, the recipients, transfer abroad, and their rights.

What the Exam Tests

The ten-step checklist — its order answers “What is the first step?” questions:

  1. Map the data: what personal data, from whom, through which systems, stored where (including prompts, embeddings, logs and backups), and shared with which processors.
  2. Define the purpose and the legal basis: a specific purpose; consent or another basis; and stricter conditions for sensitive data.
  3. Minimise: delete the fields the model does not need; use pseudonymisation; and avoid training on personal data when retrieval or aggregation suffices.
  4.   6. Retention schedule: a period for each dataset and copy, with automatic deletion or anonymisation.
  7. Processor and transfer checks: contracts with cloud and model providers that restrict use (such as no training on your data), security obligations, sub-processors, and audit rights; and a basis for transfer if any data leaves the Kingdom.
  8. Security controls: encryption according to classification, access control, logs, and data loss prevention.
  9. Rights handling: the system’s ability to find, export, correct and delete a person’s data everywhere, including vector indexes and caches, within the response deadline.
  10. Record of processing activities: documented and ready to present.

3.6 When a Breach Occurs, and the Data Subject Request Path

In Plain Terms

When a leak occurs: six steps. (1) Contain: stop the bleeding (withdraw access, change keys, stop the feature). (2) Assess: which data, how many people, and which of it is sensitive. (3) Notify the competent authority within 72 hours with what is known so far. (4) Inform individuals without delay if harm is likely, with practical advice. (5) Document. (6) Remediate and learn.

The data subject request path: verify their identity → log the request → search for their data in all the systems → act → respond within 30 days → document. “all the systems” includes the vector index, logs, backups and training sets. The lesson for models: prevention is easier than cure. Do not put personal data into training, and keep it in RAG where deletion is straightforward.

Real-World Example

A customer asked for their data to be deleted. The team deleted it from the main database and said “done”. But: it is still in the vector index of a RAG assistant, in the conversation logs, in last week’s backup, and in the dataset on which the recommendation model was trained. The request was not actually fulfilled. If the model itself had been trained on the customer’s data, removing it from the model is nearly impossible, which is why prevention is better.

4.1 Data Classification Levels, and the Classification and Sharing Principles

In Plain Terms

The National Data Management Office sets four levels of data classification, and each level determines where an AI system may be hosted. Top Secret: exceptionally grave damage, so fully internal isolated environments only. Secret: serious damage, so inside the Kingdom with tight control and no external APIs. Restricted: limited damage, so an approved cloud inside the Kingdom with encryption and access control. Public: no damage, so greater flexibility.

Among the classification principles is one very important principle: the highest level of protection. Mixed data takes the highest classification among its parts. So if a RAG index combines “Public” and “Restricted” documents, the whole index is Restricted unless separated. The classification must be known before choosing the architecture.

Real-World Example

An entity built a RAG index with 9,000 “Public” documents (published service guides) and 50 “Restricted” documents (internal procedures). It said: “99% is public, so we will use an external model API.” The mistake: under the highest level of protection principle the whole index is “Restricted”, so no external API. The solutions: separate the restricted documents into a separate index hosted inside the Kingdom, or host everything inside the Kingdom.

4.6 Deepfake Principles by Audience

In Plain Terms

An educational company wants to produce lessons with the voice and image of a famous teacher generated by AI, in multiple languages. Is this prohibited? No, it is a legitimate educational use. But with safeguards: the teacher’s consent to the use of their voice and image, a clear label “This video is AI-generated”, a watermark that can be detected, and a mechanism to remove the content if someone misuses it.

What the Exam Tests

SDAIA’s AI Principles in Media document (June 2026) applies the ethics framework to newsrooms and content producers. The recurring themes are what you already know, applied to content:

  • Disclosure of AI-generated or AI-assisted content.
  • Accuracy and verification before publication (editorial responsibility remains human).
  • Avoiding deepfakes and manipulating the images or words of real people without consent.
  • Respect for intellectual property.
  • Fairness in representation.
  • Privacy of the people in the news.
  • Accountability of the publishing organisation.

In media scenarios the decisive controls are: labelling, editorial review and consent.

The briefing note cautions: check the exact names of the principles against the official document (verify) — the above are summarised themes, not official names.

Applied Scenario

Identify the principle, the obligation, the deadline and the control in a government scenario with several violations, using the exact official names.

The Scenario

A government entity launched a generative assistant to answer citizens’ enquiries about social-support services. It runs on RAG over documents, some classified “Public” and some “Restricted”, and uses a language-model API hosted by a provider outside the Kingdom. The assistant introduces itself as “Sara from the customer service team” with no indication that it is AI.

The team reused call-centre conversation logs (collected for support) to fine-tune the model, and no impact assessment was carried out before launch. The raw logs are kept with no retention period. On the 10th of the month at 14:00, the team discovered that the assistant was displaying excerpts from other citizens’ files, and decided to wait for the internal investigation to finish “in about a month” before reporting anything. There is no path to appeal the assistant’s answers, and no one knows who approved its launch. In addition, an employee of the entity pasted a budget table classified “Restricted” into a public generative tool to summarise it.

Solution Steps

  1. Classification first: the index combines “Public” and “Restricted”, so it takes the highest classification in it (Restricted) unless separated — under the “highest level of protection” principle. Suitable hosting for Restricted: an approved cloud inside the Kingdom with encryption and access control; and sending to an external API is a transfer violation (Privacy and Security + transfer outside the Kingdom). Encryption does not cure it.
  2. Pretending to be a human employee: the “Transparency and Explainability” principle, together with the disclosure duty in the generative AI guidelines.
  3. Reusing support conversations for fine-tuning: the “Privacy and Security” principle, and the obligations of purpose limitation and legal basis. The absence of an impact assessment before launching a generative system on personal data violates the impact assessment obligation and the official control in Planning and Design.
  4. Logs with no retention period: “Privacy and Security” + retention and destruction; the schedule must cover logs, embeddings and fine-tuning data.
  5. The leak: immediate containment (stop the feature, fix the ACL), then assessment, then notifying SDAIA within 72 hours of becoming aware: the 10th at 14:00 + 72 hours = the 13th at 14:00 — with the facts known, updated later; the investigation continues in parallel. Waiting a month is a violation touching “Privacy and Security” and “Accountability and Responsibility”.
  6. No appeal and no one knows who approved: “Accountability and Responsibility” — named owners, an approval record, and a redress path.
  7. The employee and the public tool: a breach of the rule against entering data classified “Restricted” or above into generative AI tools; use is limited to “Public”; and it is also the bad-prompt pattern “asking it to analyse a classified budget statement”.

Exam Traps

  • Writing a principle’s name in a reworded form: الأمن والخصوصية (“Security and Privacy”), المتانة والسلامة (“Robustness and Safety”), or الشفافية وقابلية التفسير (“Transparency and Explainability” without the article, the risk framework’s form, not the Principles’).
  • Confusing the risk levels in the Ethics Principles (little or no · limited · high · unacceptable) with the risk scores in the National Framework (low · medium · high · catastrophic), or confusing 4 stages with 5 stages.
  • Treating credit data or location data as “sensitive under the Law” after the 2023 amendment, or treating pseudonymised data as non-personal.
  • Waiting for the investigation to finish before notifying SDAIA, or counting the deadline from the date the investigation ends instead of the date of awareness.
  • Thinking that incentive badges or accreditation certificates replace the Law’s obligations, or that transferring risk through insurance removes the entity’s responsibility.
  • Saying that imprisonment is still the penalty for unlawful transfer outside the Kingdom, or that a 5 million fine is not doubled on repetition.

Exercises & Unit Review

Try to answer on your own first, then open the question to see the worked answer.

  1. 1Write the seven principles by their official Arabic names.
    Answer

    النزاهة والإنصاف (Integrity and Fairness); الخصوصية والأمن (Privacy and Security); الإنسانية (Humanity); المنافع الاجتماعية والبيئية (Social and Environmental Benefits); الموثوقية والسلامة (Reliability and Safety); الشفافية والقابلية للتفسير (Transparency and Explainability); المساءلة والمسؤولية (Accountability and Responsibility). Word order is part of the name.

  2. 2A system poses a risk to fundamental human rights; which risk level is it, and what does it require?
    Answer

    High risk: conformity assessments before and after deployment, together with the applicable regulations. Social scoring and exploiting children, by contrast, are unacceptable risks and are not permitted.

  3. 3A risk has likelihood “almost certain” (4) and impact “low” (1) in the National Risk Management Framework; what is its level?
    Answer

    4 × 1 = 4, which is medium (3–6). High likelihood alone does not make a risk high; high is 8–12 and catastrophic is 16.

  4. 4A company transferred a residual risk to an insurance company; is its responsibility removed?
    Answer

    No. The transfer option (contracts, guarantees, insurance) in the National Framework does not remove the entity’s responsibility, and the decision remains documented in the risk register.

  5. 5At which stage, and under which principle, does the control “making sure that third-party system documentation is available and traceable before procurement” fall?
    Answer

    The Transparency and Explainability principle, at the Building and Performance Measurement stage.

  6. 6Who is the strategic lead and who is the operational lead for AI ethics within the entity?
    Answer

    The Compliance Officer is the strategic lead and reports to the Data Management Officer; the AI Officer is the operational lead and works with the data management team. The AI System Assessor audits and issues audit reports.

  7. 7What are the five rights in Article 4 of the Law, and what is the response deadline?
    Answer

    To be informed, to access, to obtain a copy in a readable format, to request correction, and to request destruction; and withdrawal of consent is in Article 5. The response is due within 30 days, extendable by 30 days in specified cases.

  8. 8What is the only criminal offence that remains in the Law after the 2023 amendments, and what is its penalty?
    Answer

    Disclosing or publishing sensitive data in violation of the Law with intent to harm or for personal benefit: imprisonment of up to 2 years and/or a fine of up to 3 million riyals. Other violations carry a warning or a fine of up to 5 million riyals, doubled on repetition.

  9. 9A government employee wants to summarise a document classified “Secret” with a public generative AI tool; what is the ruling?
    Answer

    Not permitted: the Generative AI Principles for Government Entities prohibit entering any data classified “Restricted” or above, and use is limited to data classified “Public”.

  10. 10What are the three dimensions of the AI Adoption Framework, and which one does a shortage of talent belong to?
    Answer

    Directions, Enablers and Outputs. A shortage of talent (its number, specialisation and retention) is a gap in Enablers, along with infrastructure, data and partnerships.

Exam-Style Questions

Choose an answer to see the correct one and its explanation. These 10 questions are drawn from the 81 questions assigned to this unit in the exam simulator.

  1. 1Model weights and the Privacy & Security principleHard

    A Saudi fintech fine-tuned an Arabic credit-risk model on two years of proprietary data. The model performs to specification, passed robustness tests and has a named owner. During a penetration test, the red team finds the fine-tuned weights in a storage bucket readable by any authenticated cloud user, with no access logging; a copy was downloaded from an unknown account last month. The board asks which SDAIA AI Ethics Principle the team's design most directly failed. Which principle?

    Explanation

    Privacy & Security protects personal data and the system itself — the memo stresses it explicitly includes model weights, and its Planning & Design controls require security design against attacks on data, the model or the weights. Reliability & Safety is the close pair: it concerns the system behaving correctly and robustly, which the stem says it does. Missing logs weaken accountability, but the root failure is unprotected weights, so the decisive principle is Privacy & Security.

  2. 2Legitimate interest and sensitive dataHard

    A health-insurance company wants to train a claims-triage model on members' medical records. Collecting fresh consent from 600,000 members would take months, so the legal advisor proposes relying on 'legitimate interest', added to PDPL in 2023, arguing faster claims benefit members. The data will stay in-Kingdom and be encrypted. Which assessment is correct?

    Explanation

    Lawful basis is the first PDPL control: consent records with a withdrawal path, or another basis. Legitimate interest has been available since 2023, but not for sensitive data, and health data is sensitive. Encryption and in-Kingdom hosting are security controls; they do not fix a lawful-basis problem. Pseudonymised records remain personal — and here still sensitive — data, so the basis question remains.

  3. 3Enabling a GenAI tool in a government entityHard

    A ministry wants to let staff use a commercial generative-AI writing tool for drafting public announcements and other content classified Public. The IT department proposes simply unblocking the tool's website. Under SDAIA's Generative AI principles for government entities, what else should the entity do?

    Explanation

    SDAIA's Generative AI principles for government entities limit use to data classified Public — employees must not enter Restricted, Secret or Top Secret data — and also require the entity to apply PDPL protections, check each tool's privacy policy (disclosure and sharing terms), weigh data-leakage, misinformation and deepfake risks, assess risk using the Ethics risk levels, and have users review outputs critically. The entity sets clear usage policies, with roles for its Data Management Office and users. Unblocking alone skips all of this; allowing Restricted data breaks the core rule whatever the contract says; a total ban is not what the principles require.

  4. 4Weak enablers in the Adoption FrameworkHard

    A ministry has a clear AI vision aligned with the national strategy, approved governance policies and a prioritised list of use cases. However, its data is fragmented and of poor quality, it has no GPU capacity, and it has lost most of its AI specialists to the private sector. In SDAIA's AI Adoption Framework, which dimension is weakest?

    Explanation

    SDAIA's AI Adoption Framework assesses three dimensions. Directions (strategic and institutional) covers vision and goals, initiative design, alignment with national strategy, governance policies and use-case prioritisation — this ministry is strong here. Enablers covers infrastructure, data and human capabilities (number, specialisation, development and retention of AI talent) and partnerships — exactly what is missing. Outputs covers results of actual adoption; weak outputs would follow from weak enablers, but the root gap described is in the enablers. The framework is not strategy-only.

  5. 5Unequal service quality for dialect speakersHard

    A government services voice assistant resolves 91% of requests from speakers of Najdi and Hijazi Arabic but only 64% from speakers of southern dialects, who are then routed to a slower queue. The training audio came mostly from Riyadh and Jeddah call centres. The assistant clearly discloses that it is AI, explains its routing, and has a complaint channel with a named owner. Which SDAIA AI Ethics Principle is most directly violated?

    Explanation

    Fairness requires the absence or limitation of bias against individuals or groups arising from data or algorithms, with data representative of all target segments and no systematic exclusion of under-represented groups. A 27-point resolution gap between dialect groups, caused by unrepresentative training audio, is an unequal outcome between groups — Fairness. Humanity is the close pair but concerns manipulation, deception and dignity; disclosure and redress are already in place.

  6. 6Obtaining an AI service-provider accreditation certificateHard

    A private Saudi AI vendor already holds an AI Ethics incentive badge for its document-processing product. Government clients now ask for an AI service-provider accreditation certificate. The vendor's COO assumes the certificate is issued automatically with the badge, or by NCA after a penetration test. According to SDAIA's AI Market Governance Tools, how is the certificate obtained?

    Explanation

    SDAIA's AI Market Governance Tools (2025) describe three voluntary instruments: incentive badges (five levels), accreditation certificates for private-sector entities that obtained a badge, and a self-assessment tool. The certificate is obtained through the National Data Governance Platform: register the entity, appoint an AI officer, add the product, complete the questionnaire with documents, then committee review. The badge is a prerequisite, not the certificate; the self-assessment tool is for learning and improvement; NCA does not issue it.

  7. 7Human oversight for irreversible decisionsHard

    A hospital network is building a model that will automatically cancel scheduled surgeries when it predicts high post-operative risk, with no clinician review, to save planning time. Accuracy is good and the data is protected. During Build & Performance Measurement, the ethics officer blocks integration testing sign-off. Under the stage-by-stage controls of the SDAIA AI Ethics Principles, which principle's control is the officer most directly enforcing?

    Explanation

    At Build & Performance Measurement, Reliability & Safety requires rigorous testing with built-in oversight, stakeholder-approved integration testing, and human oversight for systems making irreversible decisions or decisions about preserving life. The memo also maps 'autonomous medical-treatment decisions, no clinician review' to Reliability & Safety. Explanations and fairness checks may also be needed, but they do not cure the missing human gate on a life-related decision.

  8. 8Evidence of Accountability & ResponsibilityHard

    An auditor reviewing a bank's loan-approval model asks for evidence that the Accountability & Responsibility principle is implemented. Using the memo's table that maps principles to controls, tooling and evidence, which set of evidence fits best?

    Explanation

    The memo's table lists, for Accountability & Responsibility: engineering controls — named owners, approval workflow, audit trail, intervention or kill switch, redress; tooling — model registry, lineage, ticketing; and evidence for auditors — approval records, audit logs and a RACI. The other options are the evidence the same table assigns to other principles: fairness reports and threshold decisions for Fairness; DPIA, records of processing and pen-test/red-team reports for Privacy & Security; impact assessments and efficiency metrics for Social & Environmental Benefits.

  9. 9Developer duties: consent and removal from training dataHard

    A Riyadh start-up sells an Arabic voice-cloning service that publishers use to dub educational videos. Its model was fine-tuned on recordings from 600 voice actors who signed consent forms. One actor has withdrawn her consent and asks that her recordings no longer be used. The CTO notes that her recordings are only 0.02% of the corpus and that the next fine-tuning run is in two weeks. Under the expectations the Deepfake Principles set for developers, what is the most appropriate response?

    Explanation

    The Deepfake Principles expect developers to manage consent and to honour individuals' right to have their personal data removed from training data; PDPL also lets a data subject withdraw consent, after which processing on that basis must stop. A voice recording is personal data whatever its share of the corpus, so 0.02% is irrelevant. Watermarking and transparency reports are also developer expectations, but neither stops the processing she objected to. Exam lesson: when the scenario turns on a person's request about their own data, the decisive control is consent and removal, not a downstream mitigation.

  10. 10DPIA before launching a GenAI systemHard

    A retail bank will launch a generative-AI assistant that answers 2.5 million customers' questions about their own transactions. Encryption, role-based access and a privacy notice are ready, and a record of processing exists. The product owner proposes skipping the impact assessment because customer IDs are pseudonymised before they reach the model. Launch is in ten days. What is the most appropriate position?

    Explanation

    Step 4 of the pre-launch playbook: a DPIA is required for high-risk processing, sensitive data, large scale and new technologies such as generative AI. It describes the processing, assesses necessity and proportionality, identifies risks to individuals, plans mitigations and records residual risk and approvals. 2.5 million customers plus GenAI meets the trigger. Pseudonymised data is still personal data because it can be re-linked. Assessing after launch is the wrong order, and a penetration test checks security controls, not necessity, proportionality or risks to individuals.
