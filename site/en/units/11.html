Unit Introduction

This is the smallest domain in the exam by weight (about 10 questions out of 150), but it is the easiest place to lose marks by overthinking the technical side. Its questions ask: what does an expert engineer do when business, ethics and engineering meet? Framing the problem, defining success metrics, weighing build against buy against partner, communicating trade-offs, and escalating when a violation exists. On top of that comes a pattern reported by test-takers: 3–4 questions containing expense tables and charts that ask “is this spending reasonable? Where is the waste? What should we cut?” — closer to an engineer reviewing a cloud bill than to a certification question. This unit builds both sides together: the logic of professional decision-making, and a cost-analysis workshop with invoices solved figure by figure. All prices in it are illustrative, and the exam will give you its own figures.

Before You Start

This unit is about the non-technical side of an expert engineer’s work, and about money. Its first half: is AI the solution at all? How do you build a business case? How do you write testable requirements? How do you decide between building and buying? How do you talk to management? What do you do when your manager asks you for something wrong? This is Domain 7 (about 10 questions).

Its second half is the invoice workshop: cost tables that look like the real thing, where you must find the waste and calculate the saving. This is a fixed question pattern in the exam (3 to 4 questions). All the calculations are multiplication and addition, and we will walk through them figure by figure.

You need these first: Unit 00 (Section 5.5: cost per hour and per token), Unit 01 (Sections 4.5 and 4.6), and Unit 08 (Sections 2.3 and 4.5: utilisation as a cost multiplier, and FinOps).

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

  • Frame the business problem before the technology: the objective, the baseline, data availability, success and guardrail metrics, the risk level, and return on the basis of total cost of ownership.
  • Build a numerical business case and calculate net benefit and payback period, while distinguishing the conditions that keep it robust.
  • Write testable requirements and acceptance criteria for an AI system, and match proposals and designs against them.
  • Weigh build against buy against partner according to the factors in SDAIA’s guide and the constraints of residency and classification, and know what must precede a purchase.
  • Communicate metrics, risks and uncertainty to each audience in its own language, and escalate professionally and with documentation when a known violation exists.
  • Diagnose waste in an invoice or usage chart using the twelve-item checklist, while distinguishing what must not be cut.
  • Calculate the break-even point between a managed API and self-hosting, size a fleet from the hourly load, and compare three-year total cost after applying the hard constraints.

How to Study This Unit

Read Chapters 1 and 2 as decision logic, not as information: for each table (stages, build versus buy, roles, audiences) ask yourself “which signal in the question text leads me to this row?” Memorise the patterns of the correct answer: documented escalation, not silence and not unilateral refusal; a range with a confidence level, not a single figure; the requirement, not the leaderboard. For Chapters 3 and 4, work with a pencil: recalculate every invoice yourself before reading the solution, check every figure (hours × processors × price), then apply the five steps of Section 4.4 to every table. The exam will change the numbers, but it will not change the method.

Chapter 1

Framing and Delivering a Project: From Idea to Production

1.1 Framing: Is AI the Solution at All?

In Plain Terms

Before any model, ask the business questions. Which decision will change? (“Reduce fraud losses by 20%”, not “build a smart model”.) What happens today without AI? Would a simple rule be enough? Is there data that is labelled and lawful? How do we measure success? What is the risk level? Is the value worth the cost?

The common exam trap: a proposal that looks profitable because its cost was calculated from the price of the hardware only. The real cost includes everything: data labelling, engineering, infrastructure, monitoring, retraining, compliance and support. This is called the total cost of ownership.

Real-World Example

Buying a car. The price is SAR 100,000. But the total cost of ownership over five years is: the price + insurance (15,000) + fuel (40,000) + maintenance (20,000) + registration and inspection (5,000) = SAR 180,000. Comparing two cars on purchase price alone can lead you to the wrong decision. The calculation: return = (benefit − cost) ÷ cost. If the benefit is 300,000 and the total cost is 200,000: (300 − 200) ÷ 200 = 50%.

Key Terms
Business objectiveهدف الأعمال
The concrete result the organisation wants, such as reducing cost, increasing revenue or reducing errors, expressed in numbers.
Guardrail metricsمقاييس الحماية
Metrics that must not get worse while you improve the main metric, such as fairness, latency and cost.
Return on Investment — ROIالعائد على الاستثمار
(Benefit − cost) ÷ cost. How much you earn for every riyal you spend.
Total Cost of Ownership — TCOالتكلفة الكلية للملكية
All the project’s costs over its whole life: build, operation, people, monitoring, retraining, compliance and support, not the price of the hardware alone.
Heuristicالاستدلال البسيط
A simple rule of thumb that gives a reasonable result without a model, such as “a transaction over SAR 10,000 from a new, suspicious country”.

What the Exam Tests

Before any model, the expert engineer starts with business questions. Many questions in this domain reward the person who stops to ask: would a simple rule be enough? What would actually change if the system succeeded?

1.2 From Proof of Concept to Minimum Product to Production

In Plain Terms

A project passes through three stages, and each stage asks a different question. Proof of concept: is the idea technically possible on real data? Minimum product or pilot: does it deliver value to real users within a small scope? Production: is it reliable, monitored and governed at scale?

The signal that recurs in the exam: “the proof of concept succeeded but the project stalled”. The reason is usually: it was not ready for production (no data pipelines, no MLOps, no owner, no integration with the systems), or “success” was never tied to a business indicator in the first place.

Real-World Example

A restaurant wants to add a new dish. Proof of concept: the chef tries the recipe in the kitchen: can it be made? Does it taste good? Pilot: he serves it to twenty selected customers: do they like it? Do they order it again? Production: he adds it to the menu in every branch, with a standard recipe, fixed suppliers, training for the chefs, and quality monitoring. Succeeding at the first does not mean being ready for the third.

Key Terms
Proof of Concept — POCإثبات المفهوم
A small experiment to prove that the idea is technically possible on real data.
Minimum Viable Product — MVP / Pilotالمنتج الأدنى
The simplest version that delivers real value to real users within a limited scope, so as to learn what they actually need.
Exit criteriaمعيار الانتقال
The conditions that must be met to move from one stage to the next.

Scenario signal: “the proof of concept succeeded but the project stalled” → a lack of production readiness (data pipelines, MLOps, ownership, integration), or the success metric was never tied to a business indicator.

1.3 Building the Business Case

In Plain Terms

A business case answers five questions with numbers: (1) Which process will change, and what does it cost today? (2) What is the realistic improvement? As a range (“30 to 45%”), not a single figure. (3) What does it cost to build and run? (4) What are the risks and what do they cost? (5) When is the investment recovered?

Two pieces of advice. Do not count a benefit twice. And “hours saved” are not money unless the time is actually used for something else, or you avoid a new hire. If the system saves each employee two hours a day, and the employees spend them idle, there is no financial saving.

Real-World Example

The calculation in the example, step by step. Today: 120,000 claims × 18 minutes = 2,160,000 minutes ÷ 60 = 36,000 hours. × SAR 120 an hour = SAR 4.32 million a year. After the model: 40% of claims (48,000) take 5 minutes instead of 18, a saving of 13 minutes each: 48,000 × 13 = 624,000 minutes = 10,400 hours × 120 ≈ SAR 1.25 million. Payback: the build cost of 1.4 million ÷ an annual net benefit of between 1.4 and 1.8 million ≈ 9 to 12 months.

Key Terms
Business caseدراسة الجدوى
A document that shows in numbers the project’s value, cost, risks and the date the investment is recovered, in order to decide on funding.
Payback periodفترة الاسترداد
The time the benefits need to cover the initial investment cost. Build cost ÷ annual net benefit.
Net Present Value — NPVصافي القيمة الحالية
The value of all future benefits and costs, calculated at the value of money today. If it is positive, the project is profitable.
One-off vs recurring costsالتكاليف لمرة واحدة والمتكررة
One-off: build, integration and data preparation. Recurring: compute, licences, monitoring, retraining and support, every month or year.

What the Exam Tests

A credible business case for an AI project answers five questions with numbers:

  1. Which decision or process will change, and what is it worth today? The current volume, cost, error rate and cycle time. (For example: 120,000 claims a year, 18 minutes of manual review for each, and 4% fraud leakage.)
  2. What is the realistic improvement? From a pilot or comparable cases, as a range, not a single figure (for example: 30–45% less review time; and a 0.5–1 point reduction in fraud leakage).
  3. What does it cost to build and run? One-off costs (data work, development, integration, change management) plus recurring costs (compute, licences, monitoring, retraining, support, governance, auditing).
  4. What are the risks and what do they cost? Errors, regulatory exposure, reputational damage and vendor lock-in — and the cost of the controls (human review, explainability, impact assessment).
  5. When is the investment recovered, and how will we know? Payback period, net present value (NPV) / return, and the indicators that will be tracked after launch.

The usual value levers: cost reduction (automation, fewer errors), revenue (conversion, retention, pricing), risk reduction (fraud, compliance), speed (cycle time, customer waiting), and quality (consistency, accuracy). Avoid counting the same benefit twice, and remember that “hours saved” do not become money unless the time is redeployed.

1.4 Requirements Engineering for AI

In Plain Terms

The requirements of AI systems are more than “what the system does”. They cover ten types: functional, quality (in numbers: “recall ≥ 0.9”), performance (“p95 ≤ 300 ms”), data (source, classification and residency), explainability, fairness, human oversight, operations, compliance and cost. The reason: the system is probabilistic and sometimes makes mistakes, so you must define how many errors are acceptable.

The golden rule: write the acceptance criteria before building, and in a testable form. “The assistant answers HR questions” is not testable. But “90% of answers are correct on 300 real questions” is testable.

Real-World Example

An untestable requirement: “We want a fast, accurate system that respects privacy.” When do we say it has succeeded? Nobody knows. A testable requirement: “On an evaluation set of 300 Arabic questions from real tickets: ≥ 90% of answers correct and supported by the source, 100% of out-of-scope questions refused or redirected, time to first word p95 ≤ 1.5 seconds, and no personal data leaves the Kingdom.” Now you can test every sentence and say yes or no.

Key Terms
Requirements engineeringهندسة المتطلبات
Gathering what the system must achieve and writing it down precisely and testably before building.
Acceptance criteriaمعايير القبول
Measurable conditions the system must meet to be accepted, such as “accuracy ≥ 90% on such-and-such a set”.
Functional requirementالمتطلب الوظيفي
What the system must do, such as “classifies complaints into 12 types”.
Non-functional requirementالمتطلب غير الوظيفي
How the system must work: speed, availability, security, cost and compliance.

This is exactly the form of the “specification matching” questions in the exam: a proposal or design that violates a single hard requirement fails, however well it performs in other respects.

1.5 Working in Teams, Project Delivery and Roles

In Plain Terms

AI projects are teamwork. Good working practices: clear ownership (RACI), multidisciplinary reviews (security, legal and domain experts), documentation, iterative delivery and knowledge transfer. The team roles: product owner, AI engineer, data engineer, data scientist, MLOps engineer, domain experts, security, compliance and ethics, user experience, and operations.

The RACI matrix for a model launch: R (Responsible): the machine learning engineer builds and tests. A (Accountable): the product owner decides on the launch. C (Consulted): security, privacy and compliance. I (Informed): support, operations and leadership. The delivery cadence: short cycles, data and baseline first, then the simplest complete system, then improvement.

Real-World Example

The bus factor: how many people, if “hit by a bus” (or if they suddenly left the company), would bring the project to a halt? If one person is the only one who understands the data pipeline, the bus factor = 1, and that is a big risk. The remedy: documentation, knowledge transfer and pair working, until the number rises.

Key Terms
Responsible / Accountable / Consulted / Informedمصفوفة RACI
A table that specifies for each task who carries it out, who is ultimately accountable (one person), who is consulted, and who is informed.
Architecture Decision Record — ADRسجل القرارات المعمارية
A short document for each important design decision: what the decision is, why, what the alternatives are, and what the consequences are.
Bus factorعامل الحافلة
The smallest number of team members whose absence would halt the project. Higher is better.
Product ownerمالك المنتج
The person responsible for defining the problem, the value and the priorities, and for the acceptance and launch decision.

  • Accountable: the product owner or model owner (the go/no-go launch decision).
  • Consulted: security, privacy, compliance and domain experts.
  • Informed: support, operations and leadership.

In SDAIA’s ethics roles: the AI Officer leads operationally, the Compliance Officer leads strategically, and the AI System Assessor audits.

Delivery cadence: iterate in short cycles, with a working, evaluated slice each time: data and baseline first, then the simplest end-to-end integrated system, then improvements ordered by error analysis. Keep a decision log (ADRs) so that later reviewers understand why choices were made.

1.6 Change Management and Adoption

In Plain Terms

Many technically excellent systems fail because people do not use them, or use them wrongly. That is why adoption is a separate piece of work: involve users in the design, train them on what the system does and its limits, show the confidence level and the sources in the interface, collect their feedback and act on it, and measure actual usage.

Watch for two opposite risks. Over-reliance: the employee approves every suggestion without thinking. under-reliance: the employee ignores even the good suggestions. The exam signal: high accuracy with low usage = an adoption problem, not a model problem.

Real-World Example

A hospital launched a system with 95% accuracy that suggests diagnoses to doctors. After three months: the doctors use it in only 8% of cases. Is the solution “a more accurate model”? No. On investigation: the system requires opening a separate screen and typing the patient number by hand, it does not explain why it makes a suggestion, and nobody was trained on it. The solution: integrate it into the medical records system, show the reasons, and train the doctors.

Key Terms
Change managementإدارة التغيير
A plan to help people accept and use a new way of working: communication, training, support and collecting feedback.
Adoptionالتبني
The extent to which users actually use the system in their daily work.
Under-relianceالاعتماد الناقص
Users ignoring the system’s suggestions even when they are right, so its benefit is lost.

What the Exam Tests

Many technically sound AI systems fail because people do not use them, or use them wrongly. Adoption work therefore includes:

  • Involving end users in design and evaluation.
  • Training that covers what the system does, its limits, and when to override it.
  • Clear signals in the interface about the confidence level and the sources.
  • Feedback buttons that actually feed improvement.
  • Measuring adoption (usage, override rates and time saved) alongside the model metrics.
  • Monitoring over-reliance (automatically approving AI suggestions without scrutiny) and under-reliance (ignoring good suggestions).

SDAIA’s study “AI and its Impact on Productivity” (2026) frames productivity gains as depending on this institutional adoption, not on the technology alone.

High accuracy with low usage = an adoption problem, not a model problem.

Decisions, Stakeholders and the Saudi Ecosystem

2.1 Build, Buy or Partner

In Plain Terms

Do we build or buy? The decision leans towards buying when the capability is generic and commercially available (such as speech-to-text), the team is small and time is short, the volume is uncertain, and the classification allows the data to be processed at the vendor. It leans towards building when the capability is a core competitive advantage, the team is strong, the volume is large and stable, and the data must not leave.

When buying: the transparency principle requires obtaining documentation before purchase. SDAIA’s study “AI Procurement in the Government Sector” lists five challenges, and among its solutions is two-stage competition when the requirements are not yet clear. A rule: transferring the risk to the vendor does not remove the organisation’s responsibility.

Real-World Example

Two-stage competition: a body wants “a smart system that improves service for citizens visiting its offices” but does not know exactly which technology is suitable. If it issued an ordinary tender with detailed specifications now, it would write the wrong specifications. Better: in the first stage vendors present their ideas and prototypes, and the body learns what is possible. In the second stage the body writes clear specifications and issues the competition on them.

Key Terms
Commodity capabilityالقدرة السلعية
A capability available at similar quality from many vendors, such as optical character recognition, so it gives no competitive advantage if built in-house.
Competitive advantageالمزية التنافسية
A capability that distinguishes the organisation from others and is hard to imitate, so it is worth building in-house.
Open competitionالمنافسة العامة
A traditional procurement method with clear specifications on which vendors compete. It suits cases where the need is known precisely.
Two-stage competitionالمنافسة على مرحلتين
Procurement in two stages: the first to explore solutions and ideas, the second to compete on final specifications. It suits cases where the requirements are not yet clear.
Challenge-based procurementالمناقصة القائمة على التحدي
Putting a problem to vendors so that they compete in solving it in practice, instead of describing the solution in advance.

What the Exam Tests

When buying, SDAIA’s ethics transparency principle and supply-chain risks require obtaining documentation before purchase: data sources, evaluation results, limitations, security posture, data handling, hosting location, and an exit plan (to avoid lock-in).

What the SDAIA study says about procurement

SDAIA’s study “AI Procurement in the Government Sector” (January 2026) lists five challenges:

  1. Lack of clarity in requirements and outcomes.
  2. Technical infrastructure and data quality (discovering after contracting that the data is insufficient or biased).
  3. Ethical considerations and responsibility (who is accountable for a biased decision).
  4. Difficulty evaluating solutions (a vendor may not disclose its models and algorithms, so trials and prototypes are needed).
  5. Flexibility versus rigid procedures.

Among the remedies it describes: choosing between open competition (when the specifications are clear) and two-stage competition (when the requirements are not yet clear), allocating responsibility in the contract for the system’s errors and harms, and using prototypes and challenge-based tenders.

Under the risk management framework: transferring the risk to the vendor never removes the organisation’s own responsibility.

2.2 Adoption Options in SDAIA’s Guide: Build, Buy or Partner

In Plain Terms

SDAIA’s guide “Adoption of AI Systems” identifies six factors that settle the choice: availability of commercial solutions, data readiness, strategic value (a competitive advantage or an operational improvement), capability level, budget, and time. Three options with official advantages and disadvantages: build, buy and partner (with an external party who helps and builds alongside you).

The simplified logic: competitive advantage → build (or partner if capabilities are lacking). Operational efficiency with good commercial solutions → buy. But data sensitivity and hosting location can override both: a purchase that requires sending classified or personal data abroad is not an option.

Real-World Example

Three bodies. (1) A bank wants its own fraud-detection model, drawing on its expertise and unique data, and it has a strong team → build. (2) A body wants to turn meeting recordings into text, and the commercial solutions are excellent → buy (on condition of hosting inside the Kingdom if the meetings are sensitive). (3) A body wants a highly customised system, but has no engineers → partner with an expert body that builds alongside it and trains its team.

  • Verify data readiness.
  • Assess the system’s impact in advance.
  • Evaluate vendors and their offers against quality criteria.
  • Choose a procurement method suited to emerging technologies instead of rigid traditional methods — consistent with the procurement study (2026) (two-stage competition when requirements are unclear) and with the transparency principle (full documentation before purchase).

2.3 Communicating with Stakeholders and Conveying Risk and Uncertainty

In Plain Terms

Each audience needs a different language. Senior management: the impact on the business, cost, risks and schedule, not “F1 = 0.87”. Product owners: the trade-offs. Legal: data flows, retention and residency. Operations: failures and escalation. Users: disclosure, limits and a route to help.

The pattern in a strong message: translate the metric into consequences, give a range and a confidence level, not a single figure, state your recommendation and why, and name the conditions that would change your recommendation. “leadership decides the trade-off, and the engineer measures it in numbers”.

Real-World Example

A weak message to the manager: “Recall 0.9 and precision 0.75.” The manager does not understand what that means. A strong message: “On last quarter’s data, the system detected 9 out of every 10 fraud cases. Of every 4 alerts, one is false, which costs the analysts about two hours a day of unnecessary review.” Now the manager can decide: are two hours a day an acceptable price for detecting 90% of fraud?

3.3 Invoice (B): Cost Rising While Traffic Stays Flat

In Plain Terms

4.1 Invoice (D): The Break-Even Point Between a Managed API and Self-Hosting

In Plain Terms

Invoice (D): when is the managed API (you pay per request) cheaper than self-hosting (you pay a fixed price for machines)? The API: $0.003 per request, with no fixed cost. Hosting: $3,600 per machine a month, the minimum is two machines, + 2,000 for operations. So the minimum cost of hosting = 2 × 3,600 + 2,000 = $9,200 however low the volume.

The break-even point = 9,200 ÷ 0.003 ≈ 3.1 million requests a month. Below it the API is cheaper, and above it hosting is cheaper, provided the team can run it and utilisation stays high. The decisive rule: compliance can override cost. If the data must not leave the Kingdom and the API is abroad, self-hosting or an in-country provider is required even at a million requests.

Real-World Example

The calculation for several volumes. 4.2 Chart (G): Usage by the Hour

In Plain Terms

Chart (G): the number of requests varies greatly during the day (from 15 requests a second at night to 260 in the evening), but the number of machines is fixed at 10 all day. The fleet is sized for the peak, and runs at 5–18% for nine hours a day.

The solution: autoscaling that follows the load, with scheduled pre-scaling before the two known peaks (9:00 and 18:00). The calculation: for each period, the number of machines = ⌈load ÷ 30⌉ + 1 spare. The result is 132 instance-hours a day instead of 240, a saving of 45%. The distractor: “buy reserved capacity for ten machines”, because it lowers the price but locks in the waste.

Real-World Example

The calculation for two periods. Night (00–06): 15 requests a second ÷ 30 per machine = 0.5, rounded up = 1, + 1 spare = two machines × 6 hours = 12 instance-hours. The evening peak (18–21): 260 ÷ 30 = 8.67 → 9, + 1 = 10 machines × 3 hours = 30. We repeat for all the periods and add up: 12 + 9 + 27 + 21 + 18 + 30 + 15 = 132. The fixed fleet: 10 × 24 = 240. The saving (240 − 132) ÷ 240 = 45%.

4.4 How to Answer Any Cost Question in Five Steps

In Plain Terms

The method for any cost question in five steps, which you apply to every invoice. (1) Identify the largest items: usually 2 or 3 items carry most of the saving. (2) Compare utilisation with cost: actual utilisation, the hours needed against the hours running, and what changed against what is recomputed. (3) Check the class: is a premium model or machine doing work that the cheaper one would suffice for? (4) Check compliance on every item: some items are mandatory, some savings are unlawful, and some waste is also a violation. (5) Rank by saving and risk, and read what the question asks for.

The exam will give you different numbers, but the method does not change.

Real-World Example

Applying it to Invoice (A). (1) The largest items: production 42%, development 21% and the API 18%, which together carry 28,700 of a saving of 36,200. (2) Utilisation: 18% against a peak of only 4 hours, so it is idle capacity. (3) The class: a frontier model for eight categories, so it is the wrong class. (4) Compliance: logs without limit with personal data, so it is both waste and a violation. (5) The ranking: largest saving = autoscaling, safest saving = scheduling development.

The Scenario

A Saudi bank runs an internal Arabic assistant for customer-service staff (RAG + a self-hosted language model inside the Kingdom), with a step that classifies the intent of an enquiry into 5 categories through an external frontier-model API. A GPU costs $4/hour on-demand, and the month is 720 hours. (The figures are illustrative, for the purpose of the exercise.)

The total is $38,720.

The finance team has put forward four further proposals: (1) move the service to a cheaper cloud region outside the Kingdom to save 30%; (2) switch off audit logs; (3) shrink the vector database to a single replica; (4) buy reserved capacity for six GPUs to lower the price. The chief financial officer asks: what is the largest single saving? Which saving does not touch users? Which item is a compliance problem?

Solution Steps

  1. Step 1 — The largest items: production 17,280 (≈ 45%), development 8,640 (≈ 22%) and the API 7,200 (≈ 19%); together about 86% of the invoice, so this is where most of the saving lies.
  2. Step 2 — Utilisation against cost: production at 20% with a peak of only 3 hours → idle capacity. Autoscale with a minimum of 3 (two for availability + 1 headroom) for 21 hours and 6 at the peak: (3 × 21 + 6 × 3) × 30 × 4 = 81 × 120 = $9,720 → a saving of 7,560, with pre-warming before the peak.
  3. Step 2 (continued) — Non-production environments: 3 × 8 × 22 × 4 = $2,112 after scheduling and automatic shutdown of idle notebooks → a saving of 6,528 with no effect on users. Embedding: only 2% changes → incremental processing with change detection ≈ $100 → a saving of 1,700.
  4. Step 3 — The class: a frontier model to classify 5 categories → the wrong model class. A fine-tuned small classifier running on the autoscaled fleet at an additional cost of ≈ 600 → a saving of ≈ 6,600, provided accuracy is verified on an evaluation set before the replacement.
  5. Step 4 — Compliance: logs with customer data kept without limit are both waste and a violation (retention and data minimisation) → a 90-day retention policy + masking of personal data + cold tiering ≈ 500 → a saving of 1,900. Proposals (1) and (2) are rejected: moving customer data outside the Kingdom and switching off audit logs are on the “what must not be cut” list. Proposal (3) saves only about 600 against a real risk to availability. Proposal (4) locks in the idle capacity instead of removing it; reservation suits the baseline load (3 GPUs) after right-sizing, not before.
  6. Step 5 — Ranking: the new total ≈ 9,720 + 2,112 + 600 + 500 + 100 + 900 + 500 = $14,432, a saving of ≈ 24,288 (−63%). Largest single saving = autoscaling (7,560); safest saving = scheduling the non-production environments (6,528); the compliance item = the logs. The vector database and monitoring stay unchanged.

Exam Traps

  • “Move to a cheaper region outside the Kingdom to save 30%” — it breaches the residency required for personal or classified data; a trap however large the saving.
  • “Switch off audit logs or tracing” — a small saving at the price of losing observability and compliance; what is cut is the unlimited retention and the personal data in logs, not the required logs.
  • “Buy reserved capacity for the current fleet” — it lowers the price but locks in the waste; remove the idle capacity first, then reserve the baseline load.
  • “Negotiate a discount or move to a cheaper model” when token cost rises while traffic stays flat — the first step is to remove prompt bloat.
  • Confusing “largest saving” with “a saving with no risk to users” — they may be two different answers; read what the question asks for.
  • Comparing self-hosting on compute cost alone — total cost includes the team, operations and compliance, and these are what often flip the ranking.

    A logistics company's document pipeline extracts six fixed fields (sender, consignee, weight, HS code, value, date) from 1.2M Arabic and English waybills a month. A pilot shows a fine-tuned 1B model on the existing GPU reaches 98.9% field accuracy versus 99.2% for the frontier API; the requirement is ≥ 98.5%. Using the monthly bill below (USD), which change should be made first?

    LineDetailUSD/month
    Frontier LLM APIField extraction, ~2,400 tokens per waybill21,600
    OCR servicePer-page pricing3,100
    GPU node1 GPU for layout detection; utilisation 22%2,300

    Five moves: (1) biggest line — the frontier API, about 78% of the bill ($21.6k of $27.8k); (2) usage versus need — six fixed fields is simple structured extraction; (3) tier — a fine-tuned 1B model meets the 98.5% requirement (98.9%) and can use the GPU that sits at 22%; (4) compliance — the audit trail is required by customs rules and evaluation must stay; (5) rank — the tier change saves most of $21.6k at validated, low risk. The requirement, not the leaderboard, decides. Discounts come after removing waste; the GPU line is small and will absorb the new workload.

    An airline's booking assistant uses an LLM API priced at SAR 2 per 1M input tokens and SAR 8 per 1M output tokens. In March the team enabled 'conversation memory', which appends each customer's previous turns — including those from earlier visits — to every prompt. Retrieval still sends 4 chunks. The bill since then is below. What is the most likely cause and the right first fix?

    Read the columns: requests and output tokens are flat; input tokens per request grew 3.6× (1,500 → 5,400), taking the input share of cost from about 56% (6,000 of 10,800) to about 82% (21,708 of 26,532).   5. 5Training spend for a tabular modelHard

    An insurer's claims-fraud LightGBM model is retrained every day from the full six-year history on 8 on-demand GPUs for 9 hours ($4 per GPU-hour, 30 days). Online serving runs on 4 CPU replicas at 52% utilisation with p95 of 14 ms, well inside its 50 ms SLO. Drift monitoring shows only small weekly shifts. Monthly spend: training $8,640, feature pipeline $900, serving $1,100. Which plan cuts waste without hurting the service?

    Explanation

    Training cost check: 8 × 9 × 30 = 2,160 GPU-hours × $4 = $8,640. The memo's reasoning: gradient-boosted trees train efficiently on CPUs (GPU support exists but rarely justifies 8 GPUs); daily full-history retraining is unnecessary when drift is small — drift- or performance-triggered (or weekly) retraining on a recent window suffices; and training can use checkpointed spot capacity. That cuts the training line by an order of magnitude. Serving at 52% and 14 ms is healthy — halving it would push the remaining replicas to about 104% average utilisation. Reserving GPUs discounts the wrong hardware; a deep net for tabular data is a tier mistake.

  6. 6Break-even volume for self-hostingHard

    A retailer's product-description service can use a managed API at $0.004 per request, or self-host in-Kingdom at $3,000 per GPU-month reserved, each GPU carrying 3M requests a month at target utilisation, with a minimum of 2 GPUs for high availability (enough, with the spare, for up to 3M requests) plus $1,800 a month of operations effort. The data is Public product information, so residency does not constrain the choice. Roughly where is the monthly break-even volume?

    Explanation

    Self-hosting has a fixed floor: 2 GPUs × $3,000 + $1,800 operations = $7,800 a month, enough (with the spare) for up to 3M requests. The API costs $0.004 × V. Break-even: V = 7,800 ÷ 0.004 = 1.95M requests a month, which falls inside the 2-GPU range, so no extra GPU is needed. Below it the API wins; above it self-hosting wins — if the team can operate it and utilisation stays high. Leaving out the operations effort or the HA minimum is the common error.

  7. 7Vector database sizing versus availabilityHard

    A ministry's staff-wide RAG service stores 3M chunks as 768-dimension FP32 vectors on a 3-node managed vector database (SAR 9,000/month), each node sized with 64 GB of memory. A cost review proposes consolidating to a single node 'because the data is small'. The service is used by all departments during working hours and has an availability target of 99.9%. What is the best response?

    Explanation

    Size first: 3M × 768 × 4 bytes ≈ 9.2 GB of raw vectors (plus index overhead) — far below 64 GB per node, so the nodes are oversized. But, as in the memo's RAG-platform bill, the three nodes exist for availability, which is reasonable for a ministry-wide service: keep the replica count and right-size the node type. A single node becomes a single point of failure against a 99.9% target, and restoring from nightly backups means hours of outage. 'Never touch it' is the absolute-word trap — right-sizing is legitimate.

  8. 8Fine-tuning budget when the need is knowledge freshnessHard

    An electricity utility's assistant gives outdated tariff rates; tariffs are revised monthly and answers must cite the current circular. The team has costed three fine-tuning plans against a requirement of ≥ 85% on a tariff-question eval: full fine-tune of a 70B model (about $46k of experiments, 90%), QLoRA on 70B (about $2.5k, 87%) and LoRA on 8B (about $350, 85%). Which recommendation is best?

    Explanation

    Before reading any fine-tuning budget, the memo says to confirm that the need is behaviour or format (fine-tuning) and not knowledge freshness (RAG). Monthly tariff changes and a citation requirement are freshness problems: a fine-tuned model memorises last month's rates and cannot cite a source, and monthly retraining would repeat the cost every cycle while lagging every revision. RAG over the current circulars updates when the document changes and supplies the citation. The cheapest-passing rule applies only once the method is right.
